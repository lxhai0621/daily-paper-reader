## Abstract 

We introduce CAROL (Chain-based Adaptive Reconfiguration Over Lattices), a probabilistic framework for test-time hallucination reduction in large language models. Rather than relying on token-level uncertainty, CAROL defines a semantic uncertainty measure based on the consistency between generated responses and a trusted context, inducing a string-submodular objective over a lattice of textual sequences. This formulation enables hallucination mitigation to be cast as a Markov chain accept–reject process with provable convergence and near-optimality guaran-tees, allowing the model to iteratively refine outputs toward semantic consistency. By operating at the level of meaning, CAROL unifies hallucination detection and mitigation within a single framework. Empirical results on question answering and multi-agent reasoning benchmarks show that CAROL significantly reduces halluci-nations and improves reliability and interpretability compared to likelihood-based and retrieval-augmented baselines, while maintaining competitive computational efficiency. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed within high-stakes decision-making pipelines, spanning clinical reasoning, scientific discovery, and autonomous cyber–physical sys-tems Zeng et al. [2023]. Despite their impressive fluency, these models exhibit a persistent and consequential failure mode: they often generate responses that are linguistically well-formed yet semantically unsupported by their underlying knowledge, a phenomenon broadly referred to as 

hallucination Johnson and Hajisharif [2024]. In agentic and multi-step reasoning settings, such errors do not remain localized; instead, they propagate and compound across reasoning chains, undermining reliability precisely where correctness is most critical. Existing approaches to hallucination mitigation largely fall into two paradigms: prevention and 

detection . Prevention strategies intervene during generation, either through training-time modifica-tions Liu et al. [2024], Schneider and Xhafa [2022] or via test-time augmentation mechanisms such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation ( RAG ) Lewis et al. [2020], Hübotter et al. [2024], which enrich prompts with external context. While RAG improves factual grounding in many cases, adding more context does not guarantee semantic consistency. Empirically, prompt expansion introduces substan-tial overheads, including increased latency and cost Gallart et al. [2026], long-context degradation Du

Detection methods instead attempt to flag hallucinations after generation, typically by estimating uncertainty via token-level entropy or related likelihood-based proxies Gundogmusler et al. [2024]. However, such measures treat tokens independently and remain fundamentally syntactic: they fail to account for semantic relationships between generated content and trusted knowledge sources (Table 1). Recent work on semantic entropy Farquhar et al. [2024] advances this direction by measuring variability across groups of meaning-equivalent tokens (sentences). Yet this improvement comes at a cost: semantic entropy requires multiple forward passes of the same prompt and conflates consistency with correctness. A systematically wrong model may produce stable yet incorrect outputs under repeated sampling Sclar et al. [2023]. Repetition, by itself, does not confer truth. Critically, both prevention and detection address semantic correctness only through indirect, heuristic signals. They encourage consistency but do not enforce alignment with trusted knowledge, nor do they impose explicit structure on how meaning should accumulate over a generated response. As a result, inference-time guarantees remain elusive. This limitation reflects a deeper issue: hallucinations are fundamentally sequence-level semantic failures , yet most mitigation strategies operate at the level of tokens, likelihoods, or prompt manipulation. What is missing is a principled way to reason about 

how meaning evolves over a generated sequence , and how each newly generated unit contributes, positively or negatively, to semantic consistency with known facts. 

Research question and guiding hypothesis. Can hallucination be treated not as a statistical artifact of token generation, but as a measurable semantic deviation from trusted knowledge, and can this reframing yield a principled inference-time framework with theoretical guarantees, without modifying the model or requiring repeated sampling? We investigate whether grounding generation in a logically structured axiom set Γ and casting hallucination mitigation as mutual information maximization over a vocabulary lattice, novel in this context, admits closed-form sub-optimality bounds and provable convergence at inference time. A key question underlying this perspective is how to formally define “truth” in generated language. Under Tarskian semantics Niiniluoto [2004], truth is not intrinsic but defined relative to a reference model. Consequently, any hallucination mitigation method must rely—explicitly or implicitly—on an external grounding source. Existing approaches incorporate such references indirectly, without enforcing consistency. In contrast, we make this dependence explicit and algorithmically actionable through a trusted axiom set Γ, treating hallucination as a deviation from a reference model of truth. Our central hypothesis is that this view motivates a fundamental shift: from token assembly to string assembly . Rather than appending symbols from a vocabulary V and evaluating reliability retroactively via distributional proxies, the generative unit is elevated to a semantically coherent ℓ-gram, whose entailment relationship with Γ is enforced online . Crucially, Γ is not used to augment the prompt, but to define the semantic reference against which each generated unit is evaluated. This reframing naturally induces a diminishing-returns structure: once a sequence is well-aligned with 

Γ, adding redundant or weakly related content yields progressively smaller semantic gains and may even introduce errors. This property is precisely captured by string submodularity , a generalization of classical set submodularity that respects generation order and operates over the prefix structure of sequences Zhang et al. [2013]. Modeling semantic alignment as a string-submodular mutual information objective over the vocabulary lattice yields three key advantages: (i) a theoretically grounded notion of semantic consistency, (ii) tractable inference-time optimization with provable guarantees, and (iii) a unified mechanism in which hallucination detection and mitigation emerge from the same accept–reject signal. 2Building on this structure, we introduce CAROL (Chain-based Adaptive Reconfiguration Over 

Lattices), a probabilistic test-time refinement algorithm that models ℓ-gram generation as a Markov chain over the vocabulary lattice, guided by a string-submodular semantic objective. CAROL requires neither repeated sampling nor access to internal model probabilities, enabling black-box deployment with convergence and near-optimality guarantees. This section introduces the discrete optimization structures that underlies our framework. All concepts are independent of language models and hallucination and serve solely to formalize the search space and objective class instantiated in Section 3. Let V denote a ground set of atomic elements (e.g., tokens, phrases, or semantic units) and let V∗

## 4 Chain-based Adaptive Reconfiguration Over Lattices Algorithm 

As discussed in Section 1, we define reliability through the coverage that a generated response S

achieves over a set of foundational axioms Γ. Leveraging the submodularity of (6) , see Appendix E, we build upon probabilistic submodular models Gotovos et al. [2015] to introduce a hallucination-aware rejection mechanism based on a sequential greedy scheme (Algorithm 1). Our approach conceptualizes the generation process as a search over a combinatorial lattice L(V) (Appendix C). In this space, a large language model is abstracted as a policy πθ that induces transitions between states by appending tokens v ∈ V. This naturally aligns with the standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation M = ( S, A, π θ , R ), where states correspond to partial sequences, actions to vocabulary elements, and πθ defines the next-token distribution. Existing approaches optimize πθ directly, evaluating generation quality at each step. In contrast, we decouple search from optimization. Specifically, we reinterpret πθ as an auxiliary proposal distribution q, whose role is to explore the lattice L(V). Starting from S0 = q, the proposal iteratively expands the state until a semantically meaningful unit Si = St ⊖ S t−1 is formed (lines 5–8). Each candidate unit Si is then evaluated by clustering the context Γ against it (Algorithm 2), enabling the computation of its marginal contribution in terms of mutual information (line 9). The current state St

thus accumulates the query q and all accepted semantic units up to iteration t. The resulting marginal gain defines a probabilistic submodular objective, which induces a target distribution p (line 10). This distribution governs an accept–reject step (lines 11–16), determining whether the candidate Si is incorporated into the state, see Remark E.1. In this way, generation is guided not by likelihood alone, but by incremental semantic consistency with respect to Γ, effectively maximizing (6). After CAROL accepts or rejects the generated string, the pipeline either continuously to generate a new string from the current state St or step-backs into previous step St−1 and generates again from there Vendrell et al. As shown in Section 6, Algorithm 1 is sensitive to the sampling temperature β, or equivalently, to the acceptance threshold z. This parameter controls a fundamental trade-off between reliability and generative flexibility, as discussed in Appendix F. Lower values enforce stricter adherence to the axioms Γ, favoring conservative and highly consistent outputs, while higher values allow greater exploration, enabling the generation of novel but potentially less grounded content. Equally important is the role of the context Γ and the clustering mechanism, see Appendix B. The performance of 

CAROL is inherently tied to the quality and coverage of the underlying axioms: the method constrains generation to remain within the semantic support induced by Γ. Consequently, richer and more reliable context sets directly expand the space of valid, non-hallucinated responses. In this sense, 

CAROL does not increase nor decrease model capacity, but rather reallocates it toward faithfully exploiting the available trusted knowledge.

5.1 Clustering Analysis 

One of the key novelties of this work is the use of an exemplar-based clustering method. Existing approaches to semantic entropy rely on classical clustering techniques, which are highly sensitive to the choice of clustering temperature , often producing either overly coarse or overly fragmented partitions (see Figure 2). As shown in Kuhn et al. [2023], the resulting partition critically influences the computed semantic entropy. This sensitivity is further amplified when ℓ-gram probabilities are derived from cluster sizes Farquhar et al. [2024], where an increasing number of clusters can artificially inflate entropy, introducing a strong dependence on data volume for stability. To overcome these limitations, we use the exemplar-based clustering procedure in Algorithm 2, which treats generated ℓ-grams as medoids and therefore avoids temperature tuning. Cluster density, rather than cluster count, drives the semantic entropy: dense assignments indicate agreement with Γ and yield low entropy, while sparse or diffuse partitions signal disagreement and higher hallucination risk, see Appendix A. Moreover, the submodularity-aware selection, as shown in Proposition 5.1, gives a near-optimal coverage guarantee, discouraging redundant medoids and promoting sparse representatives with broader coverage of Γ.

## 6 Case Study 

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, we consider two complementary case studies. First, we assess the proposed semantic entropy-based hallucination metric (6) on the FEVER 

dataset Thorne et al. [2018], comparing it against the HalluciNot model Paudel et al. [2025] and classical token-level entropy baselines. Second, we evaluate the full CAROL framework across three benchmark datasets: TruthfulQA Lin et al. [2021], HaluEval Li et al. [2023], and HotPotQA Yang et al. [2018]. These datasets capture diverse hallucination settings, including fact fabrication under misleading prompts (TruthfulQA), hallucinations in summarization, question answering, and dialogue (HaluEval), and multi-hop reasoning with confabulation effects (HotPotQA; see Section 1). We evaluate two language models, GPT-5-nano and Llama-3.1-8B , under Algorithm 1, and compare their performance against standard RAG -based pipelines. Our evaluation considers not only halluci-nation reduction, but also the trade-off between response quality, latency, and computational cost. As described in Section 4, CAROL operates over ℓ-gram semantic units derived from the context Γ.In practice, we implement this within an agentic framework using URSA Grosskopf et al. [2025], deployed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPU, see Appendix G. The system comprises three agents: Planner , which generates the query and constructs Γ; Researcher , which produces candidate responses (treated as ℓ-grams); and Reasoner , which aggregates accepted outputs across iterations. 

6.1 Hallucination Detection 

Prior work has demonstrated the advantages of semantic entropy over classical token-level uncertainty metrics Kuhn et al. [2023]. In this section, we show that the proposed metric not only outperforms token-level baselines, but also remains competitive with specialized hallucination detection methods such as HalluciNot . We evaluate performance in terms of detection accuracy, along with implementa-tion metrics including execution time and GPU utilization. Accuracy is computed as the proportion of correctly classified instances (true positives and true negatives) over the total number of examples. Additional results and insights are provided in Appendix A, G and H. As reported in Table 4, the proposed method identifies hallucinated statements more reliably than token-level entropy, which provides limited semantic discrimination. It also achieves performance comparable to HalluciNot with substantially lower computational overhead, making it suitable for resource-constrained settings. The resulting detection policy is more conservative, as the clustering-induced sparsity prioritizes contextual consistency and reduces false positives.

## 7 Conclusions 

This work introduced CAROL , a principled framework for hallucination reduction in large language models that operates entirely at test time in a black-box setting, requiring neither access to internal probabilities nor repeated model evaluations. Central to the approach is a single-pass semantic entropy measure grounded in logical entailment, enabling direct assessment of consistency with respect to a trusted context Γ. To support this, we propose an exemplar-based clustering mechanism that eliminates sensitivity to temperature-based partitioning and avoids iterative refinement. This formulation induces a string-submodular objective over a lattice of textual sequences, allowing hallucination mitigation to be cast as a probabilistic accept–reject process with provable convergence and near-optimality guarantees. Empirically, CAROL achieves more reliable and consistent outputs than entropy- and retrieval-based baselines at competitive cost. More broadly, it reframes hallucination as a sequence-level deviation from axiomatic knowledge, providing a scalable foundation for reliable and interpretable test-time generation in agentic AI systems. 9References

In particular, temperature-sensitive clustering can produce substantially different partitions for the same semantic content, which may lead to unstable hallucination scores. In this section we clarify the nature and construction of the context Γ introduced in Assumption 1. 

Definition and role. The context Γ is defined as a collection of trusted statements, modeled as a set of axioms A, which serve as the reference for semantic evaluation throughout the generation process. Each element x ∈ Γ represents a unit of information that is assumed to be factually correct within the scope of the task. Importantly, Γ does not aim to be exhaustive; rather, it provides a reliable grounding substrate against which generated content is evaluated via entailment (Section 2). 

Relation to retrieval-augmented generation ( RAG ). From a systems perspective, the construction of Γ does not differ fundamentally from standard retrieval-augmented generation pipelines. In both cases, a query q is used to retrieve relevant documents or pieces of information from external sources, forming an augmented prompt, see Figure 1. The distinction lies not in how the context is retrieved, but in how it is interpreted . Classical RAG 

treats retrieved context as auxiliary information to improve generation quality, without enforcing consistency between the output and the retrieved content. In contrast, our framework treats Γ as an 

axiomatic reference : generated statements are explicitly evaluated for semantic consistency with respect to Γ, and may be accepted or rejected accordingly. 

Trusted knowledge requirement. A key requirement is that the elements of Γ are trusted within the application domain. This does not impose additional algorithmic complexity, but rather introduces a structural constraint on the source of the information. In practice, this can be implemented by restricting retrieval to curated or authoritative sources. Examples include: • Enterprise setting: internal documents such as company policies, technical specifications, or verified knowledge bases. • Medical queries: authoritative sources such as National Institutes of Health [2026], or peer-reviewed clinical guidelines. • Scientific research: curated repositories such as arXiv or domain-specific digital libraries. The RAG literature implicitly assumes that the retrieved context is reliable, although this assumption is rarely stated explicitly. In this work, we emphasize a closer connection with the logic literature, where the correctness of the underlying premises is fundamental. In particular, it is not sufficient for retrieved information to appear coherent or plausible; it must be grounded in verifiable truth. While this distinction is often overlooked in practical implementations, it is essential for ensuring that generated responses remain semantically valid and free from hallucination. 

No increase in computational complexity. We emphasize that this requirement does not increase the computational complexity of the pipeline. The retrieval mechanism remains unchanged; only the selection criteria for admissible sources differs. Therefore, Γ can be constructed using standard retrieval systems, with an additional filtering or curation step to ensure reliability. 

Interpretation. Conceptually, Γ can be understood as defining a truth-constrained semantic space .The role of the generation process is not merely to produce fluent text, but to construct a sequence S

that remains within the entailment closure of Γ. In this sense, Γ induces the feasible region of the optimization problem in (4). This interpretation is consistent with the formulation in the main text, where hallucination is charac-terized as a deviation from the semantic support provided by Γ.

Beyond axiomatic truth. Although we model Γ as an axiom set in this work, the same formulation can be extended to broader notions of correctness. In many scientific domains, truth is not specified purely by logical axioms, but by empirical validation, experimental evidence, simulation, or formal verification. Accordingly, Γ may be generalized from a set of trusted statements to a task-specific 26 correctness oracle. For example, in theorem proving or mathematical reasoning, Γ could represent a proof checker that verifies whether each generated step follows from previous ones; in scientific settings, it could represent empirically validated knowledge or a domain simulator. This extension would not change the overall structure of CAROL : candidate strings would still be accepted or rejected according to their marginal contribution to correctness with respect to Γ. Formally, only the evaluation rule defining the acceptance probability changes, while the Markov chain interpretation, submodular objective structure, and resulting convergence bounds remain applicable whenever the induced correctness score satisfies the same diminishing-returns assumptions. 27 C Lattice Representation

E.1 Greedoid structure of LLMs generation Proposition E.1 (Greedoid structure of feasible LLM generations) . Let V denote the set of semantic units (e.g., tokens, phrases, or ℓ-grams) and let I ⊆ V∗ denote the family of partial generations that satisfy a semantic feasibility oracle O : V∗ → { 0, 1}. Assume that O satisfies: 

(A1) Empty feasibility: O(∅) = 1 .

(A2) Prefix accessibility: if O(S) = 1 and S̸ = ∅, then deleting the last semantic unit preserves feasibility, i.e., O(S1: |S|− 1) = 1 .

(A3) Extendability: if S1, S2 ∈ I and |S1| < |S2|, then there exists a semantic unit s ∈ S2 ⊖ S1

such that S1 ⊕ s ∈ I .Then (V, I) forms a greedoid. □

Proof. We verify the three greedoid axioms. First, by (A1) , the empty generation is feasible, hence 

∅ ∈ I , which proves non-emptiness. Second, let S ∈ I be nonempty. Since S is a valid partial generation, O(S) = 1 . By prefix accessibility (A2) , removing the last generated semantic unit yields the prefix S1: |S|− 1, which remains feasible. Therefore, there exists an element s ∈ S whose removal preserves feasibility, establishing accessibility. Third, let S1, S2 ∈ I with |S1| < |S2|. By the extendability assumption (A3) , there exists a semantic unit s ∈ S2 ⊖ S1 such that S1 ⊕ s ∈ I . This is exactly the exchange property. Therefore, I satisfies non-emptiness, accessibility, and exchange, and hence (V, I) is a greedoid.

Dependence on Trusted Context Γ. CAROL relies on the availability of a trusted contextual reference set Γ. This is both a strength and a limitation. On the one hand, separating generation from semantic evaluation allows the method to avoid treating retrieved context merely as prompt augmentation. Instead, Γ acts as an axiomatic reference against which generated ℓ-grams are accepted or rejected. On the other hand, the guarantees of the framework are only meaningful relative to the reliability of Γ. If Γ is incomplete, biased, outdated, or factually incorrect, then CAROL may enforce consistency with an invalid reference and thereby preserve or even amplify errors. This limitation is inherent to any grounding-based hallucination mitigation approach: factuality cannot be certified without assumptions on the truthfulness of the grounding source. In the sense of Tarskian semantics, truth is defined relative to a model or interpretation. Therefore, while CAROL can test whether a generated statement is semantically supported by Γ, it cannot independently certify that Γ itself is universally true. Ultimately, trust must be grounded externally, and it is in general impossible for the system to verify it in isolation. For this reason, practical deployments should construct Γ from curated, auditable, and domain-authoritative sources, especially in high-stakes applications such as medicine, law, finance, or cyber–physical systems. 

Clustering as a Computational Bottleneck. A second limitation concerns the clustering step used to compute semantic entropy and marginal semantic gain. Since CAROL evaluates candidate ℓ-grams by comparing them against the trusted context, the clustering operation may become a computational bottleneck when Γ is large. However, this bottleneck is not fundamental. Because Γ is fixed for a given query or task instance, its embeddings can be precomputed and cached. Distance computations can be accelerated using approximate nearest-neighbor search, low-dimensional indexing, or relative-distance schemes that compare candidates only against representative key points rather than all elements of Γ Jain et al. [2010]. In addition, stochastic variants of clustering Harris et al. [2018] can estimate the marginal gain using random subsets of Γ, reducing computational cost while preserving the accept–reject structure in expectation. These approximations provide natural directions for scaling CAROL to large contexts without altering the conceptual framework and are left for future work. 

Dependence on Sufficient ℓ-gram Generation. The clustering mechanism requires a sufficient number of generated ℓ-grams to form meaningful semantic partitions. If the model produces only a very short response, or if the task admits only a single atomic answer, the entropy estimate may become less informative due to the lack of diversity in semantic units. Similarly, highly creative or open-ended tasks may require a richer context Γ to distinguish unsupported novelty from valid creative extrapolation. However, this requirement is not a significant practical limitation in the agentic settings targeted by 

CAROL . Modern LLM-based systems typically generate intermediate reasoning steps, tool outputs, and candidate responses across multiple iterations. These intermediate outputs naturally provide enough ℓ-grams for clustering to be meaningful. Thus, while CAROL may be less informative in extremely short single-step settings, it is well aligned with multi-step agentic workflows where sufficient generation is inherently available. A deeper analysis on the effect of clustering size is left for future work. 

Broader Impact and Responsible Use. CAROL is designed to reduce hallucinations and improve interpretability by explicitly conditioning generation on semantic consistency with trusted information. This can enhance reliability in applications where unsupported outputs may lead to downstream harm. However, the same mechanism may also induce a false sense of certainty if consistency with Γ is interpreted as absolute truth. It is therefore important to emphasize that CAROL enforces grounded consistency, not universal correctness. Responsible deployment requires transparency in how Γ is constructed, awareness of its potential incompleteness or bias, and human oversight in high-stakes domains. Specifically, that is why Assumption 1 is grounded on axioms, which are the basic information unit in any scientific approach. When used appropriately, CAROL provides a principled tool for improving reliability in large language model systems without modifying their internal structure. 36 Integration in verifiers pipelines. All experiments are implemented using URSA (Universal Research and Scientific Agent), an open-source framework for building modular agentic systems. URSA provides a standardized interface for defining, composing, and executing agents, enabling reproducible multi-agent experimentation. 

Installation. To ensure reproducibility, we rely on a fixed version of URSA . The framework can be installed directly from the official repository: 

git clone https://github.com/lanl/ursa.git cd ursa pip install -e . 

We recommend using a virtual environment with a fixed Python version (e.g., Python 3.10) and freezing dependencies via requirements.txt .

BaseAgent abstraction. URSA defines a core abstraction, BaseAgent , which standardizes agent behavior through a unified interface. Each agent implements: • an internal model (e.g., LLM or tool), • a forward() method that maps inputs to outputs, • optional memory and state management. This abstraction allows all agents in our pipeline to be defined as modular and interchangeable components, facilitating controlled experimentation. 

Agent definitions. Following the architecture described in Section 6 (see also Fig. 1), we instantiate three agents: • Planner Agent: generates the initial query q and constructs the context Γ.• Researcher Agent: produces candidate responses (treated as ℓ-grams in CAROL ). • Reasoner Agent: aggregates and refines responses across iterations. Each agent is implemented by subclassing BaseAgent . A minimal example is shown below: 

from ursa.agents.base_agent import BaseAgent class ResearcherAgent(BaseAgent): def forward(self, input_text): response = self.model.generate(input_text) return response 

Integration with CAROL . URSA is used as the execution backbone for CAROL . At each iteration: 1. The Planner provides (q, Γ) .2. The Researcher generates a candidate string St.3. CAROL evaluates St via the submodular objective, Algorithm 1. 4. The Reasoner aggregates accepted responses. Importantly, CAROL treats each Researcher output as a semantic unit (i.e., an ℓ-gram), aligning with the agent-level abstraction rather than token-level generation.

Statistical Significance and Experimental Variability. To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of our empirical findings, all reported results are evaluated over multiple independent runs with different random seeds controlling (i) stochastic decoding in the language model, (ii) the probabilistic acceptance–rejection mechanism of CAROL , and (iii) dataset shuffling. We perform k = 3 independent runs per configuration using fixed seeds {0, 1, 2}. For each metric, we report the mean performance together with the standard deviation. In addition, we compute 95% confidence intervals via non-parametric bootstrap resampling (with 1000 resamples) over the evaluation set. 39 To assess statistical significance of performance differences between CAROL and baseline methods (e.g., RAG and HalluciNot ), we perform paired hypothesis tests over identical evaluation samples. Specifically, we use two-sided paired t-tests, and confirm results with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to account for potential non-normality. Improvements reported in Tables 4 and 3 are statistically significant at the p < 0.05 level. We observe consistently low variance across runs (see reported standard deviations), indicating that the proposed method is robust to stochastic effects in both generation and inference-time control. This suggests that performance gains of CAROL are not driven by favorable sampling effects, but reflect consistent improvements across stochastic realizations.

We evaluate CAROL on four widely used benchmarks covering factual verification, hallucination detection, and multi-hop reasoning: FEVER , TruthfulQA , HaluEval , and HotPotQA . Each dataset provides a query (or claim), optional supporting context, and a ground-truth label or reference answer. In our framework, the query is used to construct the prompt ρ, while the retrieved or provided context is mapped into the trusted axiom set Γ.
