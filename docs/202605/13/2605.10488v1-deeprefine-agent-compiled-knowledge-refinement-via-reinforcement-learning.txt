## Abstract 

Agent-compiled knowledge bases provide persistent external knowledge for large language model (LLM) agents in open-ended, knowledge-intensive downstream tasks. Yet their quality is systematically limited by incompleteness , incorrect-ness , and redundancy , manifested as missing evidence or cross-document links, low-confidence or imprecise claims, and ambiguous or coreference resolution is-sues. Such defects compound under iterative use, degrading retrieval fidelity and downstream task performance. We present DeepRefine , a general LLM-based reasoning model for agent-compiled knowledge refinement that improves the qual-ity of any pre-constructed knowledge bases with user queries to make it more suitable for the downstream tasks. DeepRefine performs multi-turn interactions with the knowledge base and conducts abductive diagnosis over interaction history, localizes likely defects, and executes targeted refinement actions for incremental knowledge base updates. To optimize refinement policies of DeepRefine without gold references, we introduce a Gain-Beyond-Draft (GBD) reward and train the reasoning process end-to-end via reinforcement learning. Extensive experiments demonstrate consistent downstream gains over strong baselines. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities in handling natural language understanding and generation tasks [Yang et al., 2025, Jaech et al., 2024]. And retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds LLMs in external knowledge bases outside model parameters [Lewis et al., 2020, Gao et al., 2023]. A dominant recipe indexes external knowledge as vectors for dense retrieval. Graph-based RAG systems additionally organize salient content into graph-structured knowledge bases to support multi-hop retrieval and reasoning [Edge et al., 2024, Guo et al., 2024, Gutiérrez et al., 2025, Huang et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2026]. In both cases, the external knowledge base is usually built once and static at serving time, which provides LLMs with richer external knowledge and enhances their capabilities in domain-specific or knowledge-intensive tasks. Yet a static knowledge base is largely stateless with respect to ongoing use. It does not accumulate structured lessons from repeated queries, user-specific context, or iterative agent workflows. Recent agent-compiled knowledge bases (e.g., LLM-Wiki [Karpathy, 2026]) address this gap by having LLM-based agents ingest sources, synthesize articles, and maintain cross-references over time. For example, toolkits that compile documents using agentic workflows and file outputs back into a user-owned persistent store. As shown in the left part of Figure 1, such systems make external knowledge bases evolvable rather than frozen at test time.

Despite this progress, agent-compiled knowledge bases often exhibit systematic quality defects, including incompleteness , incorrectness , and redundancy [Subagdja et al., 2024, Cimiano and Paulheim, 2017]. In practice, these defects manifest as missing evidence chains or cross-document links, low-confidence or imprecise assertions, and ambiguous or coreference resolution issues. Such issues compound and degrade both retrieval fidelity and end-task performance. Recent studies mainly optimize the construction policy of knowledge base builders [Wang et al., 2025, Tsang et al., 2025], however, in many real deployments the knowledge base has already been constructed, so re-training the constructor and re-compiling the whole knowledge base is often expensive and operationally impractical at scale [Choubey et al., 2024, Wolff and Bennati, 2026], motivating a post-construction agent-compiled knowledge refinement paradigm. Achieving effective agent-compiled knowledge refinement presents two core challenges: (1) defect localization , i.e., identifying problematic regions in a large knowledge base without performing expensive traversal checks; and (2) policy optimization without golden references , i.e., learning effective refinement strategies when no reference refinement actions or golden knowledge bases are available. As shown in the right part of Figure 1, Challenge (1) arises because compiled knowledge bases are typically large and relationally connected, making exhaustive inspection infeasible for locating defects that stem from incompleteness, incorrectness, or redundancy [Dong et al., 2025]. Challenge (2) arises because the LLM-based agents have difficulty refining knowledge bases and learning how to refine knowledge bases. The real-world refinement lacks golden references. We generally do not observe the “correct” sequence of refinement actions that best aligns a knowledge base with downstream tasks. Consequently, learning effective policies requires training objectives that can evaluate refinement quality through downstream task utility signals. To address these challenges, we propose DeepRefine , a general LLM-based reasoning model for agent-compiled knowledge refinement that improves any pre-constructed knowledge base with user queries to make it more suitable for the downstream tasks. DeepRefine follows a three-step reasoning process: Answerability Judgement Loop , Error Abduction , and Refinement Actions Generation . For Challenge (1), instead of traversing the full knowledge base, DeepRefine performs multi-turn query-conditioned interaction with external knowledge to localize potentially defective neighborhoods, substantially reducing refinement complexity while preserving effectiveness. For Challenge (2), DeepRefine abductively infers likely defect causes from interaction histories, then generates refinement actions to update the knowledge base incrementally. Because the utility of refinement actions is only observable through downstream task performance and is non-differentiable, we introduce a Gain-Beyond-Draft (GBD) reward and optimize the refinement policy end-to-end via reinforcement learning. In summary, we make the following main contributions: • To the best of our knowledge, DeepRefine is the first general LLM-based reasoning model for agent-compiled knowledge refinement , capable of refining any pre-constructed knowledge base using only user queries to make it more suitable for the downstream tasks. • We design a three-step reasoning process including Answerability Judgement Loop , Error Abduction , and Refinement Actions Generation . We further introduce a Gain-Beyond-Draft (GBD) reward with reinforcement learning to enhance the knowledge base refinement capabilities of LLMs. 2• We conduct extensive experiments across five datasets to demonstrate that DeepRefine can steadily improve the performance of state-of-the-art baseline methods. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Agent-Compiled Knowledge Base Management 

Agent-compiled knowledge bases are effective because they usually capture relational and multi-hop dependencies, organize evidence hierarchically [Huang et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2025], and support efficient retrieval [Yang et al., 2026]. Different from graph-enhanced RAG methods that largely treat constructed structures as static artifacts [Edge et al., 2024, Jimenez Gutierrez et al., 2024], recent agent memory systems (e.g., Zep [Rasmussen et al., 2025], Mem0 [Chhikara et al., 2025]) emphasize continuous maintenance and evolution. Existing RL-based approaches, such as Mem-α [Wang et al., 2025], Memory-R1 [Yan et al., 2025], and AutoGraph-R1 [Tsang et al., 2025], primarily optimize construction-time knowledge base management policies. In contrast, our work focuses on 

post-construction refinement of already deployed agent-compiled knowledge bases. 

2.2 LLMs and Reinforcement Learning 

Reinforcement learning (RL) [Kaelbling et al., 1996] is an effective optimization framework for improving the sequential decision-making capabilities of LLMs through learning from environmental interaction and reward feedback [Xi et al., 2025]. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) [Ouyang et al., 2022] is one of the foundational methods used to align LLM outputs with preferences. And Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [Schulman et al., 2017], and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [Shao et al., 2024] have provided scalable and efficient optimization algorithms for various structured decision-making tasks in several domains. For example, Graph-R1 [Luo et al., 2025] employs RL to learn effective graph-structured tools navigation to retrieve more accurately. Search-R1 [Jin et al., 2025] trains LLMs to optimize web search queries using RL to maximize final answer correctness and s3 [Jiang et al., 2025] further proposes a principled, model-agnostic reward signal that quantifies improvements over standard retrieval. 

2.3 Knowledge Refinement 

Knowledge refinement aims to mitigate incompleteness , incorrectness , and redundancy in existing artifacts [Subagdja et al., 2024, Cimiano and Paulheim, 2017]. Classical knowledge graph (KG) completion is one of the knowledge refinement methods, such as KG-BERT [Yao et al., 2019] and KGRefiner [Saeedizade et al., 2022], are typically limited to adding links or relations. Entity alignment and disambiguation methods, such as NeuSymEA [Chen et al., 2024] and K-NED [Feng et al., 2020], focus on correction and simplification. In contrast, knowledge refinement has a broader scope that subsumes both types of operations [Cimiano and Paulheim, 2017], yet remains underexplored. TRAIL [Zhao et al., 2025] enables LLM-based agents to iteratively explore and update KGs during reasoning, however, its edits are not explicitly optimized against downstream task utility. Our method instead learns refinement policies with an explicit downstream reward, yielding tighter alignment between knowledge base updates and end-task performance. 

## 3 Preliminaries 

In this section, we formalize the knowledge base and the refinement task with respect to RAG-based downstream tasks. We represent a knowledge base as structured triples that are more general and not limited to classical KG triples. The heads and tails may be entities, events, or document-grounded spans, which we term as knowledge items, and relations may capture taxonomy, time, causality, or cross-document linkage. We denote the pre-constructed knowledge base by a set of triples Gf = {(h, r, t ) | h, t ∈ E f, r ∈ R f},where Ef is the set of knowledge items and Rf is the set of relations. Error Abduction. We assume that there are some potential issues in the knowledge triples of a query-specific subgraph if the query is not answerable with the 0-hop retrieved subgraph. In this reasoning step, given the interaction history Hq[−Lh :] , DeepRefine will abductively reason the potential issues Iq in the retrieved subgraphs from three perspectives: incompleteness, errors and redundancy, which are based on the definition of KG refinement [Subagdja et al., 2024] proposed by Cimiano and Paulheim [2017]. The error reasons will be generated within the <abduction> and 

</abduction> tags as the prompt template demonstrated in Figure 7 of Appendix E. For example, if the query is about a multi-hop relationship between two knowledge items, but the retrieved subgraphs wrongly include or even do not contain some intermediate knowledge items or relations, DeepRefine will reason the incorrect or missing parts that may lead to the unanswerable result. Similarly, as for redundancy, DeepRefine will also check if there is any ambiguous knowledge that would affect the answerability. The analysed results in this step will be served as the reference for the following refinement actions generation.

The generated refinement actions will be parsed into three pre-defined types of operators: insert_edge() , delete_edge() ,and replace_node() . The action scope is designed to refine the knowledge base along the three main targets discussed above. Edge insertion action complements missing relations or knowledge items to address incompleteness. Edge deletion action removes incorrect or redundant relations to mitigate errors and redundancy. 4.2 Refinement Policy Optimization 

Reward Design . To train DeepRefine’s knowledge refinement policy πθ , we consider knowledge refinement as an end-to-end RL problem. Specifically, as shown in the Figure 2, we introduce Gain Beyond Draft (GBD) as the reward objective, which quantifies the gain in RAG generation accuracy achieved by the refined knowledge base ˆGf relative to the draft knowledge base Gf:GBD (q) = ACC (Arefined , A ) − ACC (Adraft , A ), (8) where Arefined and Adraft are the generated answers with the refined knowledge base ˆGf and the draft one Gf, which are both obtained through the simple dense retrieval method we mentioned before. A is the golden answer for the query and ACC (·, ·) is a task-specific metric following the prior work [Jiang et al., 2025] (details see Appendix A.2).

## 5 Experiments 

In this section, we will report the performance of DeepRefine on both RAG and long-term conversation memory benchmarks in Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) settings. We focus on the performance gain of DeepRefine over various baseline methods and the efficiency of the knowledge refinement process. 

5.1 Experimental Settings 

Implementation Details. We implement the DeepRefine training and inference processes using both of the Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507en as the backbone model for their better instruction following capabilities. And we employ the Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B as the embedding model for all vector search in both our method and the baseline methods and the frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct 

as the reader model for the RAG generation. For the 4B model, we use a batch size of 32 with a mini-batch size of 16 and a group size of 6 for the 60 steps’ GRPO training. The learning rate is 

5e-7. For the 8B model, we use a batch size of 64 with a mini-batch size of 16 and a group size of 6

for the 30 steps’ GRPO training. The learning rate is 1e-7. To further improve the efficiency of the refinement process, we select a subset of queries before refinement using a greedy maximum-coverage procedure over query-related triples, as described in Appendix B. Our models are trained on 8×

NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPUs. 

Baselines. We select baseline methods from two perspectives. On the one hand, we evaluate the performance gain from DeepRefine over various knowledge base constructor: base knowledge base constructor, which is denoted as Base , the RL fine-tuned constructor, which employs AutoGraph-R1 [Tsang et al., 2025] and is denoted as AR1 , and the agent-compiled knowledge constructor, which is a version of LLM-Wiki and employs Graphify [Shamsi, 2026]. Both Base and AR1 share the 6same base model Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct . And we uses the Composer-1.5 of Cursor as the agent in Graphify. On the other hand, conditioned on each of the above knowledge base constructor, we evaluate whether the performance gains brought by DeepRefine remain stable across various RAG methods. For graph knowledge retrieval, we employ Subgraph Retriever and ToG . For graph-based text retrieval, we employ HippoRAG [Jimenez Gutierrez et al., 2024] and HippoRAG2 [Gutiérrez et al., 2025]. And we use the same settings of them as those in AutoGraph-R1. 

Training Datasets. We randomly select 5, 000 samples from the training set of HotpotQA [Yang et al., 2018] to construct the training data for DeepRefine, and split them into training and validation sets with a ratio of 8:2 . For each original HotpotQA data point, we construct an individual knowledge base. During the rollout process of the training, DeepRefine only refines the full knowledge base associated with the query in each sample individually. 

Evaluation Datasets. We evaluate DeepRefine on three types of OOD benchmarks, which include 

Simple QA , Multi-hop QA and Conversation QA . For simple QA, we use the Natural Questions (NQ ) [Adelani et al., 2021] and PopQA [Mallen et al., 2023] datasets. For multi-hop QA, we use the 

2WikiMultihopQA [Ho et al., 2020] and Musique [Trivedi et al., 2022] datasets. For conversation QA, we use the LOCOMO [Maharana et al., 2024] dataset. For NQ and PopQA, we adopt a unified knowledge corpus constructed from the lead sections of the December 2021 Wikipedia dump [Izacard et al., 2023]. For 2WikiMultihopQA and Musique, we construct benchmark-specific corpora from the documents associated with each dataset’s 1, 000 evaluation instances, consistent with the settings of Jimenez Gutierrez et al. [2024]. Similarly, for LOCOMO, we also sample 1, 000 evaluation instances from the dataset. 

5.2 Experimental Results 

DeepRefine brings consistent downstream gains over various knowledge base constructors and knowledge retrievers. We refine the knowledge bases constructed by the naive constructor (Naive), the RL fine-tuned constructor (AR1) and the agent-compiled knowledge constructor (Graphify). And we evaluate the performance of the original knowledge bases and the refined knowledge bases with various knowledge retrievers under the OOD settings. As shown in Table 1, with DeepRefine-8B ,the quality of the constructed knowledge bases can be stably improved for their better downstream performance in most cases. Although there are some cases where DeepRefine degrades the perfor-mance, the drops are marginal and slight compared with the cases where DeepRefine improves the performance. This is mainly because with multiple interactions with the external knowledge base, DeepRefine can abduct the potential issues in the knowledge base and then generate refinement actions to fix them. And DeepRefine-8B can perform better than DeepRefine-4B, which suggests the effectiveness of the knowledge refinement process can be improved by a larger scale model. Note that our evaluations are fully under the OOD settings, which demonstrates the generalization ability of DeepRefine. DeepRefine achieves this by being optimized with the GBD reward finetuned RL policy, which is a generalizable reward function for the knowledge refinement task. We have also demonstrate some cases about how DeepRefine can resolve issues in the constructed knowledge bases in Appendix D. 

DeepRefine can sometimes introduce larger or competitive downstream gains than the reconstruc-tion method. The downstream performance of the knowledge bases constructed by a naive knowledge constructor can even outperform AR1 in some cases after they are optimized by DeepRefine, even though DeepRefine does not optimize the whole knowledge base content like the reconstruction method AR1 does. For example, in 2WikiQA, the performance of “HippoRAG2+DeepRefine” with the naive constructor is higher than that of “HippoRAG” with AR1 (53.02 vs. 52.61). And in Musique, the performance of “HippoRAG+DeepRefine” with the naive constructor is much higher than that of “HippoRAG” with AR1 (28.27 vs. 26.21). This is because the downstream performance gain may not always be related to the whole knowledge base content, but may only depend on some specific smaller regions of the knowledge base. Regenerating the whole knowledge base may introduce additional noise that may harm the downstream performance. 

Knowledge base quality and refinement effectiveness can complement each other. As shown in Table 1, the downstream performance gain introduced by DeepRefine would be higher when the refinement process is done on the knowledge bases constructed by AR1 or LLM-Wiki methods. This is mainly because DeepRefine locates the potentially problematic regions in the knowledge bases by iterative subgraph expanding during the multiple interactions with the knowledge base. As shown in Table 2, on all three different kinds of benchmark data, DeepRefine exhibits obvious superiority with respect to the reconstruction method. The efficiency of DeepRefine comes from two aspects. First, instead of taking into account all documents and regenerating the whole knowledge base, the agentic framework of DeepRefine makes the model will only focus on the important and potentially problematic parts, which largely reduces the amount of information that is needed to be processed by the model. Further more, the design of generating refinement actions with the format of code to edit the knowledge base can also further reduce the decoding time cost, which eliminates the need of inefficiently regenerating the subgraph knowledge. Second, as illustrated in Appendix B, we select a subset of queries before the refinement process using a greedy maximum-coverage procedure over query-related triples, which further improves the efficiency by 8avoiding duplicated refinement of the same region of the full knowledge base. Consequently, taking into account both efficiency and effectiveness, DeepRefine is a better choice to improve the quality of agent-compiled knowledge base than the reconstruction methods. ## A Details for Metrics 

A.1 Metrics for Benchmark Evaluation 

Following existing works [Trivedi et al., 2022, Jimenez Gutierrez et al., 2024, Yan et al., 2025], we employ the token-level F1 score as the metric for benchmark evaluation. 

A.2 Metrics for GBD Reward 

Following the prior work [Jiang et al., 2025], we also use the Generation Accuracy (GenAcc) as the task-specific metric for the GBD reward, which is a more robust and stable metric than the token-level F1 score and is more suitable for RL training of DeepRefine. The GenAcc is defined as follows: GenAcc = span_check ∨ judge_check , (10) which is a combination of span-matching test [Ma et al., 2021, Lin et al., 2021] and a LLM-based correctness check with DeepSeek-V3 [Liu et al., 2024] as a judge. Additionally, to effectively shape the refinement behavior of DeepRefine with the GBD reward, we further design a transition-based reward matrix that evaluates the model’s action by comparing the GenAcc of the draft answer ( Adraft ) and the refined answer ( Arefined ). Specifically, we define the reward gain R(Adraft , A refined ) over four possible GenAcc transition states: a successful correction ( 0 → 1)receives the highest reward of +1.0 to strongly incentivize the model to leverage retrieved evidence for error correction, which is the same as the original performance gain of the GBD reward; an erroneous alteration of a correct draft ( 1 → 0) incurs a penalty of -0.3 to discourage over-editing, which is bigger than the original performance gain −1.0 in the GBD reward to prevent the policy from receiving excessive penalties when performing exploration; a correct preservation ( 1 → 1) receives a modest reward of +0.2 to reinforce confidence in already accurate answers; and a failed correction ( 0 → 0) is assigned a neutral reward of 0.0 , as penalizing this state would disproportionately destabilize training given its high natural frequency in complex multi-hop reasoning tasks.

Considering there could be overlappings among the effective regions of the refinement processes with different queries in each benchmark dataset, to make the knowledge refinement process both efficient and effective, we select a small subset of queries before refinement using a greedy maximum-coverage procedure over query-related triples. For each candidate query respect to the full knowledge base in a benchmark dataset, we obtain a set of triples by (i) taking the top-k related triples with their sentence embeddings, and (ii) augmenting with up to m additional triples drawn from the one-hop neighborhoods of the entities appearing in those triples, where neighborhood triples are ranked by embedding similarity to the query 13 and the highest-scoring ones are kept. Treating each distinct triple as a coverage element, we greedily choose queries that maximize the number of newly covered elements until either a budget of B queries is reached or the fraction of covered elements in the union of all candidates’ sets exceeds a target threshold ρ. Only the selected queries are then passed through the refinement pipeline to update the shared knowledge base. Then all benchmark queries are evaluated on the resulting knowledge base with the standard RAG pipeline. The corresponding hyperparameter values are listed in Table 4. For LOCOMO dataset, m is set to 100 and ρ is set to 1.0. For other larger datasets, m is set to 500 and ρ is set to 0.8.

## C Additional Experiments 

To further evaluate the effectiveness of DeepRefine on resolving incompleteness, incorrectness or redundancy issues, we provide a rigorous evaluation. We construct a high-quality benchmark characterized by explicit error types and diverse corruption patterns. By editing the knowledge bases with controlled defects that mirror real-world failure modes, it enables a precise assessment of a model’s performance in defect localization and refinement processes across various error types. We constuct the benchmark based on the HotpotQA (Dev-Distractor) [Yang et al., 2018] dataset. It consists of 501 samples, with 167 samples dedicated to each of the three systematic error types defined in our study. Each data point includes the original query, the corrupted knowledge base, and comprehensive metadata (e.g., corruption details and action counts). The construction pipeline utilizes Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct (denoted as the corruption model) for generating defects, 

Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct as the base knowledge base constructor, and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B for vector-based retrieval. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct serves as the reader model for RAG-based evaluation. 

Error Types and Corruption Strategies. For each error type, we apply a specific corruption strategy to a high-quality knowledge base: Incompleteness : We identify and remove critical triples that are essential for the reasoning path, creating "missing evidence" scenarios. Incorrectness : We modify correct knowledge items into incorrect claims based on the original context, simulating low-confidence or imprecise information. Redundancy : This is achieved via two methods: (1) Inserting semantically similar but redundant triples to introduce ambiguity. (2) Replacing entities in existing triples with aliases or synonymous names to create coreference resolution challenges. 

Construction Pipeline. The construction pipeline follows a rigorous multi-stage pipeline. Initially, for each sample, we construct an individual KB based on its context. We then perform RAG. Only samples achieving a F1 score higher than 0.95 are retained. This ensures that the initial KB is relatively accurate and contains sufficient information to answer the query. Then, for the filtered samples, the corruption process is specifically targeted at the top-N triples retrieved during the previous RAG stage. This ensures that the induced defects are concentrated on the critical knowledge components most relevant to the query, rather than introducing peripheral noise. Within this focused scope, the corruption model generates a series of actions. To ensure the generation of ideal, high-quality defects, we designed specialized prompt templates and carefully curated few-shot samples tailored to each specific error type. These actions are subsequently parsed into three predefined operators: insert_edge(), delete_edge(), and replace_node(). The action space is designed to be symmetric with DeepRefine’s refinement action set to facilitate direct performance mapping. Notably, the replace_node() operator is restricted to acting upon a single, specific edge. This fine-grained precision allows us to create nuanced coreference resolution issues and subtle semantic defects that test the model’s diagnostic depth. To maintain the structural integrity and ensure the refinability of the knowledge base, the total number of corruption actions is strictly limited to 5 per sample. Then we perform a second RAG evaluation on the corrupted KB. Only samples where the RAG F1 score drops below 0.6 are included in the final benchmark. In this work, we propose a general LLM-based reasoning model for agent-compiled knowledge base refinement. Although DeepRefine has shown promising performance in the experiments, there are still some potential improvements that can be made to the model. For example, we only construct the training data from HotpotQA with easy filtering criteria, which makes the quality of the training data not high enough. Finding out higher quality and more diverse training data is a promising direction for future work. And how to combine DeepRefine with a robust systematic refinement framework to make the performance better and more stable is also underexplored. 20
