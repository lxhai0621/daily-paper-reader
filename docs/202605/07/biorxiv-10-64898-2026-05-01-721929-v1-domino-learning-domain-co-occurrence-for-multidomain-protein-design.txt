Abstract 7

Multidomain proteins arise through the reuse and recombination of structural domains, yet natural architectures 8

represent a sparse, structured sample of the possible domain-combination space. Here, we introduce DOMINO, a two-9

stage framework that learns domain co-occurrence from TED-annotated multidomain proteins and uses the learned 10 

patterns to generate new multidomain sequences. DOMIN, a contrastive retrieval model, embeds domains into a latent 11 

compatibility space and retrieves candidate partners for a query domain from a TED-derived domain pool, including 12 

pairings not observed in the TED-derived co-occurrence set. DOMO, a conditional autoregressive sequence model, 13 

converts each retrieved domain pair into a full-length protein sequence by jointly generating the specified domain 14 

regions and the non-domain sequence context between and around them. DOMIN recovers hierarchical patterns of 15 

natural domain co-occurrence and expands the observed CATH homologous-superfamily co-occurrence network with 16 

candidate novel pairings. DOMO realizes both held-out natural pairs and DOMIN-retrieved pairs as proteins with high 17 

domain recovery and high AlphaFold-predicted structural confidence. Applied at scale, DOMINO generated 5 million 18 

retrieval-derived multidomain proteins, with sampled designs showing recovery of the specified domains, diverse CATH 19 

annotations, and sequence novelty relative to UniRef100. Together, these results support domain co-occurrence as a 20 

predictive design prior and demonstrate a scalable strategy for exploring multidomain protein architectures through 21 

new combinations of existing structural modules. 22 

# 1 Introduction 23 

Protein evolution samples sequence space through genetic changes that span multiple scales. Point mutations act at 24 

the residue level to tune protein properties such as activity, specificity and stability [1–3], whereas larger events— 25 

including duplication, insertion, deletion, fusion, fission and recombination—reorganize longer sequence segments and 26 

diversify protein architectures [4]. These architectural changes are often naturally described in terms of protein domains: 27 

compact structural units that frequently serve as functional and evolutionary modules and can fold semi-independently [5– 28 

7]. Domains thus provide a modular framework for protein evolution, allowing existing structural units to be reused, 29 

reordered, duplicated or combined with new partners to produce new regulatory, spatial and functional properties. 30 

Multidomain proteins illustrate that domain modularity is constrained rather than freely combinatorial. Although 31 

fusion, recombination and related genetic mechanisms can place diverse domains within the same polypeptide chain, 32 

only a small subset of possible combinations is observed and retained in extant proteins. Viable architectures must 33 

satisfy biophysical constraints imposed by domain folding, chain connectivity, linker geometry and inter-domain contacts. 34 

Evolutionarily recurrent architectures are often additionally supported by functional coupling through shared localization, 35

historical domain combinations, but also of the constraints that make particular recombinations viable. 39 

These observations raise a broader question: can the rules implicit in natural domain co-occurrence be learned in a 40 

predictive form? Previous studies have systematically catalogued protein domain architectures [12] and modeled domain 41 

co-occurrence relationships as graphs [13], revealing non-random and constrained patterns in how domains are combined 42 

across protein families [14]. However, these patterns have predominantly been used for descriptive analyses of natural 43 

evolution, with limited efforts to develop predictive models of domain-pair plausibility or co-occurrence propensity. A 44 

predictive representation of domain co-occurrence could help identify likely partners for a given domain, distinguish 45 

recurrent or plausible combinations from arbitrary pairings, and generate hypotheses about how structural, functional, 46 

and evolutionary constraints shape multidomain protein organization. Such a representation could also provide a princi-47 

pled basis for designing new multidomain architectures, but this requires connecting domain-level pairing predictions to 48 

sequence-level protein realization. 49 

This question has become newly accessible because protein structures and domain annotations are now available at 50 

unprecedented scale. Structural classification resources such as SCOPe [15] and CATH [6] have long organized protein 51 

domains according to structural and evolutionary relationships, but their coverage was historically limited by the num-52 

ber of experimentally solved structures in the Protein Data Bank [16]. Recent structure prediction methods, including 53 

AlphaFold 2/3 [17, 18], together with the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database [19], have greatly expanded structural 54 

coverage across protein sequence space. The Encyclopedia of Domains (TED) [20] further decomposes this predicted 55 

structural universe into domain-level annotations across hundreds of millions of proteins, revealing extensive domain 56 

diversity, multidomain organization and previously unobserved domain-superfamily interactions. These resources trans-57 

form domain organization from a sparse catalogue into a large-scale statistical system, making it possible to learn domain 58 

co-occurrence as a source of evolutionary, structural and functional constraints. 59 

Despite this progress, it remains unclear whether natural domain co-occurrence can be represented in a form that 60 

supports both partner inference and the construction of full-length multidomain proteins. Although deep learning has 61 

advanced protein structure prediction and design [21–25], most approaches operate primarily at the level of sequences, 62 

structures or local functional motifs, rather than explicitly learning domain-level compatibility across natural multidomain 63 

architectures. Here, we introduce DOMINO (DOMain co-occurrence INference and multidomain prOtein generation), a 64 

two-stage framework that links domain-level compatibility learning with sequence-level generation. First, DOMIN learns a 65 

latent compatibility space from natural multidomain proteins, enabling retrieval of candidate domain partners for a query 66 

domain, including combinations not observed during training. Second, DOMO uses the inferred domain specifications 67 

as conditioning information to generate full-length protein sequences, transforming proposed domain combinations into 68 

coherent multidomain chains. We show that DOMIN recovers hierarchical patterns of natural domain co-occurrence and 69 

identifies structurally plausible domain partners, and that DOMO realizes both natural and retrieval-derived specifications 70 

as foldable, diverse and sequence-novel multidomain designs. Together, DOMINO provides a framework for learning the 71 

organization of multidomain proteins and using the learned compatibility patterns to guide synthetic sequence generation. 72

Fig. 1 Overview of the DOMINO framework. A query domain and candidate TED domains are encoded by SaProt and projected into a contrastively learned compatibility space by DOMIN. Retrieved domain partners are paired with the query domain and supplied to DOMO as residue-level conditioning information. DOMO encodes the query and selected target sequences with an ESM encoder and uses an autoregressive decoder to generate a full-length multidomain protein sequence, including domain regions and intervening sequence context. 

DOMINO separates multidomain protein design into two coupled inference problems: selecting compatible domain 74 

partners and realizing the resulting specification as a single polypeptide sequence (Fig. 1). Given a query domain, DOMIN 75 

retrieves candidate partners from a TED-derived domain pool. The selected query-target pair is then passed to DOMO, 76 

which generates a full-length multidomain sequence conditioned on the two domain specifications. 77 

DOMIN is a contrastive retrieval model over protein domains. Query and candidate domains are encoded by a shared 78 

SaProt [26] encoder and mapped through separate query and target projection heads into a common compatibility space. 79 

SaProt is used in this stage because DOMIN performs retrieval over TED-derived domains with predefined boundaries 80 

and available structural context. In this setting, structure-aware representations can capture fold-level features that 81 

complement sequence patterns and are useful for compatibility retrieval. Training pairs are derived from TED-annotated 82 

natural multidomain proteins, with domains from the same protein treated as positives and non-co-occurring domains 83 

used as negatives. The contrastive objective organizes the latent space according to natural domain co-occurrence rather 84 

than sequence identity alone. At inference, DOMIN ranks candidate TED domains by similarity to the query embedding 85 

in this learned space, producing putative compatible partners for downstream generation. 86 

DOMO is a conditional autoregressive sequence model that realizes a selected domain pair as a full-length protein. In 87 

contrast to DOMIN, DOMO uses a sequence-only ESM [27] encoder to construct residue-level conditioning embeddings 88 

for the query and target domain sequences. This choice reflects the different nature of the generation problem: the model 89 

must generate not only the specified domain regions, but also linkers and other intervening sequence context whose 90 

structures are not known before generation and may depend on the final combined chain. Sequence-based conditioning 91 

therefore provides residue-level information from the selected domains without requiring structural annotations for regions 92 

that are generated de novo. The query and target domain sequences are separately tokenized and encoded with the ESM 93 

encoder. Their residue-level embeddings are concatenated to form a joint domain specification, which is supplied to an 94 

autoregressive decoder that generates the output sequence token by token. Because the decoder attends to domain-level 95 

conditioning rather than copying a preassembled concatenation, DOMO can generate the domain regions together with 96 

linkers and sequence context required for a coherent multidomain chain. 97 

Together, these modules define a hierarchical design procedure in which domain architecture is inferred before sequence 98 

realization. DOMIN narrows the combinatorial space of possible multidomain architectures using a learned co-occurrence 99

# 3 Retrieving and expanding domain co-occurrence patterns with DOMIN 103 

We evaluated DOMIN as a domain-partner retrieval model on a held-out test set using an all-to-all retrieval protocol 104 

(see Section A.1 for dataset construction details). For each query domain, DOMIN ranked all remaining test-set domains 105 

according to their predicted co-occurrence compatibility. The annotated partner domains from the same multidomain 106

Unless otherwise stated, retrieval correctness was defined at the CATH 107 

homologous-superfamily level: a retrieved domain was counted as a correct hit only if its four-level CATH classification 108 

(C.A.T.H) matched that of an annotated ground-truth partner domain. This criterion evaluates whether DOMIN recovers 109 

the correct partner domain type rather than the identical domain instance. 110 

We first analyzed top-1 retrieval results across the CATH hierarchy as a measure of structural relatedness between 111 

retrieved and annotated partner domains. As shown in Fig. 2a, 38.4% of top-1 retrieved domains matched the ground-112 

truth partner at the Homology level (C.A.T.H). Match rates increased at progressively coarser levels of the hierarchy, 113 

reaching 59.5% at the Topology level (C.A.T), 68.7% at the Architecture level (C.A), and 77.8% at the Class level (C). 114 

These coarse-level matches should not be interpreted as retrieval accuracy in the strict sense, but rather as evidence that 115 

DOMIN tends to retrieve domains with related structural classification even when the exact homologous superfamily is 116 

not recovered. 117 

To examine how retrieval quality changes across the ranked list, we computed rank-wise hierarchical match rates for 118 

the top ten retrieved domains (Fig. 2b). The rank-1 Homology-level match rate was 38.4%, corresponding to the strict 119 

C.A.T.H retrieval criterion. Homology-level match rates generally declined with retrieval rank, decreasing from 38.4% 120 

at R1 to 18.3% at R10. However, matches at broader CATH levels remained common across the top ten candidates, 121 

particularly at the Class and Architecture levels. Thus, even when a retrieved domain did not match the annotated 122 

partner at the Homology level, it often remained structurally related to the annotated partner at broader levels of the 123 

CATH hierarchy. These results indicate that DOMIN enriches the retrieved set for domains structurally related to the 124 

annotated partner, including Homology-level matches in a substantial fraction of top-ranked predictions. Because CATH-125 

H superfamilies can contain functionally diverse members and because many biologically plausible partners may be absent 126 

from the annotated natural architecture, these metrics should be interpreted as conservative measures of superfamily-level 127 

co-occurrence recovery. 128 

We next analyzed domain co-occurrence patterns at the level of CATH homologous superfamilies. Natural co-129 

occurrences were defined as pairs of CATH homologous superfamilies observed within the same multidomain protein 130 

architecture based on TED domain segmentation [20]. To identify DOMIN-predicted candidate novel co-occurrences, we 131 

performed all-to-all retrieval on the retrieval database (see Section A.2), using each domain as a query and recording its 132 

top-1 retrieved domain. Each query-retrieval pair was mapped to the corresponding pair of CATH homologous superfam-133 

ilies and compared with the TED-derived natural co-occurrence set. As shown in Fig. 2c, the combined co-occurrence set 134 

contained 36,988 TED-derived natural pairs (75.4%) and 12,058 DOMIN-predicted candidate novel pairs not observed 135 

in the TED-derived set (24.6%). These results show that DOMIN expands the observed CATH homologous-superfamily 136 

co-occurrence space by proposing additional candidate pairings beyond those captured by TED-derived multidomain 137 

architectures. We then examined the distribution of these co-occurrences across CATH class combinations (Fig. 2d). 138 

Natural co-occurrences were unevenly distributed, with class combinations such as 1-3, 2-2, 2-3, and 3-3 contributing 139 

many pairs. DOMIN-predicted candidate novel co-occurrences showed a broadly similar class-level distribution, suggest-140 

ing that they follow coarse-grained structural patterns present in natural multidomain architectures rather than being 141 

uniformly distributed across CATH classes. Finally, we visualized the CATH homologous-superfamily co-occurrence space 142 

as an adjacency matrix, where entries represent TED-derived natural co-occurrences and DOMIN-predicted candidate 143

# 4 Designing multidomain proteins with DOMO 159 

We first examined how training-set refinement and model scaling affect DOMO generation quality. On the held-out TED 160 

benchmark (Section A.1), filtering the training set to retain proteins with pLDDT greater than 70 improved performance 161 

relative to training on the full dataset. On DOMIN-retrieved domain-pair specifications (Section A.2), scaling the model 162 

from 200M to 1.2B parameters produced further gains (Fig. 3c). These results suggest that DOMO benefits from higher-163 

confidence protein sequences and increased model capacity, despite being trained only on sequence data. We therefore 164 

used the 1.2B-parameter model trained on the refined dataset for the following analyses unless otherwise stated. 165 

We next evaluated whether DOMO can realize domain-pair specifications as full-length multidomain proteins. On 166 

held-out TED multidomain proteins (Section A.1), DOMO was conditioned on annotated domain pairs, and the generated 167 

sequences were assessed for recovery of the input domains. For each generated protein, sequence and structural recovery 168 

were computed by matching generated domains to the input domains and averaging recovery across the input domains; 169 

the recovery curves therefore report the fraction of generated proteins whose averaged recovery exceeds each threshold 170 

(Section C.3). DOMO recovered the input domain pairs with high fidelity: 96.8% of the generated proteins achieved a 171 

sequence recovery of 0.9, and 98.1% achieved a structural recovery of 0.5 (Fig. 3a). The generated full-length proteins 172 

also showed high AlphaFold3-predicted confidence, with robust pLDDT and pTM distributions, and remained highly 173 

similar to the native targets in both sequence identity and TM-score. 174 

Beyond reconstructing existing domain arrangements, DOMO also produced alternative organizations of the same 175 

domain set. In approximately 10% of the held-out TED test proteins, the generated sequences realized the target domains 176 

in an order different from that of the native protein (Fig. 3d). These designs nevertheless preserved the same constituent 177 

domains and formed plausible overall architectures, suggesting that DOMO is not limited to reproducing native domain 178 

orderings, but can also generate alternative organizations of the same domain set. 179

We then used DOMO to generate 5 million proteins conditioned on domain pairs produced by DOMIN. In a random 180

We further assessed the novelty of proteins generated from DOMIN-retrieved domain pairs by comparing each gen-185 

erated protein with known proteins in sequence and structure space. For each generated protein, we computed its 186 

highest sequence identity to UniRef100 and its highest structural similarity to PDB structures, measured by TM-score 187 

(Section C.4). The generated proteins covered a wide range of similarities to known proteins (Fig. 3f), with many show-188 

ing low sequence identity to UniRef100 while retaining moderate to high structural similarity to PDB entries. Together 189 

with the strong recovery and AlphaFold3-predicted confidence shown in Fig. 3b, these results suggest that DOMO can 190 

transform DOMIN-retrieved domain-pair specifications into plausible multidomain proteins that are not merely direct 191 

recapitulations of known sequences. 192 

Finally, we examined the diversity and organization of the full set of 5 million proteins generated from DOMIN-193 

retrieved domain pairs. The paired domains covered a broad set of CATH annotations (Fig. 3e), indicating broad coverage 194 

of domain classes among the generated combinations. At the global level, the generated proteins organized into a struc-195 

tured recombination landscape containing diverse representative multidomain architectures (Fig. 3g). Together, these 196 

results suggest that DOMINO can generate a large, diverse, and structured collection of plausible multidomain proteins 197 

from DOMIN-retrieved domain-pair specifications, enabling systematic exploration of multidomain architectures beyond 198 

known natural examples. 199 

# 5 Discussion 200 

Natural multidomain proteins are not arbitrary concatenations of structural modules, but exhibit recurring patterns of 201 

domain co-occurrence that likely reflect a mixture of evolutionary history, structural constraints, chain connectivity, and 202 

functional context. In this work, we used these patterns as a learnable prior for multidomain protein design. Rather 203 

than treating each domain independently or relying solely on manually specified domain architectures, DOMINO learns 204 

from observed domain associations and uses this information to guide the generation of full-length sequences containing 205 

specified domain combinations. The results show that domain co-occurrence provides useful information for proposing 206 

domain partners and for realizing these combinations at the sequence level, supporting the view that multidomain protein 207 

design can benefit from explicit modeling of architecture-level organization. 208 

The proposed domain combinations generated by DOMINO should be interpreted as design hypotheses rather than 209 

validated biological entities. Natural co-occurrence provides evidence that certain domains have been observed together 210 

in the context of a single protein chain and may therefore represent compatible architectural units, but it does not define a 211 

complete set of allowable combinations. Similarly, the absence of a domain pair from current databases may reflect limited 212 

evolutionary sampling, annotation coverage, or dataset bias, rather than true incompatibility. By learning statistical 213 

regularities from observed multidomain proteins, DOMINO prioritizes candidate architectures that are informed by 214

natural organization while still allowing exploration beyond directly observed co-occurrences. These candidates can then 215 

be evaluated by structural prediction, targeted computational analyses, and ultimately experimental characterization. 216 

A major limitation of the present study is that all evaluations are in silico. Domain recovery, predicted structural 217 

confidence, structural similarity, and sequence novelty provide useful evidence that the generated proteins are computa-218 

tionally plausible, but they do not demonstrate that the proteins will express, fold, remain stable, or function in biological 219 

settings. AlphaFold-based confidence scores are useful for large-scale filtering and comparative evaluation, but they are 220 

not substitutes for experimental characterization. Future work should therefore include synthesis and biochemical testing 221 

of representative designs selected across different CATH classes, different degrees of sequence novelty, and different types 222 

of retrieved domain combinations. Measurements of expression, solubility, folding, thermal stability, oligomeric state, 223 

and, where appropriate, functional activity will be necessary to estimate the experimental success rate of the framework 224 

and to determine which computationally prioritized architectures are physically and biologically realizable. 225 

A second important consideration is that multidomain protein design requires evaluation beyond global structure-226 

confidence metrics. Whole-chain pLDDT, pTM, and global structural similarity are informative, but they can obscure 227 

the local features that determine whether a domain combination is architecturally coherent. For domain–domain com-228 

binations, the most critical regions often include domain boundaries, linkers, inter-domain interfaces, and the sequence 229 

context surrounding each domain. A design may have high average confidence while still containing an implausible 230 

linker, a strained domain boundary, or a poorly packed interface. Conversely, a protein with moderate global confidence 231 

may still recover the intended domains and place them in a plausible relative arrangement. Future evaluation should 232 

therefore emphasize multidomain-specific metrics, including domain-wise confidence, boundary quality, linker geometry, 233 

inter-domain contact consistency, interface confidence, and relative domain orientation. Such analyses will be essen-234 

tial for distinguishing proteins that merely contain recognizable domains from those that form coherent multidomain 235 

architectures. 236 

The present work focuses its systematic evaluation on pairwise domain specifications, which provide a controlled 237 

setting for testing whether co-occurrence can be learned and converted into sequence-level designs. However, natural 238 

multidomain proteins frequently contain more than two domains, repeated modules, and higher-order architectural 239 

dependencies. The current framework may in principle be extended to such settings, but the behavior of the model 240 

on larger domain sets has not yet been systematically evaluated. Future studies should therefore examine whether 241 

learned pairwise compatibility patterns generalize to architectures with three or more domains, and whether additional 242 

conditioning information such as domain order, copy number, spacing, or desired inter-domain contacts improves the 243 

design of higher-order multidomain proteins. These extensions would allow a more complete assessment of multidomain 244 

organization beyond pairwise co-occurrence. 245 

Finally, the large-scale designs generated in this study suggest a route for exploring multidomain architectures beyond 246 

those captured in natural protein databases. Natural databases record domain combinations sampled and retained by 247 

evolution, whereas DOMINO can propose additional combinations supported by learned co-occurrence patterns and 248 

favorable in silico properties. With further curation, such design sets could form the basis of future resources for mul-249 

tidomain design, providing prioritized candidates for studying domain recombination, benchmarking design methods, 250 

and selecting targets for experimental testing. Thus, generative design can complement natural domain databases by 251 

highlighting underexplored regions of multidomain protein space. 252

Fig. S1 Overview of the data pipeline used to construct the data splits for DOMIN and DOMO. 

We constructed the training data from TED (The Encyclopedia of Domains) [20], which provides systematic domain 319 

annotations for proteins in the AlphaFold Database (AFDB) [19]. As summarized in Fig. S1, the pipeline consists of a 320 

shared preprocessing stage followed by two task-specific branches, one for DOMIN learning and the other for DOMO. 321 

In the shared preprocessing stage, we first removed proteins with mean pLDDT scores below 70 to reduce noise from 322 

low-confidence AlphaFold predictions, which can propagate to inaccurate TED domain segmentations. We then retained 323 

multidomain proteins and directly adopted the Foldseek clusters assignments [28]. Briefly, Foldseek clusters first grouped 324 

AFDB proteins by MMseqs2 sequence clustering, selected the highest-pLDDT representative from each cluster, and then 325 

constructed Foldseek clusters based on structural overlap. Clusters were split at the cluster level into training, validation, 326 

and test partitions, with 1,000 clusters reserved for validation and 1,000 clusters reserved for testing. This cluster-level 327 

split was used as the common starting point for both downstream branches. 328 

For the DOMIN branch, we further restricted the data to proteins with exactly two annotated domains, matching the 329 

pairwise domain co-occurrence task used for retrieval. The resulting two-domain proteins were separated into training, 330 

validation, and test sets according to the cluster split defined above. Finally, to prevent information leakage, we removed 331 

from the validation and test sets any proteins containing a domain that also appeared in the training set. 332 

For the DOMO branch, we instead retained the broader multidomain set defined by the shared preprocessing stage. 333 

The generation validation and test sets were taken from the held-out validation and test clusters described above. 334 

Similarly, to prevent information leakage, we removed from the validation and test sets any proteins containing a domain 335 

that also appeared in the generation training set. 336 

The statistics of the dataset are summarized in Table S1. 337

B.2 DOMO: domain orchestration 377 

For DOMO, we used a Transformer-based autoregressive model [33], in which ESM2 [34] serves as the domain feature 378 

encoder. The decoder adopts a modern dense design [35], including grouped-query attention [36] with QK-Norm [37], 379 

SwiGLU feed-forward layers [38], rotary positional embeddings [39], and RMSNorm [40] with pre-normalization, while 380 

being augmented with cross-attention to domain-derived conditioning features. 381 

Each input domain sequence was first encoded independently by the domain feature encoder to obtain contextual 382 

residue-level embeddings. For each protein, the embeddings of all input domains were reassembled at the protein level, 383 

concatenated after removing intra-domain padding tokens, and then truncated or padded to a fixed length. The resulting 384 

embeddings were projected by a learned linear layer from the encoder hidden dimension to the decoder hidden dimension, 385 

and together with its attention mask were supplied to the autoregressive decoder as cross-attention memory. This design 386 

enables DOMO to attend to residue-level features from multiple domains while generating the full-length multidomain 387 

protein sequence. 388

# C In silico evaluation 397 

C.1 Retrieved domain pairs are more designable than random pairs 398 

To assess whether retrieval improves the designability of domain combinations, we compared DOMIN-retrieved domain 399 

pairs with several random-pairing baselines. 400 

Pure random sampling . For each query domain, we randomly sampled a partner domain from the full retrieval 401 

pool (Section A.2), without imposing any constraint on structural similarity or natural co-occurrence. 402 

CATH-level stratified random sampling . To examine whether structural similarity alone can account for des-403 

ignability, we constructed random baselines at four levels of the CATH hierarchy. For each query domain, we identified 404 

its native partner domain and then randomly sampled a domain matching that partner at one of the following levels: 405 

• Class (C): same Class (e.g., C1) 406 

• Architecture (A): same Architecture (e.g., C1.A1) 407 

• Topology (T): same Topology (e.g., C1.A1.T1) 408 

• Homology (H): same Homology superfamily (e.g., C1.A1.T1.H720) 409 

Among the 5,000 query domains, 3,452 had native partners with CATH annotations and were therefore eligible for 410 

CATH-level stratified sampling. For these queries, the mean candidate-pool sizes were approximately 11 million, 4 million, 411 

and 1 million domains at the Class, Architecture, and Topology levels, respectively. For the Homology-level baseline, 412 

2,585 of the 3,452 queries had a non-empty H-level candidate pool, with a mean pool size of approximately 160 thousands 413 

domains per query. For the remaining 867 queries, no H-level pool was available and we therefore reverted to Topology-414 

level sampling. Queries whose native partners lacked CATH annotations were instead assigned partners by pure random 415 

sampling from the retrieval pool. 416 

Sampling was performed independently for each query domain, yielding 5,000 query–partner pairs for each strategy. 417 

Using the same set of query domains, the DOMIN baseline was constructed by taking the top-ranked partner retrieved 418 

by DOMIN for each query. DOMO was then used to generate full-length proteins from each query–partner specification, 419 

and the resulting designs were evaluated using AlphaFold3 (AF3) pLDDT and pTM. 420

C.2 Cluster-based evaluation of predicted domain pairs 426 

To evaluate the predicted domain co-occurrences, we clustered domains in the retrieval database at 90% sequence identity, 427 

where each cluster contains multiple domains with different ground truth paired domains. Each cluster representative 428 

was used as a query domain, and the top-1 retrieved domain was compared against all corresponding ground truth paired 429 

domains within the same cluster. The maximum sequence identity and maximum TM-score were retained for analysis. 430 

C.3 Domain recovery metrics 431 

To quantify how well a designed protein realizes its target domains, we defined domain recovery metrics in both sequence 432 

and structure space. Because some TED domains are discontinuous in the primary sequence, a target domain may 433 

correspond to one or more sequence intervals even though these intervals jointly form a single structural domain. The 434 

recovery metrics below therefore operate at the domain level rather than treating each target domain as a single contiguous 435 

sequence segment. 436 

For sequence-based domain recovery, each target domain was represented as the union of its constituent sequence 437 

segments. Each segment was matched against the designed full-length protein sequence using local pairwise alignment, 438 

and the number of identical residues was accumulated across all segments belonging to the same domain. The sequence 439 

recovery score for a domain was then defined as the total number of matched residues divided by the total domain length. 440 

Protein-level sequence recovery was computed by averaging this score across all target domains in the same design. 441 

For structure-based domain recovery, each target domain was first mapped back to its corresponding residue interval 442 

or intervals in the reference protein sequence, and the associated structural fragments were extracted from the reference 443 

structure. When a domain comprised multiple sequence intervals, these fragments were combined to form a single reference 444

C.4 Protein novelty assessment 452 

Protein novelty was assessed in both sequence and structure space. For sequence novelty, each designed protein was 453 

searched against UniRef100 [42] using MMseqs2 [43], and novelty was quantified relative to the closest sequence matches 454 

in the database. For structure novelty, each designed protein structure was searched against the Protein Data Bank 455 

(PDB) [16] using Foldseek, and novelty was quantified relative to the closest structural matches returned by the search. 456 

For protein novelty assessment, we compared each designed protein against known natural proteins in both sequence 457 

and structure space. Sequence-based novelty was evaluated by searching the designed sequences against UniRef100 with 458 

MMseqs2 and using the closest sequence matches to quantify similarity in sequence space. Structure-based novelty was 459 

evaluated by searching the predicted structures of the designed proteins against the PDB with Foldseek and using the 460 

closest structural matches to quantify similarity in structure space. In the main text, UniRef100 sequence identity and 461 

PDB-TM refer to these nearest-neighbor similarities in sequence and structure space, respectively. We considered designs 462 

with UniRef100 sequence identity below 0.3 and PDB-TM below 0.5 to be novel. 463
