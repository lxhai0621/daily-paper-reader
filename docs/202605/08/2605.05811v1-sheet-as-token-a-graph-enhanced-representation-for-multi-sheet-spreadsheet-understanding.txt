Abstract 

Workbook-scale spreadsheet understanding is increasingly important for language-model-based data analysis agents, but remains challenging because relevant information is often distributed across multiple sheets with heterogeneous schemas, layouts, and implicit relationships. Existing retrieval-augmented approaches typically decompose spreadsheets into rows, columns, or blocks to improve scalability; however, such chunk-centric representations can fragment worksheets into isolated text spans and weaken global sheet-level semantics. We propose Sheet as Token , a graph-enhanced framework that treats each work-sheet as a unified semantic unit for multi-sheet spreadsheet retrieval. Our method extracts schema-aware records from sheet names, column headers, representative values, and layout features, and encodes each worksheet into a compact dense token. Given a natural-language query, a Graph Retriever constructs a query-specific candidate graph over sheet tokens using semantic, query-conditioned, schema-consistency, and shape-compatibility relations, and composes these channels through a multi-stage graph transformer to retrieve supporting sheet sets. Experiments on a constructed multi-sheet spreadsheet corpus show that sheet-level tokenization learns stable representations, and that graph-enhanced cross-sheet reasoning improves listwise retrieval over a shallow graph baseline with limited additional graph-side computation. These results suggest that sheet-level tokenization is a promising abstraction for scalable multi-sheet spread-sheet understanding. Code and data are available at https://github.com/xiaoqi-7/SheetasToken/ .

# 1 Introduction 

Spreadsheets remain one of the most widely used formats for organizing, analyzing, and exchanging structured information in enterprise and analytical workflows. They are used for financial reporting, project tracking, sales analysis, inventory management, human-resource records, and many other operational tasks. Recent work has shown that spreadsheet understanding is an important and challenging problem for large language models, because spreadsheets differ substantially from plain text documents: they encode information through worksheets, headers, cell values, formulas, layouts, and workbook-level organization [1, 2 ]. As a result, many real-world spreadsheet tasks require not only understanding an individual table, but also identifying how multiple sheets within a workbook jointly support a user request. In this paper, we study the problem of multi-sheet spreadsheet understanding through query-conditioned sheet retrieval. Given a natural language query and a collection of workbook sheets, the goal is to retrieve the set of sheets that jointly provide the evidence needed for downstream reasoning, question answering, or code generation. This task requires two complementary capabilities. First, the system must understand the semantic role of each sheet, including what the sheet represents, what attributes it contains, and how it is structurally organized. Second, it must model dependencies among sheets, since the answer to a query may depend on multiple related worksheets rather than a single isolated table. 1

A straightforward solution is to serialize the entire workbook and feed it into a long-context language model. However, this approach is expensive, noisy, and difficult to scale. Enterprise workbooks may contain many sheets, large grids, heterogeneous cell types, repeated headers, irrelevant rows, and redundant local values. Full-workbook serialization forces the model to process substantial irrelevant content before identifying the supporting evidence. It also makes the retrieval decision dependent on the model’s ability to sift through a long, semi-structured context, which becomes increasingly inefficient as workbook size grows. Retrieval-augmented generation provides a natural way to reduce this burden by retrieving only relevant external evidence before generation [ 3]. Dense retrieval methods further show that textual units can be embedded into a shared semantic space and retrieved efficiently for downstream question answering [4]. Following this direction, recent spreadsheet-oriented retrieval systems decompose workbooks into fine-grained units such as rows, columns, blocks, or textual chunks, and then retrieve these units using lexical, dense, or hybrid retrieval strategies [ 5]. Such decomposition improves scalability because it avoids encoding the entire workbook at once. However, fine-grained decomposition also changes the retrieval primitive in a way that may be misaligned with multi-sheet spreadsheet understanding. A worksheet is not merely a collection of independent rows, columns, or cell spans. It is a functional unit with a name, schema, shape, representative values, and relationships to other worksheets. Once a workbook is broken into isolated chunks, global sheet identity and cross-sheet dependencies may be weakened or lost. Consequently, a system may retrieve locally similar fragments while missing the coherent set of sheets needed for reasoning across a workbook. This limitation is especially important for queries whose evidence is distributed across temporally related sheets, structurally aligned worksheets, or functionally complementary tabs. Raw Sheets

Raw workbook sheets are first converted into schema-aware records, encoded into dense Sheet Tokens, and then processed by a query-specific Graph Retriever to identify supporting sheet sets. This raises a natural question: if multi-sheet understanding ultimately requires identifying relevant worksheets, should the sheet itself be treated as the basic semantic unit for retrieval? Instead of starting from rows, columns, or blocks, we revisit the representation level of spreadsheet retrieval and argue that each sheet should be represented as a unified object. This perspective is analogous to treating a worksheet as a high-level semantic token in the workbook, preserving its global role while avoiding the cost of full-grid 2encoding. Based on this idea, we propose Sheet as Token , a unified sheet-level representation for multi-sheet spreadsheet understanding. Rather than encoding raw grids or ingesting full rows, we extract sparse but informative schema-aware features from each sheet, including the sheet name, column headers, representative example values, and shape. These features are serialized into a structured semantic record and encoded into a dense vector, which we call a Sheet Token . The goal is not to preserve every cell value, but to retain the essential semantic and structural signals needed to distinguish worksheets and support sheet-level retrieval. Nevertheless, isolated sheet tokens are insufficient for multi-sheet reasoning. Supporting evidence is often relational: a relevant sheet may be connected to other sheets through schema overlap, temporal alignment, shape compatibility, semantic similarity, or query-conditioned co-relevance, even when it does not share direct lexical overlap with the query. Graph neural networks provide a natural inductive bias for modeling such relational structure, since they propagate information through dependencies among connected entities [6, 7]. At the same time, spreadsheets differ from formal relational databases: they often lack explicit schemas, violate normalization assumptions, and encode important relationships implicitly through names, headers, layouts, and workbook organization, unlike the more explicit relational structures studied in recent relational learning benchmarks [8]. To address this challenge, we introduce a graph-enhanced relational retriever for cross-sheet reasoning. As Figure 1, given a query and a candidate set of sheets, the retriever constructs a query-specific graph in which nodes correspond to sheets and edges encode complementary cross-sheet relations, including semantic similarity, query-conditioned relevance, schema consistency, and shape compatibility. We then apply a multi-stage Graph Transformer Network-style architecture to compose these relation channels and perform message passing over the candidate graph. This enables the model to capture multi-hop spreadsheet dependencies before making final retrieval decisions, rather than relying only on isolated query-sheet similarity. Our framework is trained in two stages. First, a semantic Sheet Encoder learns reusable Sheet Token representations from pairwise sheet supervision. Second, a relational Graph Retriever performs query-conditioned listwise retrieval over candidate sheet sets using graph-based reasoning. Experiments on our IndustryTab corpus show that the sheet encoder learns a representation space, while the graph-enhanced retriever improves multi-sheet retrieval. Efficiency analysis further shows that the graph reasoning module adds only a small amount of computation relative to transformer-based encoding, and that Sheet Tokens can be cached offline for efficient inference. In summary, this paper makes the following contributions: 

• We propose Sheet as Token , a unified sheet-level representation that treats each worksheet as a cohesive semantic unit rather than a collection of fragmented chunks. 

• We introduce a graph-enhanced cross-sheet retriever that models query-conditioned multi-hop depen-dencies among candidate sheets using semantic, query-relevance, and schema. 

• We develop a two-stage training and inference framework for multi-sheet understanding, showing that sheet-level representation is stable and that graph-based reasoning improves retrieval with limited additional computation. 

# 2 Related Work 

## 2.1 Table Understanding Datasets and Benchmarks 

Procedural schema-matching fabricators. Valentine [ 9] provides a pipeline for generating paired source 

and target tables from a single sheet via controlled structural partitioning and noise injection. While this yields deterministic column-to-column ground truth for evaluating pairwise semantic alignment, it fails to capture inter-sheet dependencies or workbook-scale context. Consequently, we adopt Valentine’s fabricator to provide column-level supervision within our broader pipeline. 

QA and retrieval over real tables. A second line of work curates QA and retrieval tasks over real-world tables to evaluate long-context reasoning [ 10 ] and realistic query distributions. While these benchmarks capture authentic user intents across varying scales—ranging from single-table reasoning suites [ 11 , 12 ] to 3workbook-scale retrieval over enterprise spreadsheets [ 2, 1, 13 ]—they lack the column-level alignment ground truth and inter-sheet topology necessary for multi-sheet reasoning. Because no existing dataset provides both forms of supervision simultaneously, we construct a novel corpus in Section 4.1 to train and evaluate the two-stage method described in Section 3. 

## 2.2 Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Structured Data 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [ 3] has been increasingly adapted to tabular settings to bypass the scalability bottlenecks inherent in full-context encoding. Unlike traditional open-domain pipelines that process documents as flat, linear text sequences, spreadsheet-based retrieval must actively model the multi-dimensional, semi-structured topology of the data [ 14 ]. Current state-of-the-art approaches, such as FRTR [ 5], attempt to maintain scalability by decomposing Excel workbooks into granular row, column, and block embeddings, which are then surfaced via hybrid lexical-dense retrieval using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF). However, chunk-based decomposition often reduces spreadsheet components to isolated text spans, which may weaken global sheet semantics and cross-sheet dependencies. Drawing inspiration from contrastive representation learning and dual-encoder retrieval architectures [ 4], we instead treat each individual sheet as a unified, cohesive entity within a latent semantic space. The resulting sheet-level representation is designed to prioritize structural and functional consistency over localized keyword matching. Combined with query-conditioned graph reasoning, this design is intended to improve retrieval when relevant sheets share structural, functional, or relational signals even with limited direct lexical overlap. 

## 2.3 Graph Neural Networks for Relational Data 

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) provide a powerful framework for relational data by embedding structural inductive biases into iterative message-passing architectures [ 6, 7]. Unlike k-nearest neighbor ( k-NN) methods that rely on static, feature-space proximity [ 15 , 16 ], GNNs explicitly model complex, heterogeneous interactions through trainable edge aggregation, capturing dependencies that extend beyond simple geometric similarity. While recent frameworks like RelBench [ 8] successfully apply GNNs to formal relational databases, enterprise spreadsheets present a fundamentally different challenge: they lack explicit schemata, heavily violate normalization principles, and encode critical dependencies implicitly through spatial layouts and formulas. To address this gap, our Graph-Enhanced Cross-Sheet Reasoning module specifically adapts graph-based representation learning for this highly semi-structured, schema-free environment.

## 3.1 Problem Formulation 

Let a workbook collection W consist of n sheets, denoted as W = {S1, S 2, . . . , S n}. Each sheet Si is defined as a two-dimensional grid of cells of size Ri × Ci, where Ri and Ci represent the number of rows and columns, respectively. Each cell c(i)

This transformation effectively encodes the entire workbook W as a constellation of points in a d-dimensional manifold, capturing the macro-architecture of the spreadsheet. Before projecting a sheet Si into the Sheet Token space, we decompose it into four typed feature channels, as illustrated in Figure 2. This extends the schema-aware paradigm established by Valentine [ 9]. Each sheet is materialized as a semantic record containing the following fields: 

(i) Sheet name. The concatenation of the workbook filename and the tab identifier. This provides critical high-level domain, entity, and temporal semantics as the strongest single distinguishing signal. 

(ii) Column headers. The canonical column names, which define the sheet’s structural schema. Explicitly encoding this array (e.g., ["Index", "Date", ...] ) provides the model with a lexical map of the data attributes. This captures the table’s semantic axes, which is essential for differentiating sheets that share similar names but contain divergent fields. 

(iii) Example value. The first non-null cell per column, truncated to 60 characters. Extracting a concrete instance (e.g., 2000-01-07 ) resolves overloaded headers without requiring full-row ingestion. High-cardinality text is replaced with typed placeholders to mitigate vocabulary inflation. 

(iv) Shape. The grid dimensions (Ri, C i), which map directly to the num_rows and num_cols .Together, these channels form a sparse, schema-aware representation capturing what the table is about , what it contains , and how it is shaped . By relying on a unified lookup into this preprocessed state, we ensure all downstream stages are evaluated on consistent, deduplicated inputs.

The resulting Sheet Tokens zi serve as the foundational representations for the entire workbook, acting as the initial node embeddings for the Graph Retriever. 63.4 Graph-Enhanced Cross-Sheet Reasoning 

While sheet-level representations capture the local semantics of individual tables, multi-sheet spreadsheet understanding requires reasoning over interconnected sets of sheets rather than isolated tables. For a given query, supporting evidence often spans multiple semantically related, structurally aligned, or shape-compatible sheets. To model these dependencies, we introduce a Graph Retriever that constructs a query-specific candidate graph and performs cross-sheet reasoning before final retrieval. 

Candidate graph construction. Given a query q, we form a candidate workspace C(q) = {S1, . . . , S m},where each node corresponds to a candidate sheet. The query and candidate sheets are mapped into a shared embedding space using the Stage 1 Sheet Encoder. We construct multiple adjacency priors to capture complementary cross-sheet relations. In our setting, these priors encode four signals: semantic similarity, query-conditioned relevance, schema consistency, and shape compatibility. Together, these channels provide a structured relational view of the candidate workspace under the current query. 

Graph retriever. Our graph-enhanced retriever applies a multi-stage graph transformer adjacency generator that repeatedly mixes and composes the primitive relation channels to form a more expressive query-conditioned graph. The resulting graph is then used to propagate information across candidate sheets through graph convolution, allowing the model to capture multi-hop dependencies within the workspace. Finally, the refined node representations are aggregated through a query-guided pooling step to obtain a set-level representation for retrieval. 

Training objective. The model is trained on query-centered candidate sets containing both positive and negative sheets. We optimize the retriever using a joint objective with three components: a set-level contrastive retrieval loss, an alignment term that encourages stronger separation between positive and negative sheets within the same workspace, and node-level binary supervision over candidate relevance. This design jointly supervises workspace-level retrieval and fine-grained node discrimination, enabling the model to learn both which sheet set supports the query and which individual sheets are responsible for that support. 

Summary. Overall, the design provides a principled mechanism for query-conditioned cross-sheet retrieval and serves as the core reasoning module. Detailed formulations are provided in Appendix A. 

# 4 Experiments 

## 4.1 Data preparation 

Our corpus is indexed by a single integer sheet_id and serialized into three JSON files: 

• sheets.json maps each sheet_id to a record of ( filename , dimensions , {header , example }). 

• train.json provides pairwise supervision as tuples (sheet_id 1, sheet_id 2, label ), where the label indicates whether the two sheets originate from the same underlying table. 

• query.json provides listwise supervision as tuples (query , sheet_ids_positive , sheet_ids_negative ),associating a natural-language query with disjoint positive and negative candidate sets. 

Label preparation. Both supervision forms are defined over the same sheet_id space. 

• Pairwise labels. Positives ( label = 1 ) are taken from Valentine outputs (Appendix B details the fabrication procedure). For every positive pair we sample five negatives ( label = 0 ) by pairing sheets from different source workbooks, giving a 1:5 positive-to-negative ratio. 

• Listwise labels. Each query’s positive set is defined by sheets sharing a key attribute (company, fiscal year, quarter, sub-category, or two-attribute combinations). An equal-sized negative set is sampled uniformly from the remaining sheets. 7Dataset statistics. The corpus contains 614 sheets and 1842 pairwise instances (positives:negatives = 1: 5). The listwise set contains 134 queries with average 12.5 positive and negative sheet IDs each. 

Dataset split. We use a 90 /10 pair-level split for the pairwise encoder and an 80 /20 query-level split for the listwise retriever. All reported metrics are computed on these held-out splits. 

## 4.2 Experiment Setup 

Training forward flow. Our pipeline separates sheet-level representation learning from query-conditioned cross-sheet retrieval through two sequential training phases. In Stage 1 , the Sheet Encoder learns reusable sheet-level representations from pairwise sheet supervision. The forward pass fetches the corresponding sheet metadata, serializes each sheet into a structured textual record, and encodes the two records using a shared bi-encoder backbone. The resulting embeddings are passed to a pairwise matching head and optimized with a binary classification objective. In Stage 2 , the Graph Retriever performs query-conditioned listwise retrieval. The query and candidate sheets are encoded using the frozen Sheet Encoder from Stage 1, from which the retriever constructs a multi-channel adjacency prior capturing semantic, query-conditioned, schema, and shape relations. These relation channels are then composed and used for message passing over the candidate graph. Finally, query-guided pooling aggregates the refined sheet representations into a set-level representation for retrieval. The retriever is optimized with a joint objective combining contrastive set retrieval, alignment regularization, and node-level binary supervision. 

Inference execution. During inference, the Sheet Encoder provides semantic representations for candidate sheets, while the Stage 2 Graph Retriever performs query-specific cross-sheet reasoning over the candidate workspace. Given a natural-language query, the system encodes the query and candidate sheets, dynamically constructs a query-conditioned interaction graph, performs cross-sheet message passing, and outputs final relevance scores for the candidate set. After training, sheet representations can be precomputed or cached for efficient retrieval, while final ranking decisions remain grounded in query-specific inter-sheet relationships rather than isolated query-sheet similarity. 

Evaluation metrics. We report accuracy and normalized entropy to analyze the optimization behavior of both training stages. For the Stage 1 Sheet Encoder , accuracy denotes pairwise sheet-matching accuracy. For the Stage 2 Graph Retriever , accuracy denotes listwise retrieval accuracy on the query-conditioned evaluation split. Entropy denotes normalized predictive entropy to measure model confidence during training and evaluation.

## 4.4 Efficiency Analysis 

A key advantage of our framework is that expensive computation is concentrated within a compact retrieval pipeline, avoiding the quadratic overhead of serializing full workbooks into Large Language Model (LLM) context windows. To quantify this efficiency, we report forward FLOPs alongside observed training throughput. This allows us to compare the computational profiles of the Stage 1 Sheet Encoder and the Stage 2 Enhanced Graph Retriever under identical configurations. As detailed in Table 1, the Stage 1 Sheet Encoder requires approximately 45.9 GFLOPs per pairwise sample, achieving a throughput of 35–39 samples per second on one NVIDIA A40 GPU. The Stage 2 Graph Retriever is computationally heavier due to the multi-sheet workspace, requiring roughly 235 GFLOPs per query instance with a throughput of ∼18 queries per second. Our schema-aware features only include sheet names, headers, representative values, and shape. This design improves efficiency and avoids full-grid ingestion, but it may miss fine-grained evidence stored in individual cells, formulas, charts, formatting, comments, or embedded visual elements. In addition, our graph retriever models semantic, query-conditioned, and schema relations among candidate sheets, but the current experiments use a limited set of graph variants and baselines. Future work will evaluate the framework on larger real-world workbooks, stronger chunk-based and long-context baselines, multi-seed statistical tests, and downstream spreadsheet reasoning tasks. 

# 6 Conclusion 

Existing spreadsheet retrieval methods often rely on either full-workbook serialization or fine-grained chunk decomposition. While these strategies provide useful baselines, they may incur high computational cost, fragment sheet-level semantics, and weaken the cross-sheet dependencies needed for multi-sheet reasoning. Motivated by these limitations, we revisit the retrieval primitive for multi-sheet spreadsheet understanding and propose Sheet as Token , a unified sheet-level representation that encodes each worksheet as a compact semantic object using sparse schema-aware features. The resulting Sheet Tokens preserve essential sheet identity and structural signals while avoiding full-grid ingestion, allowing sheet representations to be cached for efficient inference. Since multi-sheet reasoning often depends on relational evidence beyond isolated query-sheet similarity, we further design a graph-enhanced relational retriever to model semantic, query-conditioned, and schema-level dependencies among candidate sheets. Experiments on IndustryTab show that the proposed framework learns stable sheet-level representations and that graph-enhanced reasoning improves multi-sheet retrieval.

Pair generation. We use the Valentine fabricator [ 9, 17 ] with column overlap in [0 .5, 0.7] and row overlap of ≈ 0.5. Three noise channels are applied: 

• Schema noise. We use the table-name prefix rule from Valentine (e.g., Vendor →Sales_Vendor ); the abbreviation and vowel-deletion rules are not applied. 

• String noise. Following Valentine’s keyboard-proximity scheme, non-ASCII characters are first transliterated via unidecode ; each resulting character on the source side is then independently replaced by a keyboard-proximity neighbor with probability p = 0 .20 .

# C Ablation Studies 

To understand the contribution of both the graph retriever architecture and the Stage 1 sheet serialization strategy, we conduct ablations on the listwise evaluation split under a matched setting. We use the same Stage 2 training protocol and keep the Sheet Encoder frozen during retrieval training. Under this controlled setup, the full model employs the example-enhanced Stage 1 Sheet Encoder together with the Stage 2 Enhanced Graph Retriever. We then study two ablated variants: a shallower retriever (formulated in Appendix A) with the shared Stage 1 initialization, and an enhanced retriever initialized from a Stage 1 encoder trained without column examples. The ablation results support both design choices in the final system. Compared with the full model, the Shadow model achieves lower final evaluation accuracy and higher entropy, showing that the enhanced graph retriever provides stronger query-conditioned cross-sheet reasoning under the same frozen-encoder setting. Removing column examples causes an even larger drop in retrieval accuracy and leads to less confident optimization behavior, indicating that richer Stage 1 sheet serialization is also critical for downstream retrieval quality. Overall, these results suggest that the gains of the final system arise from both the stronger Stage 2 graph retriever and the example-enhanced Stage 1 sheet representation.
