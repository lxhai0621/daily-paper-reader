# Abstract 

Structural generalization in semantic parsing requires systems to apply learned 

compositional rules to novel structural combinations. Existing approaches either 

rely on hand-written algebraic rules (AM-Parser) or fail to generalize structurally 

(Transformer-based models). We present an alternative requiring no hand-

written compositional rules, based on a neural cellular automaton (NCA) with 

a discrete bottleneck: all compositional rules are learned from data through 

local iteration. On the SLOG benchmark, the system achieves 100% type-exact 

match on 11 of 17 structural generalization categories, including three where 

AM-Parser scores 0 to 74%, with an overall standard deviation of 0.2 across 

10 seeds (vs. AM-Parser’s 4.3). Analysis reveals that all 5,539 failure instances 

reduce to exactly two mechanisms: novel combinations of wh-extraction context 

with reduced verb types, and modifiers appearing on the subject side of verbs. 

When we decompose results by CCG structural features, each sub-pattern either 

succeeds on all instances or fails on all. Intermediate scores (e.g., 41.4%) are 

mixtures of structurally distinct CCG patterns, not partial generalization. All 

failures correspond to directed operations absent from training; all successes 

correspond to operations already covered. 

# 1 Introduction 

Structural generalization is a central challenge in semantic parsing  [1] : systems must apply com -

positional rules learned from training data to structural combinations never seen during training. 

This differs from lexical generalization (applying known rules to new words) and distributional 

generalization (handling samples outside the training distribution). Structural generalization 

demands mastery of  compositional rules themselves , what Humboldt called “the infinite use 

of finite means”  [2] .

On benchmarks specifically designed to test structural generalization, such as COGS  [3]  and SLOG 

[4] , end-to-end trained neural networks (including T5  [5]  and LLaMA  [6] ) perform poorly  [7] ,

with overall accuracy around 40%. The most successful approach to date is the neuro-symbolic 

AM-Parser  [8] , [9] , which uses a neural network as a supertagger (predicting algebraic types) and 

hand-written AM algebra for compositional reasoning, achieving 70.8%±4.3 overall accuracy on 

SLOG, significantly outperforming end-to-end models. 

This paper follows the neuro-symbolic line but proposes a  fully learnable  (requiring no hand-

written compositional rules) alternative: discrete bottleneck encoding via Gumbel-Softmax  [10] ,

combined with the local iterative dynamics of a neural cellular automaton (NCA  [11] ) for compo -

sitional reasoning. The key difference from AM-Parser is that compositional rules are not hand-

written AM algebra but local update rules automatically learned by the NCA through training. 

The entire system is end-to-end differentiable, with approximately 81K parameters (excluding the 

frozen BERT encoder).

On the 17 structural generalization categories of the SLOG benchmark, the system achieves 100% 

accuracy on 11 categories, including three that AM-Parser cannot handle (RC_iobj_extracted: 100% 

vs. AM-Parser 0%). Across 10 random seeds, 15 out of 17 categories have zero standard deviation, 

with an overall standard deviation of 0.2 (compared to AM-Parser’s 4.3). 

Detailed analysis of the results reveals clean success/failure patterns. When test samples are sub-

classified by CCG (Combinatory Categorial Grammar) structural features, every structural pattern 

either succeeds on all instances or fails on all instances. Categories with surface-level intermediate 

scores (such as Q_modified_NPs at 41.4%) are in fact mixtures of structurally distinct CCG patterns. 

# 2 Background 

2.1  Structural generalization and COGS/SLOG 

COGS  [3]  is a compositional generalization benchmark for semantic parsing, containing 21 

generalization categories (3 structural, 18 lexical). SLOG  [4]  extends COGS by adding 17 structural 

generalization categories, covering complex syntactic phenomena including relative clauses (RC), 

wh-questions, and center embedding. 

The SLOG training set is identical to that of COGS (32,755 examples), but the gen set contains 

17,000 test samples (1,000 per category), requiring systems to parse correctly on structural combi -

nations never seen during training. Notably, the training data contains no relative clauses or 

wh-questions whatsoever. These structures appear only in the test set, by deliberate design of the 

benchmark. 

2.2  AM-Parser and the neuro-symbolic approach 

AM-Parser  [8] , [9]  decomposes semantic parsing into two steps: (1) a neural network predicts 

the AM algebra type of each word (supertagger), and (2) hand-written AM algebra rules perform 

compositional reasoning. This design ensures that structural generalization ability derives from 

the compositional closure of the algebraic rules, rather than depending on specific structural 

combinations present in the training data. 

AM-Parser has demonstrated the strongest structural generalization on both COGS  [12]  and 

SLOG, achieving 70.8%±4.3 on SLOG. Supertagging-based approaches have also shown promise on 

COGS  [13] . However, the hand-written nature of its rules means that extending to new linguistic 

phenomena requires manually designing new algebraic operations, and the system cannot be 

embedded in an end-to-end differentiable training pipeline. 

2.3  Combinatory Categorial Grammar (CCG) 

CCG  [14]  is a lexicalized grammar formalism whose core feature is encoding  directionality  in 

types: 

• 𝑆  \ NP : requires an NP on the left to produce S (backward application) 

• 𝑆 / NP : requires an NP on the right to produce S (forward application) 

In the CCG framework, directionality is not an optional add-on but a core property of combinatory 

operations. 

We do not use the full CCG combinatory calculus. Our system retains forward and backward appli -

cation ( 𝐴 /𝐵  + 𝐵  → 𝐴  and  𝐵  + 𝐴  \ 𝐵  → 𝐴 ) but simplifies several standard CCG mechanisms 

into atomic types with explicit merge rules:

not to build a CCG parser but to use CCG’s directed types as training targets and analytical tools 

for a learnable system. 

# 3 Method 

3.1  Design motivation 

Our goal is to build a system that inherits the compositional reasoning spirit of AM-Parser while 

making all components learnable. AM-Parser’s architecture is “neural supertagger + symbolic 

algebra”; ours is “neural encoder + discrete bottleneck + neural local iteration.” 

The discrete bottleneck corresponds to the supertagger’s discrete output in AM-Parser: compress -

ing continuous representations into finite symbols to provide clean input for subsequent compo -

sitional reasoning. The NCA’s local iteration corresponds to the AM algebra’s compositional rules: 

achieving structural composition through local interactions between positions. The difference is 

that the rules are learned from data, not hand-written. 

3.2  Overall architecture 

The system consists of four components: 

1.  Encoder : a frozen BERT  [15]  encodes the input sentence into contextualized word vectors. 

Function words (articles, “was”, “did”, etc.) are stripped after encoding, retaining only content 

words. 

2.  Discrete bottleneck : Gumbel-Softmax discretizes the continuous BERT embeddings into one 

of  𝐾  = 32 codes. This step strips BERT’s global information, restoring the locality that the NCA 

requires. A straight-through estimator is used during training; argmax during inference. 

3.  NCA reasoning layer : code embeddings serve as the NCA’s initial state and undergo  𝑇  steps 

of local iteration. Each step’s update depends only on the current position and its left and 

right neighbors (Conv1d kernel=3  → GELU  → Conv1d + Tanh bottleneck + LayerNorm). All 

positions share the same parameters. 

4.  Readout head : an independent linear projection at each position ( ℝ64 → ℝ24, no nonlinearity, 

no cross-position interaction). It maps the NCA’s continuous state to logits over 24 CCG types. 

Structural reasoning is entirely performed by the NCA dynamics; the readout head merely 

performs measurement. 

Key design choices: 

• No skip connections : all cross-position information must propagate through the NCA 

dynamics and cannot bypass them. 

• No backpropagation through time : the first  𝑇  − 1 steps do not retain gradients 

(detached rollout); only the final step backpropagates. 

• T-curriculum : 𝑇  is gradually increased from 1 to 60, so the NCA learns short-range 

rules before long-range dynamics. 

33.3  CCG type system and training objective 

We use 24 CCG types to cover all syntactic structures in COGS and SLOG. Of these, 20 base 

types (NP, S,  𝑆  \ NP , (𝑆  \ NP )/ NP , passive, prepositions, etc.) cover all 21 COGS categories; 4 

extension types (RC_THAT, TV_GAP, S_GAP, WH) cover the relative clauses and wh-questions 

added in SLOG. 

CCG types are deterministically derived from gold logical forms (LFs). CKY parsing performs 

bottom-up merging on type sequences, producing parse trajectories that serve as NCA training 

targets: initial types provide the supervisory signal for the encoder, and final types (head surviving, 

other positions collapsed to  ∅) provide the supervisory signal for the NCA dynamics. 

3.4  Evaluation metric 

We use  type exact match  as the primary evaluation metric: a test sample is scored correct when 

the predicted CCG types at all positions match the gold CKY trajectory. This is a structure-level 

metric that measures whether the system has correctly completed the CCG parse. 

Given correct type predictions, CKY parsing and subsequent semantic edge extraction are fully 

deterministic symbolic operations, meaning that type correctness entails structural correctness. 

We verified this: on the full 17,000 gen samples, the agreement between CKY edge extraction and 

gold LF edges is 99.9% (16,987/17,000). The remaining 13 discrepant samples arise from argument 

structures that are isomorphic in the CCG type system but differ in semantic roles (e.g., a transitive 

verb and a ditransitive verb missing one argument share the type  (𝑆  \ NP )/ NP ). 

Therefore,  type exact match  ≈ edge exact match  ≈ LF exact match . Concretely, the system’s 

overall LF exact match is 67.3% (at most 13 samples below the 67.4% type exact match), directly 

comparable to the LF exact match reported by AM-Parser. 

3.5  Training details 

• Data: SLOG training set (32,755 examples), 100% CKY coverage 

• Optimizer: AdamW (lr= 10−3, weight decay= 10−4)

• Gumbel temperature: annealed from 1.0 to 0.1 over 50 epochs 

• Parameters: 81K (excluding frozen BERT) 

• Training time: approximately 20 minutes (single GPU)

# 5 Analysis 

5.1  Two failure mechanisms 

The fine-grained analysis above reveals a unifying structure: all failures, regardless of SLOG label, 

reduce to exactly two CCG mechanisms. 

Mechanism A: verbs appear with reduced argument types in wh-extraction contexts.  The 

training data contains no wh-questions, so verbs always retain their full argument types: transitive 

verbs are always  (𝑆  \ NP )/ NP , CCOMP verbs are always CCOMP. In wh-questions, the extracted 

forward argument ( / direction, i.e., object) no longer appears in the sentence, and the verb must 

appear with a type missing that argument (e.g.,  (𝑆  \ NP )/ NP  → 𝑆  \ NP ). Note that  𝑆  \ NP  is 

not itself a new type: intransitive verbs already carry this type in training. What is truly absent 

is the  combination  of this type with a wh-question context: the encoder has never needed to 

assign a reduced type to a transitive verb in a wh-context, and the NCA has never processed 

the local context of WH + reduced verb type. Q_long_mv further compounds this with cross-

clause extraction: CCOMP verbs never appear in wh-questions during training (novel trigram: 

WH NP CCOMP). Categories affected: Q_dobj_ditransV (all 1,000 samples), Q_iobj_ditransV (all 

1,000 samples), Q_long_mv (all 1,000 samples), and the 586 active object extraction samples in 

Q_modified_NPs. 

Mechanism B: modifiers appear on the subject side (left of verb).  In the training data, PP/ 

RC modifiers always appear on the object side (right of verb). The modification operation itself 

is identical on both sides ( NP  + NP  \ NP  → NP ), and  NP  \ NP  is not a new type. What is truly 

absent is the  position  of the modified NP: the object-side NP is consumed by the verb via forward 

application (verb on the left, NP on the right), while the subject-side NP combines with the verb 

via backward application (NP on the left, verb on the right). All modifiers in training appear on the 

forward application side; the backward application side has never been seen. Categories affected: 

PP_modif_subj (all 1,000 samples) and the 953 samples in RC_modif_subj that directly modify the 

main clause subject.

5.2  Comparison with AM-Parser 

AM-Parser uses direction-agnostic AM algebra (the paper explicitly states “there are no forward 

or backward slashes in AM types”), and its results exhibit an empirical pattern consistent with our 

CCG analysis. 

On categories involving Mechanism B (PP/RC on subject side), AM-Parser is the only system that 

achieves substantial nonzero results on the subject side (PP_modif_subj 57.6%, RC_modif_subj 

55.8%), whereas systems without injected algebraic rules (NCA, T5) score near 0% on these 

categories. This suggests that AM-Parser’s nonzero results come from the explicit injection of its 

direction-agnostic algebra, not from generalization learned from training data. 

It is noteworthy that the NCA outperforms AM-Parser on object-side modification (PP/ 

RC_modif_iobj: 100% vs. 90.4%/74.4%), while AM-Parser outperforms the NCA on subject-side 

modification (57.6%/55.8% vs. 0%). The complementarity of the two systems corresponds precisely 

to their respective design choices: the NCA learns entirely from data and is more precise on opera -

tions covered by training; AM-Parser injects direction-symmetric algebra and achieves nonzero 

performance on operations that training does not cover but the algebra can express. 

5.3  Intersection of CCG features and benchmark categories 

SLOG’s category labels are named after linguistic phenomena (“PP modification”, “wh-questions 

with modified NPs”), which is a reasonable organizational scheme. However, when we re-partition 

the data using CCG directed features, two patterns orthogonal to the SLOG labels emerge: 

1.  CCG features split SLOG labels. Q_modified_NPs (41.4%) is split by CCG into subject extraction 

(100%) and object extraction (0%); RC_modif_subj (4.7%) is split into main clause subject modi -

fication (0%) and complement clause subject modification (100%). These intermediate values are 

9not “partial success”; they reflect CCG-structurally distinct patterns grouped under the same 

label. 

2.  CCG features merge different SLOG labels. The failure portions of PP_modif_subj and 

RC_modif_subj share Mechanism B (modified NP on the left side of the verb); Q_dobj_ditransV, 

Q_iobj_ditransV, and Q_long_mv share Mechanism A (forward argument extraction). From the 

CCG perspective, they are the same phenomenon. 

This suggests an empirical observation: for predicting the success and failure of structural 

generalization, CCG directed type features offer higher resolution than phenomenon-level classi -

fication. Structural generalization is fundamentally about “the infinite use of finite means,” but 

what counts as “the same rule” depends on the theoretical framework. SLOG’s design is based on 

phrase-structure grammar assumptions (a modified NP is equivalent to a bare NP), which is a well-

motivated theoretical choice. Yet our experimental results suggest that CCG type sequences may 

be a worthwhile alternative evaluation framework to explore. 

# 6 Discussion 

6.1  Resolution of CCG features 

This paper uses CCG types as both training targets and analytical framework. When decomposed 

by CCG directed features, all surface-level intermediate scores in SLOG (41.4%, 4.7%) resolve into 

deterministic 0%/100% sub-patterns. This resolution is invisible under SLOG’s original phenom -

enon-level classification. The model’s clean binary results simultaneously reveal two things. First, 

within the CCG theoretical framework the model has learned the correct compositional rules: 

all directed operations present in the training data are precisely mastered, and all absent ones 

consistently fail. Second, SLOG’s category boundaries do not partition data along CCG structural 

boundaries. Patterns that are structurally distinct in CCG are mixed under a single label, and CCG 

features reveal finer structure in the benchmark data itself. 

6.2  System ceiling and NCA dynamics 

The system is approaching its theoretical ceiling on SLOG: within the CCG framework, all gener -

alizable categories already reach 100%, and all non-generalizable categories correspond to directed 

operations missing from training. Further improvement would require expanding the training data, 

which would alter the benchmark’s generalization testing nature. Injecting hand-written algebraic 

rules (such as AM-Parser’s direction-agnostic MOD) can bypass some CCG constraints, but this 

abandons the fully learnable design goal. 

The NCA’s iterative dynamics exhibit bottom-up type merging behavior, consistent with the 

proto-CKY emergence we previously observed on formal languages  [17] : local iterative rules 

spontaneously learn hierarchical structure parsing under minimal supervision. The theoretical 

foundation of this NCA architecture is described in  [18] .

6.3  Limitations of LF evaluation and CCG types as an alternative target 

COGS/SLOG use logical forms (LFs) as the prediction target, but LF as an evaluation medium 

has inherent limitations. In  Table 2 , T5′s plain exact match on Q_dobj_ditransV is 47.2%, while 

reformatted exact match is 98.5%. More than half of the “errors” are merely differences in variable 

naming or conjunct ordering; the model has actually understood the structure. This is not an 

isolated case: prior work has noted that COGS’s LF format imposes formatting constraints on 

seq2seq models that are unrelated to structural understanding, systematically underestimating 

models’ semantic capabilities  [19] .

The fundamental problem with LF evaluation is that it conflates two things:  structural 

understanding  (whether the model has mastered the correct compositional rules) and  format 

compliance  (whether the model happens to produce a particular string format). For testing 

structural generalization, the former is the target; the latter is noise. 

10 CCG type sequences as an alternative evaluation target avoid this problem. The core design feature 

of CCG is the synchronous composition of syntax and semantics: each type reduction (e.g.,  (𝑆  \

NP )/ NP  + NP  → 𝑆  \ NP ) simultaneously performs syntactic merging and semantic function 

application, with grammatical types directly determining the mode of semantic composition. CCG 

type sequences therefore simultaneously encode structural information and semantic composition 

rules, without requiring an additional LF representation layer to capture semantics. As an evalu -

ation target, types are discrete labels rather than strings, eliminating ambiguities from variable 

naming or conjunct ordering; types explicitly encode directionality; and the evaluation metric 

(type exact match) directly measures structural-semantic understanding without the indirection 

of LF generation. 

More generally, linguistic theories are not incorrigible axioms; they should be subject to empirical 

testing. The deterministic behavior of learnable systems on theory-driven benchmarks provides a 

new kind of empirical signal for adjudicating between competing theories. 

# 7 Conclusion 

This paper presents a fully learnable structural generalization system that inherits the neuro-sym -

bolic spirit of AM-Parser while replacing hand-written algebraic rules with learned local iteration. 

The system achieves 100% on 11 of 17 SLOG categories (including three that AM-Parser cannot 

handle), with an overall standard deviation of 0.2 across 10 seeds (compared to AM-Parser’s 4.3). 

CCG analysis of the results shows that all 5,539 failure instances reduce to exactly two mechanisms: 

forward argument extraction changing verb types, and modified NPs moving from the right side 

to the left side of the verb. Both are, in essence, directed CCG operations absent from training. 

After reclassification by CCG features, every sub-pattern in SLOG yields deterministic results (0% 

or 100%); the surface-level intermediate values (41.4%, 4.7%) are mixtures of distinct CCG patterns. 

CCG features provide higher resolution on SLOG data than SLOG’s own phenomenon-level labels. 

This is an empirical fact about the structure of the benchmark data, independent of any particular 

model choice. Our results suggest that CCG type sequences may be a worthwhile alternative 

evaluation framework to explore.
