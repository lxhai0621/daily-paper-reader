Abstract 

Large Language Models (LLMs) excel academ-ically but struggle with social intelligence tasks, such as creating good compromises. In this paper, we present methods for generating em-pathically neutral compromises between two opposing viewpoints. We first compared four different prompt engineering methods using Claude 3 Opus and a dataset of 2,400 contrast-ing views on shared places. A subset of the gen-erated compromises was evaluated for accept-ability in a 50-participant study. We found that the best method for generating compromises between two views used external empathic sim-ilarity between a compromise and each view-point as iterative feedback, outperforming stan-dard Chain of Thought (CoT) reasoning. The results indicate that the use of empathic neutral-ity improves the acceptability of compromises. The dataset of generated compromises was then used to train two smaller foundation models via margin-based alignment of human preferences, improving efficiency and removing the need for empathy estimation during inference. 

1 Introduction 

Living in liberal, plural societies requires compro-mise and consensus. While these two components are essential for healthy deliberation and decision-making, they are not the same. For example, while multiple parties in one household could prefer dif-ferent types of meat for dinner (e.g., chicken, beef, lamb), they might come to a consensus that they all want meat for dinner, but they may also compro-mise by agreeing to delay eating beef and lamb for another time and eating chicken tonight, because there is only chicken in the refrigerator.

Recent research shows that LLMs can promote democratic deliberation by fostering consensus (de-veloping statements different participants can agree on) and reducing polarization, often outperform-ing human mediators in clarity and inclusiveness (Tessler et al., 2024). Additionally, Bakker et al. (2022) show that fine-tuning LLMs to generate con-sensus statements from diverse individual prefer-ences enhances group acceptance of the statements more efficiently than traditional negotiation pro-cesses. However, these examples concern using LLMs to develop consensus statements across broad view-points. Developing compromises between different and opposing viewpoints, on the other hand, re-quires a greater degree of social intelligence that LLMs may lack (Xu et al., 2024). Although LLMs can produce empathetic responses (Welivita et al., 2021; Sabour et al., 2022; Majumder et al., 2020) and can generate consensus statements, they have not been used to generate neutral compromises. Generating compromises are fundamentally differ-ent, as successful compromises (i.e., acceptable to both parties) require proposing resolutions that make both parties better off compared to in the ab-sence of any compromise (Van Parijs, 2012). This thus requires attending to a solution space that al-lows for differing viewpoints to both find positives compared to their original stances. Recent stud-ies show that while LLMs can analyze disputes and generate intervention messages, their effective-ness as neutral mediators remain inconsistent (Tan et al., 2024). Similar concerns arise in humani-tarian frontline negotiations, where neutrality and sensitivity are critical. Interviews with practition-ers highlighted that, although LLMs can synthesize large amounts of information, they may be biased,

which can lead to mixed outcomes when supporting neutral negotiation processes (Ma et al., 2024). Inspired by these earlier works, this paper ex-plores whether LLMs can generate neutral compro-mises for contrasting views (i.e., generate compro-mises that do not favor either view). This ability would be valuable in scenarios where impartiality is crucial, such as collaborative decision-making and conflict resolution. Particularly, we investigate the following research questions: 

RQ1: Can prompt engineering be used to generate compromises that are empathically neutral between two diverse viewpoints (about a place)? 

RQ2: Can smaller, open-source LLMs replicate empathically neutral compromise generation capa-bilities based on a larger, proprietary model, and remove the need for explicit empathy estimation during inference? To address these questions, we propose a task that focuses on generating compromises from a pair of contrasting views (Figure 1). As a first ex-ploration of this task, we use pairs of contrasting positive and negative viewpoints about safety and welcomingness of public spaces from Chen et al. (2025). The positive views (view_A) are about feel-ing either safe or welcome and the negative views (view_B) are about feeling less safe or excluded. Each viewpoint includes a participant’s proposal for improving the safety or welcomingness of a place. In our task, generated compromises for im-proving safety or welcomingness between a pair of positive and negative views should: (i) consider both suggestions and (ii) be empathically neutral. Since greater empathy helps people to understand each other’s opinions and viewpoints, empathy is important to compromise, which requires conces-sions from the parties involved. While individual parties agree to a compromise because they would be worse off without it (Van Parijs, 2012), if two views are judged to be similarly empathetic towards a compromise, or empathically neutral , the prob-ability of finding a suggested compromise accept-able should naturally increase. To address RQ1, we explore several prompt-based controls to guide the generation process. We investigated guiding the model toward empathic neutrality by providing explicit feedback on the empathic similarity (Shen, 2023) between each of the two views and the generated compromise (Sec-tion 3.2). When the empathic similarity scores across both views are balanced, the generated com-promise is considered empathically neutral . A 50-participant human evaluation of a subset of the compromises was conducted to assess the accept-ability of the different compromise generation ap-proaches and showed that compromises generated by the model informed with empathic neutrality was highly preferred. To address RQ2, we use the compromises gener-ated in RQ1 to explore an alignment technique to teach empathetically neutral compromise genera-tion abilities to smaller, pretrained language mod-els (Liu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024) Motivated by how humans internalize social norms not just by observing conforming behavior, but by distinguish-ing instances of norm conformation from instances of norm violation (Bicchieri, 2005) and fair and unfair outcomes (Fehr and Fischbacher, 2004), we use NCE-based alignment to explicitly contrast val-idated compromises against inadequate alternatives. Our experiments show that the approach improved the quality of smaller LLM-generated compromises over fine-tuning alone, which only exposes models to acceptable compromises. In this paper we present a method that uses em-pathic neutrality to enhance prompt engineering to generate compromises. These compromises are then used, together with poorer quality compro-mises, to teach an alignment model to align smaller models. In the following sections, we describe the different prompt-based models that we inves-tigated and the automatic and human evaluation of the generated compromises. We also describe the method for aligning smaller language models to generate empathically neutral compromises that we investigated. 

2 Related work 

LLMs excel in academic tasks (GPT-4 achieving 92.1% on MATH) but exhibit limitations in social intelligence, scoring only 54.4% on SESI bench-mark (Xu et al., 2024). Similar limitations appear in interactive gaming contexts, where LLMs per-form approximately 20% below human baseline in theory of mind tasks (Liu et al., 2024). Stud-ies from First-person perspective confirm that de-spite possessing basic theory of mind capabilities, LLMs show considerable limitations in managing complex social interactions compared to human performance (Hou et al., 2024). Current evalua-tion methods include traditional psychological as-sessments like ToMi (Le et al., 2019) and special-ized datasets such as SocialIQA (Sap et al., 2019), Figure 1: Example showing two contrasting views, View_A and View_B, two compromises generated for the two views using prompt engineering, and notes about each compromise. Compromise 1 balances both views and is empathicly neutral, while Compromise 2 does not. 

SocKET (Choi et al., 2023), and SECEU (Wang et al., 2023). However, newer benchmarks like SO-TOPIA (Zhou et al., 2024) and EmoBench (Sabour et al., 2024) face limitations in reflecting real-world interactions. Prior work generated consensus state-ments using supervised fine-tuning and re-ranking (Bakker et al., 2022), or involved humans in se-lecting common ground (Tessler et al., 2024). We instead generate multiple compromise suggestions from open-ended prompts, aiming for balanced empathy across differing views without human se-lection or re-ranking. 

Synthetic datasets offer a promising solution for enhancing LLMs’ social capabilities (Ghana-dian et al., 2024; Hassan et al., 2024; Balog et al., 2024; Gabriel et al., 2024). These datasets leverage LLMs’ generative abilities (Dankar and Ibrahim, 2021) while ensuring diverse representation (Ya-magishi and Nakamura, 2024). Advanced prompt engineering techniques, particularly using GPT-4 or Claude (Achiam et al., 2023; Anthropic, 2023), have improved dataset quality (Hikov and Murphy, 2024; Shi et al., 2023). Chain of Thought (CoT) approaches (Wei et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2023) show enhanced reasoning capabilities (Shao et al., 2023), with significant improvements in specific tasks (Nong et al., 2024). Structured Chain-of-Thought (SCoT) (Sultan et al., 2024) fur-ther improves accuracy in document-grounded QA conversations. Recent development suggest using parameterized soft prompts instead of traditional hard-prompting (DeSalvo et al., 2024). 

Model alignment with human preferences has progressed from Reinforcement Learning from Hu-man Feedback (RLHF) (Kaufmann et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2022) to more efficient alternatives (Liu et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b). While RLHF aligns models with complex human values through reward models and policy optimization (Song et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024), it remains resource-intensive and complex (Schulman et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2024a; Hong et al., 2023). Direct Preference Opti-mization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2024) simplifies training by removing the reward model and opti-mizing the likelihood of preferred responses. Ex-tensions such as reward model distillation (Fisch et al., 2024) and KL regularization (Kullback and Leibler, 1951; Wesego and Rooshenas, 2024; Azar et al., 2024) further enhance stability. DPOP (Pal et al., 2024) adds a penalty to maintain preference alignment with the reference model. InfoNCA and NCA (Gutmann and Hyvärinen, 2010; Chen et al., 2024) adapt DPO for multi-response settings by optimizing absolute likelihoods. Finally, methods like TODO (Guo et al., 2024) and RPO (Yin et al., 2024) introduce ternary ranking and contrastive weighting to capture finer-grained preference sig-nals. This study focuses on aligning model outputs in the context of neutral compromise generation. Prompt engineering is guided by the empathic sim-ilarity between viewpoints and generated compro-mise, and this setup is used to generate synthetic training data. This data is used for fine-tuning and aligning pretrained LLM to generate these compro-mises. 

3 Compromise Generation using Empathy-Informed LLM 

This section describes the dataset used for our ex-periments and the generation of candidate compro-mise sentences through diverse prompt engineering techniques. 

3.1 ContrastingViews Dataset 

We adapt the public EmpathyFromPerspectives dataset Chen et al. (2025) composed of pairs of contrasting viewpoints by human participants about feeling safe/less safe and welcome/excluded in pub-lic spaces (see Appendix C for a summary). Each pair of viewpoints includes: view_A, which is ei-ther a safe or welcome viewpoint and a correspond-ing view_B, which is either a less safe or excluded viewpoint. Each viewpoint is composed of a place description, reasons for the viewpoint, and sug-gested improvements. We randomly selected 1200 safe/less safe and 1200 welcome/excluded pairs for our generation experiments, and refer to this subset as the ContrastingViews dataset. 

3.2 Empathic similarity model 

We adopt the empathic similarity framework from Shen (2023), which computes an empathic similarity score sim (fθ(s1), f θ(s2)) between pairs of stories (s1, s 2), where higher scores indicate greater alignment in empathic content. While Shen (2023) used Sentence-BERT (Reimers, 2019), we replace it with the e5-large model (Wang et al., 2022) for improved sentence representation. To adapt the similarity model to our scenario, we com-bined 2,000 annotated story pairs from Shen’s EM-PATHICSTORIES dataset (Shen, 2023) with 1,000 story pairs from the ContrastingViews dataset. Prior work (Schopf et al., 2023; Hupkes et al., 2022) has shown that such pretrained encoders can support semantic generalization even when ex-posed to mismatched discourse styles, such as nar-rative and structured text. The combined dataset of 3,000 pairs is used to train the similarity model with a 75/5/20 train/validation/test split. During inference, this model estimates empathic similarity between a generated compromise and each of its corresponding pair of viewpoints. 

3.3 LLM generation of compromise data 

We aim to generate compromises for each given pair of positive and negative views for a place. Each view consists of (i) description of the place (ii) rea-son of the view (iii) suggestions of improvement. In order to use all this information as a context for feeding into an LLM, we follow a prompt template similar to the Alpaca prompt (Taori et al., 2023). Recent work suggests that proprietary LLMs excel at synthetic dataset generation (Cascante-Bonilla et al., 2023; Lei et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2023) with the help of task specific prompt based strategies, providing an alternative to human data collection. We use Claude 3 Opus (Anthropic, 2023) for gen-erating compromise data. Using a single compromise as the target con-strains model generalization capabilities (Gong et al., 2019). We generated multiple candidate compromises per view pair, enhancing the model’s ability to learn diverse resolution patterns. Addi-tionally, we observed that single prompts were inad-equate (see section E of the appendix) for our task as they frequently focus on only one perspective, which necessitated our exploration of more sophis-ticated prompt engineering techniques. All three CoT-based strategies share a common structural principle designed to promote balance between the two viewpoints. Each begins by separately ex-tracting the suggestions from view_A and view_B before identifying commonalities. This explicit decomposition ensures that both perspectives are represented in the model’s reasoning context prior to compromise generation, preventing the model from anchoring on one viewpoint, a failure mode we observed frequently with single prompts (Sec-tion H). The strategies differ in how they reinforce this balance using empathic similarityafter initial generation, as described below. 

Chain of Thought (CoT) : We initially used CoT (Chu et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2022) to identify sug-gestion similarities and derive compromises, as illustrated in Figure 2. This approach, however, proved ineffective, the generated text either empha-sizing a single suggestion too heavily or extending beyond their intended scope, ultimately leading to less suitable compromises. 

CoT+LLM : LLMs have demonstrated the poten-tial to refine their outputs through self-generated feedback, closely aligning with human refinement techniques (Madaan et al., 2024). In this approach, we integrated LLM-based self-evaluation scores (Figure 2). These scores measure empathic simi-larity between the generated compromise and each view, ideally achieving high similarity for both perspectives with minimal discrepancy. 3.4 Candidate compromise selection 

For each prompting strategy, four compromises are collected per view pair (resulting in 4 × 4 = 16 

compromises per view pair across four prompting strategies: Single Prompt, CoT, CoT+LLM and CoT+Feedback). From this set of 16, the top four responses are selected as candidate compromises (step 2 in Figure 3) by ranking them according to an estimate of empathic neutrality (see Table 1). Neutrality is evaluated using the similarity model to compute the empathic similarity between the gener-ated compromise and both view_A and view_B, de-noted as score_A and score_B , respectively. Then empathic neutrality is computed as the absolute dif-ference in empathic similarity |score A − score B |

In an ideal state of empathic neutrality, the absolute difference between these scores approaches zero.

4 Human Evaluation of Compromise Methods 

The previous evaluation indicates how well the gen-erated compromises achieve empirical empathic neutrality as measured by comparing the empathic similarity of two opposing viewpoints. We next conduct a human evaluation. 

4.1 Evaluation Methodology 

For this, we asked 50 humans to rate compro-mises generated by the Single Prompt, COT , and 

COT+Feedback methods. The participants were shown pairs of statements about modifications pro-posed for a place, where one statement was written by someone who thought positively about the place and the other statement was written by someone who felt negatively about the place. Instead of ask-ing directly how fair a compromise was, which could be biased by the rater’s personal perspective and from cognitive priming and carryover effects of viewing two viewpoints sequentially (Epley and Gilovich, 2006), a rater was asked to take the view-point of either the person who felt positively or who felt negatively for all pairs of statements presented (assigned viewpoint). The number of raters taking the positive and negative viewpoints was balanced. For each pair of statements, raters were asked to rate on a scale of 1-100 the acceptability of each of five compromises from their assigned viewpoint. One of the “compromises” was the positive or neg-ative statement that they were not to identify with (‘Opposing View’). The four other compromises were generated: one by Single Prompt, one by COT ,and two by the COT+Feedback method. Each par-ticipant rated the compromises for five pairs of statements, providing ratings from the positive and negative viewpoints for a total of 100 pairs of state-ments and 400 generated compromises. For details about the collection of compromise ratings, see Appendix D. 

4.2 Preference Results 

Table 2 shows the percentage of time that each of the compromise methods was the top choice (First Preference) and second choice (Second Pref-erence). We observed that COT+Feedback com-promises were most preferred (COT+Feedback 1: 37% first, 33% second; COT+Feedback 2: 40% first, 29% second), outperforming CoT (18%, 24%) and Single Prompt (5%, 13%). The opposing view was rarely preferred (0%, 1%). The results shown in Table 3 indicates that the preference for COT+Feedback is statistically significant compared to the Single Prompt baseline (p ≤ 0.002 ) for both the Wilcoxon and Permuta-tion tests. We pursue an analogous goal compu-tationally: aligning smaller, pre-trained language models so that empathically neutral compromise generation becomes an intrinsic capability rather than an externally guided one. Instead of using standard fine-tuning to teach a model to imitate target outputs, we use an approach that teaches a model to distinguish good compro-mises from poor ones. This choice is motivated by cognitive research on social norm acquisition showing that humans learn evaluative standards through contrastive experience, e.g., comparing norm-conforming behavior against violations (Fehr and Fischbacher, 2004; Bicchieri, 2005). We adopt Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE) (Gutmann and Hyvärinen, 2012; Chen et al., 2024), which directly optimizes the absolute likelihood of preferred outputs without requiring a separate reference model. This is important for our task because the model must retain broad world knowledge to generate contextually appropriate compromises. In contrast, alignment methods that optimize likelihood ratios, such as DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024), risk suppressing the absolute likelihood of even preferred outputs, degrading generation quality. 

5.1 NCE based alignment 

We train an NCE model using the candidate com-promises preferred by human evaluators (Section 4.2) as positive examples to align the model with verified human social preferences. The negative examples are the fine-tuned base model’s outputs, which serve as contrasting cases. For training efficiency, all parameters are frozen except the last three layers. Let s_target denote the sum of log-probabilities that the model assigns to the tokens of the candidate compromise, and let s_hypo denote the corresponding sum for the fine-tuned base model’s own generated output. 6 Experimental setup on alignment 

Datasets: We use the ContrastingViews dataset together with the synthetic compromises generated by CoT+Feedback, described in Section 3, to vali-date our alignment targets. Our smaller models are trained, when given a pair of opposing views, to mimic the compromises, whose quality was evalu-ated by humans in Section 4. Thus our alignment process optimizes towards a socially valid objec-tive. The ContrastingViews dataset together with the genereated compromises were split into training (75%), development (5%), and test (20%) subsets. 

Setup: For all the experiments, we use Llama-3.1 8B (Dubey et al., 2024) and mistral-7B in-struct (Jiang et al., 2023) as base models (referred as Llama and Mistral-7b). We initially fine-tune the base models for one epoch and use this fine tuned model for further alignment tasks. We imple-mented a linear warm-up and cosine decay sched-uler to dynamically adjust the learning rate with an initial learning rate of 3e−5. Optimization is per-formed using the Adam optimizer with β1 = 0 .90 

and β2 = 0 .99 . Through empirical tuning, we found that a weight margin value of 10 was optimal for task-based loss alignment. We train for 8 and 12 epochs for the NCE-based alignment and task-loss based alignment, respectively. 

Evaluation metrics: We employ a multi-faceted evaluation strategy: (i) Generation Quality: We report ROUGE scores (Lin, 2004) to measure se-mantic alignment with the human-validated candi-date compromises (see Table 4). (ii) Empathic Neutrality: We measure the "neutrality gap" (|score A − score B |), where lower values indicate better balance between viewpoints. 7.1 Quantitative Evaluation 

As shown in Table 4, initial generations from pre-trained baseline models perform poorly in produc-ing high-quality compromises ( row 1 ). To ex-plore sampling-based improvement, we generate five samples using multinomial sampling and se-lect the best one based on the evaluation metrics (row 2 ). However, sampling still fails to produce suitable compromises. Fine-tuning these models for a single epoch improves performance ( row 3 ), but remains insufficient. Although DPO is a stan-dard baseline, we excluded it from this generation quality analysis because our initial experiments re-vealed it caused significant catastrophic forgetting (detailed in Appendix K). Given that social rea-soning requires retaining broad world knowledge, we focused on our proposed alignment algorithms. This leads to consistent and substantial improve-ments in ROUGE scores across both Llama and Mistral-7b, thereby demonstrating the effective-ness of alignment-based training objectives for the compromise generation task ( rows 4 and 5 ). In Appendix L, we describe our investigation of a task-based alignment objective with contrasting examples, which also lead to improved ROUGE scores. And we explored conditioning the genera-tion on participant demographics (i.e., age, gender) to test if explicit social context improves neutrality. However, our experiments yielded no statistically significant performance gains (Section J).

8 Conclusion and Future Work 

Our work presents an initial framework for improv-ing the social intelligence of LLMs for the novel task of empathically neutral compromise genera-tion. We propose and conduct human evaluation of the effectiveness of several prompting strategies, and show that use of empathic similarity improves performance. We also show that alignment meth-ods enable smaller, open-source models (Llama-3.1 8B and Mistral-7B) to approach the compromise generation quality of Claude 3 Opus, the propri-etary model used to generate the training data. To extend this work, we hope to explore socially intel-ligent tasks beyond place-based conflicts, includ-ing ethical or policy disagreements, and develop methods that further maximize empathy toward both viewpoints. This would help to broaden the framework’s applicability to real-world mediation scenarios. References

The COT+feedback approach sought to produce compromise text that is equally acceptable to both viewpoints. In the future, we would also like to include consideration of maximizing the empathy of each viewpoint to the compromise. Our collection of compromises was constrained to keep the data collection required manageable. The compromises are generated based on two dif-ferent human viewpoints for a similar type of place. Although many of the comments about a place ap-ply generally to a place type, i.e., trails and fencing in a park, in a real scenario, the expressed view-points are about the same type of place, rather than exactly the same. We chose this approach because collecting compromise data for the same place would be a much larger undertaking. The prompts were based on feeling safe/unsafe or welcome/ex-cluded. Generalization to other prompts was not done because this would have also increased the required size of the negative viewpoints. These generalizations are left for future work. While this place based focus on safety and inclu-sivity enables a controlled exploration of empathy-neutral compromises, it inherently limits the frame-work’s immediate generalization to broader real-world scenarios, such as interpersonal conflicts, policy debates, or collaborative decision-making in non-spatial domains (i.e., ethical dilemmas in team projects or ideological disagreements in com-munity forums). The reliance on physical "places" as the locus of conflict may overlook nuances in abstract or multifaceted disputes, potentially reduc-ing the framework’s utility for diverse mediation contexts where viewpoints diverge on intangible issues. To enhance generalizability, future work could expand the dataset to include contrasting view-points on non-place-based topics, such as political policies (i.e., environmental regulations) or social norms (i.e., workplace diversity initiatives), by scal-ing negative viewpoint collection through crowd-sourcing platforms or synthetic augmentation tech-niques. This would test the framework’s robustness across domains, potentially incorporating multi-turn dialogues to simulate ongoing negotiations. While our neutrality metric captures empathic balance, it does not account for societal biases (i.e., race, gender, class) present in inputs or LLM gen-erations. LLMs may overlook power asymmetries or ethical subtleties due to limited social reasoning. Moreover, the dataset’s narrow domain limits gen-eralizability. Future work should explicitly evaluate and mitigate such biases. The Human Study comparing different compro-mise generation texts presented only a sample of 4 of the generated texts per viewpoint pair, rather than all 16, because humans would have difficulty rating all items reliably. 

B Ethical Considerations 

Our goal in this work was to generate compromises that are equally acceptable to the two parties with different viewpoints. Similar to the general dangers of some people trusting all results generated by an LLM, the generated compromises should be viewed as suggestions for discussion areas, rather than a compromise that each person should agree to. Our work is a first step . In the future, it should be used as an aid rather than being relied upon directly. However, a possible future misuse is in sensitive conflict scenarios, where the compromises are not used as suggestions but instead as direct solutions. This is a societal problem, similar to other issues that generative AI has created, such as the generation of fake videos of real people and the generation of mental health advice.

G Iterative Prompt Engineering Procedure 

This section describes the iterative prompt engi-neering procedure used to generate collected com-promises guided by a similarity model. The process consists of a fixed prompt component (computed once) and an iterative refinement loop that reuses these fixed outputs. 

G.1 Fixed Prompt Component (Computed Once) 

The following prompt steps are executed once at the beginning, and their outputs are cached and reused throughout all iterations: Fixed Prompt - Computed Once    

> Output 1: Identify the suggestions in view_A
> Output 2: Identify the suggestions in view_B
> Output 3: Identify similarities in suggestion between view_A and view_B
> Note: These outputs (suggestions_A, suggestions_B, similarities) are fixed and reused in all subsequent iterations.

G.2 Step 1: Initial Compromise Generation 

Using the fixed outputs from above, generate initial compromises: Generation Prompt - Step 1    

> Input: suggestions_A, suggestions_B, similarities (from fixed prompt)
> Prompt: Create N empathically neutral compromises using the similarities
> Output: Compromise_1, Compromise_2, ..., Com-promise_N

The generated compromises are then evaluated by the Similarity model using Claude API, which produces empathy similarity scores ( score_A ,

score_B ) for each compromise with respect to view_A and view_B. 

G.3 Step 2: Iterative Refinement 

Using the same fixed outputs, iteratively refine the compromises: Refinement Prompt - Step 2 (Iterative)      

> Input:
> •suggestions_A, suggestions_B, similarities (from fixed prompt) • compromise (from previous iteration) • score_A, score_B (from similarity model)
> Prompt: Create N better responses with higher em-pathy similarity score
> Output: Refined_Compromise_1, ..., Re-fined_Compromise_N

Iterative Process: Each refined compromise is evaluated by the Similarity model to obtain updated scores. The compromise with improved scores can be further refined by repeating Step 2, while always reusing the fixed prompt outputs (suggestions_A, suggestions_B, similarities) computed in the initial step. This ensures consistency across iterations while allowing the generation component to focus on improving empathy similarity scores.

To evaluate the impact of our alignment approach on pretrained knowledge retention, we assess how well the model preserves its capabilities. We use the WikiText test set (from WikiText-2-v1) 4 (Mer-ity et al., 2018), a widely used benchmark for mea-suring catastrophic forgetting (Fawi, 2024). We compute the average log-likelihood 5 across the dataset. A decrease in log-likelihood indicates that the model is forgetting previously learned knowl-edge. As shown in Table 6, our method better preserves pretrained knowledge compared to prior approaches, such as Direct Preference Optimiza-tion (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2024). This retention is critical for generating compromises that rely on the model’s foundational understanding.

wmargin (weight margin) modulates the influence of ROUGE difference during training. Note that while ROUGE is non-differentiable, our objective avoids direct backpropagation through the metric. The term (target rouge − hypo rouge ) is computed during the forward pass and treated as a static scalar margin detached from the computation graph. This effectively scales the gradient applied to the log-probabilities, penalizing hypotheses proportional to their semantic deviation from the target without requiring differentiable approximations.
