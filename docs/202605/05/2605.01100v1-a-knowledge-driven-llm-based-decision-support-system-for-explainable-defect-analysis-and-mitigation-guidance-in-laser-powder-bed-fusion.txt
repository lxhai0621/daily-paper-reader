# A Knowledge-Driven LLM-Based Decision-Support System for Explainable Defect Analysis and Mitigation Guidance in Laser Powder Bed Fusion 

Basit Mahmud Shahriar a , Md Habibor Rahman a,  1 

> aDepartment of Mechanical Engineering, University of Massachusetts Dartmouth, Dartmouth, MA 02747, USA

# Abstract 

Identification and diagnosis of defects in metal additive manufacturing processes, such as Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF), are knowledge-intensive tasks that require in-depth domain expertise encompassing defect ontologies, their causal relationships, and defect-specific control strategies. In practice, this knowledge is often scattered across the literature and difficult to integrate into decision-support workflows. While researchers have introduced Large Language Models (LLMs) and ontology-driven frameworks for information retrieval and decision support, existing approaches often rely on unstructured web-scale knowledge or static document retrieval and therefore provide limited support for domain-specific defect–process relationships, actionable mitigation guidance, and interactive defect exploration in manufacturing environments. To address these limitations, this work presents a knowledge-driven decision-support system that integrates structured defect knowledge with LLM-based reasoning to provide explainable defect diagnosis and mitigation guidance in manufacturing, using LPBF as a representative, safety-critical case study. The proposed ontology-integrated LLM-based decision support system for LPBF defect analysis and mitigation guidance is built on a knowledge base containing 27 known LPBF defect types organized into hierarchical categories and causal relationships. The developed system supports fuzzy natural language queries for systematic knowledge retrieval, literature-supported explanation of defects, and guidance on defect causes and mitigation strategies derived from encoded process knowledge. Furthermore, a multimodal image-assessment module based on foundation models enables descriptor-guided interpretation of representative microscopic defect images through semantic alignment scoring. The proposed framework was evaluated through qualitative comparisons with general-purpose vision-language models, an ablation study, and an inter-rater reliability analysis. Evaluation on the literature-derived dataset showed that the fully integrated configuration outperformed the other three evaluated system configurations, achieving a macro-average F1 score of 80.8%. Additionally, inter-rater reliability analysis using Cohen’s kappa indicated substantial agreement ( 𝑘𝑘 = 0.66 ) between the model outputs and the literature-derived reference labels. These findings suggest that ontology-guided knowledge representation can improve the consistency, interpretability, and practical usefulness of LLM-assisted LPBF defect analysis. The proposed approach helps bridge the gap between fragmented research knowledge and practical shop-floor decision-making by providing a structured, explainable, and human-centered workflow for defect exploration, assessment, and guidance on defect mitigation. 

Keywords : Knowledge-driven decision support; large language model; laser powder bed fusion; defect analysis and mitigation; metal additive manufacturing. 

# 1. Introduction 

Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) is one of the most widely adopted metal additive manufacturing (AM) processes for producing geometrically complex components for aerospace, biomedical, energy, and other high-performance applications. Despite its advantages, LPBF remains highly sensitive to process-induced defects, such as porosity, cracking, balling, surface roughness, layer delamination, and dimensional distortion [1]. These defects can compromise part integrity, repeatability, and qualification, and create significant challenges for process planning and quality assurance. In practice, effective diagnosis and mitigation of LPBF defects remain highly knowledge-intensive because relevant information is distributed across defect taxonomies, process studies, microscopy-based observations, and material-specific parameter investigations reported in the literature [2,3]. As a result, translating fragmented research knowledge into timely, actionable engineering guidance remains difficult, particularly for users and practitioners without deep domain expertise.

In response, prior research has explored several relevant directions, including structured knowledge representations for defect modeling, data-driven and vision-based approaches for defect analysis, and more recent large language model (LLM)-based systems for technical querying and decision support. Recent studies have investigated structured knowledge representations, such as ontologies and knowledge graphs, to systematically represent LPBF defects and their associated causal knowledge, thereby enabling intelligent and explainable reasoning in LPBF defect analysis. Knowledge graph embedding techniques, for example, enable the representation of defects, process parameters, and causal relationships in machine-readable form, supporting classification and semantic querying [4]. These approaches are valuable because they impose conceptual structure on a domain characterized by inconsistent terminology, overlapping defect descriptions, and multilevel cause–and– effect relationships. However, knowledge-based representation approaches often rely on static query mechanisms and lack the interactive, flexible reasoning capabilities needed for real-time, human-centric decision support on the shop floor. In parallel, data-driven and vision-based approaches have been developed for defect detection, process optimization, and in situ monitoring [5,6]. Although these methods can achieve strong predictive performance, their black-box nature offers limited transparency and interpretability for defect diagnosis, root cause analysis, and mitigation guidance [7]. More recently, LLMs and retrieval-based systems have emerged as promising tools for technical querying, multimodal reasoning, and decision support in scientific and engineering contexts, as modern manufacturing paradigms increasingly emphasize human-centric AI systems that augment, rather than replace, expert decision-making [8–12]. LLM–based agents have been investigated for defect classification in microscopy images [9], decision support in healthcare [13], and the generation of natural language–driven simulation models [14], illustrating the growing capabilities of such agents to support reasoning and decision-making. While these developments improve accessibility and user interaction, their effective use in manufacturing remains constrained by the need for domain-specific knowledge control, traceable reasoning, and protection against unsupported or overly general outputs. 

To address these limitations, recent studies in defect detection and diagnosis have increasingly focused on linking large language models with structured knowledge representations. For example, Ma et al. proposed a knowledge graph-enhanced large language model pipeline for fault diagnostic reasoning and maintenance decision support, showing how structured domain knowledge can improve reasoning and support maintenance decisions in Industry 5.0 settings [15]. Zhou et al. (2024) developed CausalKGPT for root-cause analysis of quality problems in aerospace product manufacturing, leveraging a causal knowledge graph to enhance its reasoning capabilities [16]. Liu et al. (2024) presented a collaborative knowledge graph and large language model framework for fault diagnosis in aviation assembly, in which graph-structured knowledge is incorporated into the reasoning workflow through retrieval augmentation and model adaptation, without retraining the model [17]. These studies demonstrate that generative models based on and constrained by structured domain knowledge can significantly improve domain specificity and interpretability. However, comparable work remains limited in additive manufacturing defect analysis, especially in LPBF, where real-time decision support requires hierarchical defect organization, interpretation of representative defect micrographs, and defect-specific mitigation guidance. 

Existing LPBF-focused studies further illustrate this gap. LLM-based approaches like AdditiveLLM have demonstrated the ability to predict a limited number of defect regimes, such as keyhole defect, lack of fusion, and balling, using structured inputs and natural language prompts to achieve robust classification and process parameter guidance [18]. Retrieval-augmented generation systems such as AMGPT support contextual querying of the additive manufacturing literature [19], while prior ontology-based work has provided a formal representation of LPBF defect-cause relationships [1]. Vision-based approaches have also shown strong capability for detecting and classifying hidden internal defects in additively manufactured parts [10]. However, these approaches generally address isolated capabilities, such as prediction, retrieval, representation, or detection, without integrating structured knowledge, flexible reasoning, and explainable interaction into a unified decision-support framework. LPBF-focused LLMs are also limited in defect coverage and lack explicit representation of defect hierarchies, inter-defect relationships, literature-supported explanation, and interactive support for defect investigation. A brief analysis of recent studies on LPBF defect analysis approaches and methods, as well as advanced LLM-driven diagnostics in broader manufacturing sectors, summarized in  Table 1 , provides an overview of the scope for implementing LLM agents in additive manufacturing defect identification. Prior work reveals a persistent gap between structured knowledge of defects, interactive reasoning, and actionable decision support for LPBF processes. Page 3 of 28 

To address this gap, this work develops a knowledge-driven decision-support prototype for LPBF defect analysis and mitigation guidance by integrating an ontology-guided hierarchical knowledge base with LLM-based reasoning and interactive query handling within a unified workflow. The proposed framework is built on a structured representation of LPBF defect knowledge derived from published taxonomies and is implemented to support interactive defect exploration. The resulting knowledge base contains 27 LPBF defect types organized into hierarchical categories, together with curated causal information and mitigation guidance drawn from the literature. Within the proposed workflow, the large language model serves as the reasoning and interaction layer, enabling defect exploration via natural language queries and multimodal inputs. At the same time, the knowledge base constrains the analysis through curated defect categories, causal mappings, and, where available, material-specific mitigation guidance. Rather than functioning as a black-box predictor, the proposed LPBF defect agent is intended as a human-in-the-loop decision-support prototype that supports interactive defect exploration, literature-supported explanation, and hypothesis-guided interpretation of representative defect micrographs.

The proposed knowledge-driven LLM-based decision-support system performs four related functions: (1) interactive and image-guided exploration of LPBF defects for analysis and decision making, (2) identification of major defect causes and suggestion of mitigation strategies, (3) defect-specific reasoning supported by hierarchical knowledge representation and fuzzy natural language query handling, and (4) interpretation of representative defect micrographs within a unified defect analysis workflow. Together, these functions are intended to support more accessible and structured defect exploration for LPBF users who may not possess highly specialized domain expertise. 

The primary contribution of this work is the integration of four capabilities into a single LPBF-specific prototype framework. First, the proposed multimodal large language model-based agent combines a structured hierarchical defect knowledge base with contextual information from the additive manufacturing literature and optional evidence retrieval from external sources. Second, the framework links defect knowledge to material and process-specific mitigation guidance through curated mappings derived from published studies. Third, it supports interactive defect investigation through fuzzy natural-language query handling, stepwise clarification of broad defect categories into specific defect types, and literature-supported explanations. Fourth, it incorporates a multimodal image-analysis component that enables hypothesis-guided interpretation of representative defect micrographs through a heuristic semantic alignment score intended to improve interpretability. In contrast to approaches that rely on large, dynamically retrieved knowledge graphs, the present framework uses an ontology-guided hierarchical knowledge base to constrain the reasoning workflow in a computationally lightweight multimodal setting. Within the current prototype scope, this design supports defect-specific and context-aware analysis while preserving transparency in how defect information and mitigation guidance are presented.

# 2. Methodology 

This section presents the methodological framework of the proposed knowledge-driven LLM-based decision support system to aid LPBF defect analysis and mitigation. The framework is designed to support structured and interpretable decision support by combining a hierarchical LPBF defect knowledge base, large language model-based reasoning and query handling, optional literature-supported contextual augmentation, and multimodal image analysis within a unified workflow. The proposed methodology uses curated defect knowledge to support defect exploration, causal explanation, and mitigation guidance, rather than relying solely on unconstrained generative reasoning. 

As illustrated in  Figure 1 , the proposed methodology consists of four interrelated stages: (1) construction of an ontology-guided hierarchical LPBF defect knowledge base, (2) query interpretation and defect-specific reasoning through fuzzy natural language handling and hierarchical defect clarification, (3) multimodal defect analysis with optional external evidence and knowledge augmentation, and (4) interactive deployment of the integrated workflow for prototype-level decision support. The four stages are briefly described below: 

1. Knowledge base construction . At the first stage, a structured LPBF defect knowledge base was constructed from published literature on LPBF defects and their causes. The resulting representation contains 27 defect types organized into four broad categories: global structural defects, local structural defects, surface defects, and material defects. For each defect, the knowledge base stores hierarchical category information, along with curated causal factors and mitigation guidance when supporting literature was available. This representation provides the structured backbone for subsequent defect exploration and reasoning. 

2. Query interpretation and reasoning. In the second phase, a reasoning layer was developed to interpret natural language user queries and map them to the appropriate defect information stored in the structured knowledge base. A "Smart Search" algorithm was developed using direct keyword matching and diffilib-based fuzzy matching to resolve misspelled or Page 5 of 28 

incomplete inputs, such as mapping “porsity” to "porosity". Additionally, smart filtering logic was implemented to handle broad queries. If a user searches for a general category like 'Cracking', the system identifies the ambiguity, and it displays a detailed list of defect types to ensure the final parameter recommendations are relevant to the defect. 

3. Multimodal analysis and contextual augmentation . In the third phase, two complementary modules were integrated to extend the diagnostic workflow beyond text-based querying. The first one is an external evidence retrieval module based on SerpApi that can access scholarly and web-based sources to provide supplementary contextual information. The second one is a multimodal image-analysis module based on a foundation model that supports hypothesis-guided interpretation of representative LPBF defect micrographs. In the proposed framework, external knowledge retrieval is treated as an auxiliary evidence layer for the curated internal knowledge base. Additionally, the image-analysis module is intended to support interpretable defect assessment through a heuristic semantic alignment score. 

4. Interactive deployment and decision support . In the final phase, the integrated workflow was deployed as an interactive prototype in Google Colab, supporting both menu-based navigation and free-text inquiry. Through this user-friendly interface, users ranging from researchers to shop-floor workers can explore defect categories, retrieve defect-specific causal and mitigation information, examine representative images and literature-supported context, and perform image-guided defect assessment within a single decision-support workflow. The present implementation should therefore be interpreted as a prototype framework for structured LPBF defect exploration, which can be extended to a validated industrial decision-support system.

2.1 System overview 

The proposed LBBF defect agent is an interactive decision-support system implemented in Python to support LPBF defect identification, classification, and mitigation. The framework integrates two complementary intelligence components. The first is a knowledge-driven layer that leverages a hierarchical defect knowledge base, rule-based knowledge traversal, fuzzy query processing, and curated literature-derived mappings to support defect exploration and provide mitigation guidance. The second is a multimodal reasoning layer that supports image-based interpretation of representative defect micrographs using a foundation model within a hypothesis-guided workflow. Page 6 of 28 

Within this integrated framework, the knowledge-driven layer serves as the primary source of structured defect information. It provides defect categorization, causal information, and mitigation guidance, all curated from the literature. The multimodal layer complements this functionality by supporting image-guided defect assessment when a user wishes to evaluate a representative defect micrograph. In addition, the framework can access scholarly and web-based sources through a retrieval module to provide supplementary contextual information and recent literature. It leverages the SerpApi–Google Search and Google Scholar interfaces, ensuring that recent studies are incorporated into the results [20]. 

To provide diagnostic accountability, the design imposes strict source verification on all outputs via a conditional routing protocol. The LLM is restricted to visual anomaly classification. On the other hand, internal fuzzy search algorithms map these visual classifications to formalized system nodes. For the next mitigation technique, the source is strictly defined by the material profile. For formally defined materials (e.g., IN625), the mitigation parameters are derived only from the deterministic Curated Knowledge Base (JSON application ontology). However, for unmapped or novel alloys, the system uses a Web Retrieval pipeline that summarizes generalized heuristics from the specific academic literature. All outputs are properly identified with their source origin (Ontology vs External Retrieval) for complete transparency to the end-user. 

While the dynamic literature retrieval ensures the system has access to the latest academic insights, dynamically retrieved sources may contain conflicting or out-of-context process recommendations. Hence, external retrieval is treated as an auxiliary evidence layer rather than as a replacement for the curated internal knowledge base. To preserve consistency, the framework implements an ontology-first conflict-resolution strategy: when externally retrieved information is inconsistent with the encoded internal knowledge base, the curated defect definitions and material-specific mitigation mappings stored in the internal representation take precedence. This design allows external evidence to enrich explanation and context without overriding the structured defect knowledge embedded in the current prototype agent. 

The prototype was deployed in Google Colab as an interactive command-line chatbot that supports both menu-based navigation and free-text user input. Through this interface, users can browse the defect hierarchy, retrieve defect-specific explanations, examine literature-supported context, and upload representative images for hypothesis-guided assessment.  Figure 2 presents the initial interface and system options of the LPBF defect agent. Although the primary objective of the developed agent is to help shop-floor workers, researchers, and quality specialists with defect analysis and parameter suggestions, the agent also includes an automated reporting module for recent output to improve visualization. 

For consistency across evaluations, the system’s inference engine was mathematically constrained to ensure complete reproducibility and eliminate the stochastic variability inherent to large language models. Specifically, the API generation configuration for the core model was strictly locked to a temperature of 0.0, a top-p of 0.1, and a top-k of 1. These hyperparameter constraints turn off probabilistic sampling. Thus, it forces the model to select only the absolute highest-probability tokens. Therefore, the agent behaves more like a highly deterministic classification engine than a creative text generator. This restriction guarantees that identical microscopic image inputs yield identical semantic alignment percentages and mitigation strategies across independent research trials.

2.2 Ontology-guided knowledge base design 

In this work, the term ontology refers to a structured, application-oriented representation of LPBF defect concepts and their relationships. More broadly, an ontology provides a formal semantic representation for organizing domain concepts and their interconnections in a structured, machine-interpretable form [21]. In additive manufacturing, such representations are useful for systematically organizing knowledge of defects, causal mechanisms, and interdependencies, where defect types and subtypes are defined as classes and linked by relationships that represent causal links and dependencies [22]. A structured representation is particularly important in LPBF because prior defect studies do not always maintain terminological consistency or hierarchical uniformity. Several earlier works have identified a wide range of defect modes, including porosity, cracking, surface roughness, and distortion, and have emphasized their relation to process parameters and part quality. At the same time, some studies have labeled mechanical outcomes, such as low strength or degraded mechanical properties, as defects, even though these outcomes are more appropriately interpreted as consequences of underlying physical defect mechanisms rather than as independent defect classes [23,24]. In other cases, broad defect categories and specific defect instances are presented at the same conceptual level, which makes cross-study comparison difficult. Terminological variation further contributes to ambiguity, as similar defect phenomena may be labeled as incomplete fusion holes [25], lack-of-fusion layer defect [23], and keyhole collapse [26]. These inconsistencies underscore the need for a more explicit, hierarchically organized representation of LPBF defect knowledge. 

This need for a systematic framework led to the adoption of the LPBF defect taxonomy proposed by Hasan et al. (2023), which categorizes LPBF defects by formation mechanism and physical scale into four levels: global structural, local structural, surface, and material defects [1]. This classification provides clear boundaries between hierarchical layers and offers one of the most systematic conceptual frameworks available for LPBF defect modeling. Accordingly, this taxonomy was adopted as the foundation of the knowledge base developed in this work. To support mitigation guidance within the knowledge base, process parameter recommendations for Inconel 625 reported by Paraschiv et al. (2022) were also incorporated, where relevant supporting evidence was available [27]. 

The choice of an application-oriented hierarchical representation is also consistent with broader discussions of the practical use of ontology in manufacturing. Formal semantic interoperability in manufacturing is often associated with logic-based ontologies such as OWL and RDF, as well as query mechanisms such as SPARQL. Although such approaches offer significant interoperability benefits, they may be more rigid than necessary for an interactive prototype focused on defect exploration and decision support. Ameri et al. (2022) demonstrated that manufacturing knowledge integration may benefit from a composable stack of ontologies with varying levels of formality and coverage, rather than a single, monolithic standard [28]. They discussed the Industrial Ontology Foundry (IOF) model, which is a hub-and-spoke type. The core consists of basic reference ontologies, and the spokes are very specific “application ontologies” that meet specific software and operational needs. Viewed in that context, the present framework is best interpreted as a domain-specific, application-oriented knowledge structure designed to support LPBF defect reasoning within the scope of the current prototype. 

Within this framework, the resulting representation consists of four top-level defect families as illustrated in  Figure 3 : (1) global structural defects, which are large-scale discontinuities that compromise structural integrity; (2) local structural defects, which include localized interruptions or imperfections in material continuity [29]; (3) surface defects, which manifest as imperfections on the final part surface or the active scan surface [30]; and (4) material defects, which arise from thermal stresses and affect microstructural integrity and mechanical performance [30]. The LPBF defect knowledge base was implemented as a hierarchical data structure using nested Python dictionaries. Each key represents a higher-level defect family, and each value contains either another dictionary or a list of specific defect instances. This hierarchical representation enables flexible, transparent modeling of multilevel defect relationships while remaining computationally lightweight and easily extensible. 

The current iteration of the knowledge base includes 27 critical LPBF defects. This set should be interpreted as a representative prototype rather than as an exhaustive catalog of all possible LPBF defect manifestations. Even so, the representation is intended to be extendable. A primary advantage of the deterministic JSON architecture used in this work is that it inherently supports continuous validation and updating. As new metallurgical studies emerge or additional material-specific information becomes available, new defect entries, causal links, and parameter bounds can be incorporated into the Page 8 of 28 

hierarchical structure without requiring substantial modification of the overall workflow. In this way, the knowledge base can be updated by domain experts while preserving the core structure of the present prototype.

Because LPBF defects are organized across multiple levels of abstraction, explicit encoding of hierarchy is important for both reasoning and representation. Broad categories such as global and local structural defects encompass more specific subcategories, such as cracking or porosity, which in turn include distinct defect types. Representing these levels explicitly allows the agent to support both high-level defect exploration and more fine-grained defect interpretation during analysis. To operationalize this hierarchical structure, a recursive traversal algorithm was implemented to navigate the knowledge base from Page 9 of 28 

general categories to specific defect instances. The Traverse_Defect_Categories procedure serves as the entry point, iterating through the primary knowledge base keys. For each category, it calls a recursive function, Traverse_Node , which evaluates the structural depth of the current element and determines whether further traversal or defect-level reasoning is required. The pseudocode presented in Algorithm 1 outlines this traversal logic and illustrates how hierarchical ontology navigation is integrated into the agent's reasoning workflow.

> 3print (Category)
> 4call Traverse_ Defect_ Node (defect_categories [Category], indent=1)

> 6end Procedure

> 10 print indentation (indent), defect
> 11 end for
> 12 else if node is a Dictionary:

In addition to traversal, context-aware defect interpretation requires the system to recover the full hierarchical path associated with a queried defect. To support this functionality, the framework employs a recursive path-finding algorithm outlined in Algorithm 2 that accumulates parent category keys as it traverses the hierarchy. As a result, the system reconstructs the complete category path associated with a defect (e.g., local structural defects → porosity ), thereby preserving the semantic context required for accurate defect classification and exploration. The traversal follows a depth-first search strategy and continues until the target defect is located or all branches are exhausted. When the algorithm encounters a leaf node represented as a list, it checks whether the target defect is present and returns the accumulated path if a match is found. If the current node is a dictionary representing a defect category, the algorithm recursively explores each subnode while updating the traversal path. The default return of NULL indicates that the target defect does not exist in the current branch. 

2.3 Knowledge base construction 

A structured knowledge base was created to support defect-specific reasoning, causal explanation, and mitigation-oriented guidance within the proposed LPBF defect agent. This knowledge base was implemented using a hierarchical JSON representation to store defect entries, causal factors, and mitigation information in a transparent, computationally lightweight format. Within the present prototype, the knowledge base serves as the primary internal source of structured defect knowledge. It supports process-level reasoning by linking defects to documented causes and literature-derived mitigation guidance. 

Each defect entry in the knowledge base is associated with three main types of information: its hierarchical position within the LPBF defect structure, its documented causal factors, and, where available, the corresponding mitigation guidance reported in the literature. For example, gas porosity is associated with causes such as entrapped gas and insufficient melting, together Page 10 of 28 

with parameter-level mitigation recommendations. By explicitly organizing these relations, the knowledge base enables the agent to retrieve defect-specific explanations and mitigation guidance in a structured, traceable way.

The knowledge base also supports limited causal reasoning by encoding sequential relationships among process conditions, intermediate physical mechanisms, and resulting defects. For example, an energy imbalance may contribute to melt-pool instability, which in turn can lead to balling and associated surface roughness. In the current prototype, such reasoning is not generated solely through unconstrained inference; instead, it is guided by curated relational mappings stored in the knowledge base. This design allows the large language model to generate natural language explanations that remain tied to structured defect knowledge rather than relying solely on open-ended text generation. 

As illustrated in  Figure 4 , the knowledge base organizes entities such as process parameters, intermediate mechanisms, and defects through explicitly defined hierarchical and causal relationships. The knowledge base, therefore, functions as a constrained, domain-specific knowledge structure for LPBF defect analysis, supporting defect exploration, explanation, and mitigation-oriented reasoning within the scope of the current agent prototype. Where the literature provided sufficiently specific parameter guidance, those mappings were incorporated into the knowledge base for defect-specific mitigation recommendations. In the present implementation, such guidance is most explicitly curated for Inconel 625. Accordingly, the knowledge base should be interpreted as an application-oriented representation designed for prototype-level LPBF reasoning, and the associated mitigation recommendations should be understood as context-bound guidance derived from the literature rather than as a standalone solution applicable to all materials, machines, and LPBF process conditions. 

2.4 Query interpretation and reasoning 

The proposed LPBF defect agent supports two complementary modes of interaction: menu-based navigation and free-text natural-language querying. To interpret free-text input, the system implements a Smart Search mechanism that combines direct keyword matching with fuzzy string comparison. This design allows the agent to respond to both well-formed technical queries and noisier user input containing typographical errors, incomplete terminology, or broad defect categories. 

The first stage of query interpretation uses substring matching to identify explicit defect terms embedded in longer user inputs. For example, a query such as “What is porosity?” is mapped directly to the corresponding defect category. If no exact match is found, the system uses fuzzy string matching via the difflib library as a fallback mechanism. Specifically, the Page 11 of 28 

get_close_matches() function compares the user input against a flattened representation of the defect hierarchy [31] . This approach enables robust handling of typographical errors, such as mapping "porsity" to "porosity". 

A further clarification step is applied when the interpreted query corresponds to a broad defect category rather than a specific defect instance. In such cases, the system prompts the user to refine the query by selecting from more specific child defects. For example, a query about porosity may be refined into gas porosity, keyhole porosity, lack of fusion porosity, or surface-connected porosity. This stepwise clarification process helps ensure that subsequent reasoning and mitigation guidance remain defect-specific and contextually appropriate.

2.6 Semantic alignment evaluation for image analysis 

The proposed LPBF defect agent includes a multimodal image-analysis module to support hypothesis-guided interpretation of representative defect micrographs. In the current implementation, visual feature extraction and multimodal reasoning are performed using the Gemini 2.5 Pro foundation model. The image-analysis workflow is guided by defect-specific descriptors and the structured LPBF knowledge base developed in this study, without any model fine-tuning on LPBF metallurgical data. 

Unlike a conventional discriminative classifier, the image-analysis module is not designed to output a calibrated class probability. Rather, it produces a semantic alignment score reflecting the degree of correspondence between observed image features and defect-specific descriptors provided in the prompting context. This score is heuristic and interpretive. It is intended to support transparent comparison among candidate defect hypotheses and should not be interpreted as statistical confidence or expected classification accuracy. Typical discriminative classifiers use a softmax layer to estimate class probabilities [32]. By contrast, the present approach emphasizes interpretability by assessing whether observed visual characteristics are consistent with the expected morphology of a candidate defect. The image-analysis workflow proceeds through three stages, described below. 

2.6.1 Visual feature encoding 

First, the input image is processed by the multimodal model and embedded in a high-dimensional latent vector that captures defect-related attributes such as void sphericity, edge sharpness, and the spatial distribution of pores. This latent encoding Page 13 of 28 

serves as the visual basis for the subsequent evaluation of defect-specific hypotheses. The present workflow relies on in-context guidance from structured defect descriptors rather than LPBF-specific fine-tuning of the underlying multimodal model. 

2.6.2 Hypothesis-guided prompting 

To guide defect evaluation, the agent incorporates hypothesis-conditioned prompting, in which the system explicitly queries whether the image corresponds to a suspected defect (e.g., "Are you suspecting 'X' defect?"). This conditioning mechanism directs the model's internal attention to the visual features most relevant to the underlying defect-formation mechanism, enabling targeted evaluation rather than automatic label assignment. 

2.6.3 Semantic alignment scoring 

In the third stage, the model’s response is interpreted as a semantic alignment score reflecting how closely the observed visual features match the defect descriptors. When the image exhibits a feature that strongly aligns with a candidate defect description, the corresponding semantic alignment score is higher. Conversely, when the observed morphology is inconsistent with the supplied defect characteristics, the score is lower. For example, strong alignment with keyhole porosity may be associated with deep, elongated, V-shaped cavities. In contrast, irregular voids with sharp boundaries and signs of incomplete melting may align more strongly with a lack of fusion. This interpretive workflow is intended to mirror the kind of feature-based comparison used in expert visual assessment, while keeping the output tied to structured defect descriptors.

2.7 Integrated workflow of the LPBF defect agent 

Figure 7  summarizes the operational logic of the proposed LPBF defect agent. Through the interface shown in  Figure 2 , the proposed agent integrates two complementary workflows within a unified framework: a text-based reasoning workflow for defect exploration and knowledge retrieval (options 1-4), and (2) an image-based assessment workflow for multimodal defect interpretation (option 6). At initialization, the system loads the hierarchical defect knowledge base together with the API configurations required to support interactive user input. 

After initialization, the workflow branches based on the user’s selected interface option. Options 1–4 invoke the text-based reasoning workflow, in which the Smart Search module processes natural-language input through substring matching and 

difflib -based fuzzy string matching. If the identified term corresponds to a broad defect category (such as cracking or porosity), the system applies recursive disambiguation and prompts the user to refine the selection to a more specific defect subtype. Once a specific defect is selected, the agent retrieves the corresponding causal information and mitigation guidance from the internal knowledge base. When requested, external scholarly retrieval through SerpApi and Google Scholar is used to provide supplementary contextual information and recent literature relevant to the queried defect. In the current prototype, this retrieval layer is used to augment explanation and context; it does not replace the internal knowledge base or override curated defect-specific mitigation mappings. 

Option 6 activates the image-based assessment workflow. When the user uploads a representative defect micrograph, the workflow initiates a hypothesis-testing checkpoint and prompts the user to indicate whether a specific defect is suspected. This input governs the prompt construction strategy, determining whether the model performs general defect identification or targeted hypothesis evaluation. The inference layer uses a dynamic model selection mechanism to identify an appropriate multimodal foundation model variant, cycling between Flash and Pro versions to ensure reliability and robust visual interpretation across defect scenarios. 

The output layer subsequently integrates results from both the text-based reasoning engine and the vision-based analysis module, presenting users with semantic alignment scores, supporting visual evidence, and process-level mitigation guidance. By integrating deterministic ontological reasoning with generative multimodal analysis, the workflow enables explainable, context-aware defect diagnosis and mitigation support, allowing users to seamlessly transition between knowledge exploration, image-based validation, and decision-support outputs. 

To mitigate the inherent non-determinism and quality variation of dynamic web retrieval, the SerpApi module operates under a strict 'Ontology-First' hierarchy. The deterministic JSON knowledge schema serves as the primary ground truth for all IN625 defect mitigations. The dynamic search module is used exclusively for secondary context augmentation (e.g., retrieving recent literature to support the baseline recommendation). To ensure source quality, SerpApi queries are programmatically constrained to peer-reviewed databases. Furthermore, to address conflicting information, the LLM is instructed, through its system prompt, to discard any retrieved web parameters that violate the JSON schema's established boundaries. Finally, to support reproducibility despite the dynamic nature of web search, the agent maintains a reference audit trail, explicitly logging the titles and sources of any retrieved literature used in its final output. 

At present, material-specific mitigation recommendations are limited to IN625, as the knowledge base contains curated recommendations for only that material. For other materials, the system relies on the LLM for broader reasoning support. Material selection is therefore included as an input step when the defect-analysis option is triggered. Additional material-specific information can be incorporated into the ontology knowledge base in future work.

3.3 Handling of fuzzy and ambiguous user queries

This approach enables the agent to handle noisy input and improves accessibility for users unfamiliar with exact technical terminology. Furthermore, common parent categories such as "porosity" or "cracking" are disambiguated by follow-up prompts that allow the user to select subtypes, such as "keyhole porosity", "gas porosity". 4.2 Ablation study of system components 

An ablation study was conducted to evaluate the contributions of the main components of the proposed LPBF defect agent, including the base vision-language model, the dynamic retrieval (RAG) module, and the ontology-guided knowledge base. The evaluation used a strict top-1 macro-averaged evaluation metric to assess the accuracy and usefulness of the proposed framework across defect classes. In multi-class defect classification, global aggregation may conceal weak performance in individual defect classes. Therefore, precision, recall, and F1 score were calculated at the class level and then macro-averaged to provide a class-balanced summary of performance across the evaluated defect morphologies [37]. 

To assess the diagnostic performance of the proposed knowledge-driven LPBF defect agent, a dataset of high-resolution LPBF microscopy images was compiled from several peer-reviewed studies [27,38–46]. The dataset included representative images of four commonly reported LPBF defect classes: lack of fusion porosity, gas porosity, keyhole porosity, and balling. The final evaluation dataset contained 180 microscopy images from 39 sample parts. Because the images were drawn from multiple published studies, the literature-derived dataset captures defect morphologies reported under varying LPBF process conditions. The labels reported in the source publications were used as reference labels for evaluation, providing a reproducible basis for comparing model outputs across ablation configurations. Using the compiled dataset, four system configurations were evaluated to isolate the contribution of the main components of the proposed agent. Configuration A used the base vision-language model without dynamic retrieval or ontology-guided constraints. Configuration B added dynamic retrieval without the ontology-guided knowledge base. Configuration C used the ontology-guided knowledge base without dynamic retrieval. Configuration D represented the proposed integrated system, combining the ontology-guided knowledge base with targeted retrieval and multimodal reasoning. # 5. Conclusion 

Reliable deployment of metal AM processes such as laser powder bed fusion is constrained by the prevalence of process-induced defects and the knowledge-intensive nature of defect diagnosis and mitigation. Addressing these challenges requires decision-support mechanisms that extend beyond simple information aggregation. Such mechanisms must systematically Page 25 of 28 

organize domain knowledge, enable transparent and explainable reasoning, and provide context-specific guidance to support informed engineering decision making. In response, this paper presents an ontology-guided large language model-based defect agent for LPBF defect exploration, interpretation, and mitigation guidance. The proposed framework integrates a hierarchical knowledge base containing 27 LPBF defect types with natural language interaction, fuzzy query handling, supplementary literature retrieval, and multimodal image assessment within a unified workflow. In doing so, it enables users to move beyond static defect lookup to more interactive, explainable defect exploration. 

The key contribution of this work lies in connecting structured LPBF defect knowledge with LLM-based interaction and image-assisted reasoning. The developed agent enables users to navigate hierarchical defect categories, retrieve causal information, access mitigation guidance when supporting literature is available, and interpret representative defect micrographs through descriptor-based semantic alignment. Unlike conventional defect classification approaches, the proposed framework emphasizes interpretability by connecting candidate defect assessments to observable image features and structured defect descriptions. The system also supports fuzzy and ambiguous query handling, broadening accessibility for users who may not use exact technical terminology, and incorporates literature-supported retrieval to provide supplementary scholarly context when requested. 

The empirical evaluation provided initial evidence of the value of structured knowledge integration. The qualitative comparison with general-purpose vision-language models showed that, for a representative LPBF defect micrograph, the proposed agent produced more structured, material-specific outputs by linking defect interpretation to semantic alignment scores and mitigation guidance. The ablation study further showed that less-constrained system configurations, particularly the retrieval-augmented model without ontology-guided knowledge constraints, were more prone to inconsistent defect assessment in the literature-derived evaluation dataset. By contrast, performance significantly improved when the generative model was supported by deterministic ontology-guided, material-specific knowledge representation. The proposed integrated system achieved the highest performance among the evaluated configurations, with 80.0% diagnostic accuracy and an 80.8% macro F1 score. These findings suggest that ontology-guided knowledge representation can improve the consistency of LLM-assisted LPBF defect assessment, particularly when combined with targeted knowledge retrieval and vision-based defect analysis using foundation models. 

This work also defines several important directions for continued development. The current implementation focuses primarily on IN625 because the material-specific mitigation mappings used in the prototype were curated for this alloy. However, the framework can be extended to other LPBF material systems by incorporating material-specific defect mechanisms, process windows, and mitigation guidance into the knowledge base. Similarly, although the present empirical validation is specific to LPBF, the overall framework is modular and could be adapted to other manufacturing processes by replacing the LPBF-specific knowledge base with domain-specific knowledge structures and formally evaluating the resulting system in those settings. Future work will therefore focus on expanding the defect taxonomy, incorporating additional material systems, strengthening causal relationship modeling, and validating the framework using larger, literature-derived and expert-labeled datasets, as well as user studies involving engineers and manufacturing practitioners. Another important direction is integrating in situ monitoring data to extend the framework toward real-time defect interpretation and more predictive decision support. 

The proposed LPBF defect agent establishes a systematic and extensible foundation for LPBF defect exploration, interpretable defect assessment, and decision support for defect mitigation in metal additive manufacturing. By integrating curated defect knowledge, an LLM-based interactive platform, supplementary knowledge retrieval, and defect analysis within a unified framework, the proposed LPBF defect agent contributes to the development of more explainable, human-centered decision-support tools and provides a practical step toward more informed defect management in advanced manufacturing environments.
