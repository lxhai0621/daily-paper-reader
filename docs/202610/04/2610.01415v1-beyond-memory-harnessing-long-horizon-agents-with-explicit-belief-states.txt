Beyond Memory: Harnessing Long-Horizon Agents with
Explicit Belief States
Yu Luo1, Jiamin Jiang1, Yimin Zuo1, Xidao Wen2, Rongchen Gao1, Yongqian Sun*1, Shenglin Zhang1,
Guiyang Liu2, Cheng Zhang2, Fang Situ2, Qi Zhou2, Dan Pei3
1Nankai University, 2Alibaba Group, 3Tsinghua University
∗Corresponding Author
Large language model (LLM) agents can now undertake increasingly complex tasks, but the way they organize
interaction history into memory does not ensure a coherent understanding of the current world. We introduce
PoS, an inference-time framework that constructs and continually maintains explicit belief states as the agent’s
decision context. Each belief combines an estimate of the current world state with unresolved task requirements,
making explicit what the agent still needs to learn and accomplish. To keep this belief reliable and actionable,
PoS validates its consistency and monitors task progress to detect Belief Trapping, where the agent continues
to act without making meaningful progress toward the goal. Recovery is then tailored to both the trapping
pattern and the type of unresolved task requirement. Experiments on four benchmarks spanning execution and
diagnosis show that PoS achieves the highest overall performance on every benchmark with all three LLM
backbones. Ablations demonstrate the importance of consistency validation and recovery, while context-
scaling experiments show resilience to context growth. Together, these results support belief construction and
continual maintenance as a foundation for long-horizon context management beyond history retention and
compression.

Introduction
LLM agents are increasingly equipped with ultra-long context windows (Yang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026a; Hu et al.,
2026), stronger reasoning and tool-use capabilities (Guo et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026), external memory systems (Hu
et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026b), and sophisticated execution harnesses (Ma et al.,
2026; Wan et al., 2026), substantially expanding the complexity and horizon of tasks they can undertake. However,
longer interaction horizons expose a deeper challenge, as actions may alter the environment and new observations may
invalidate earlier inferences. Accumulated context thus mixes evidence about past states with intermediate judgments,
making it increasingly difficult to identify which facts still hold, which inferences remain supported, and what remains
unresolved. Raw trajectories preserve this evidence chronologically, while memory mechanisms select, compress, or
reorganize it. While both are valuable, access to evidence alone does not ensure an explicit and coherent estimate of
the current world that can be revised and checked across steps. Recent work observes that when task state remains
implicit within a growing context, outdated or inconsistent information can persist across subsequent decisions (Ma
et al., 2026). The next barrier to reliable long-horizon LLM agents is therefore not simply retaining more history, but
maintaining an actionable representation of the current world.
A formal basis for this representation is the belief state 𝑏𝑡(𝑠) = 𝑃(𝑠𝑡= 𝑠| ℎ𝑡), the posterior over the current latent
world state 𝑠𝑡given interaction history ℎ𝑡. In a standard partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP),
this belief is a sufficient statistic for decision-making and can be recursively updated after each action and observa-
tion (Kaelbling et al., 1998). Recent methods have begun to explicitly represent the belief state through goal-relative
reflection, probabilistic hypotheses, structured graphs, or audited task records (Kim et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026; Luo
et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026). Such representations make the agent’s current understanding of the world available for
inspection and revision. However, explicit belief construction is just the start, as an LLM-generated belief must satisfy
two further requirements.
First, belief updates must remain internally consistent and supported by interaction evidence. Since these updates
are generated by the LLM itself, they may introduce conflicting states or unsupported inferences that undermine
subsequent decisions and ultimately lead to task failure. For instance, recording a microwave as both open and closed
makes it unclear whether the robot should open it before placing a mug inside. Continual validation is thus essential to
sustain the coherence and evidential support of the evolving belief state. Second, consistency alone does not prevent
the failure mode we term Belief Trapping, where the agent continues to act without making meaningful progress toward
the goal. For example, the agent may repeat ineffective actions, cycle through previously visited states, or gather
information that does not help complete the task. Unproductive interactions will exhaust the available budget before
achieving the goal, making timely detection and recovery necessary to restore meaningful task progress. T3 detects
belief deviation and truncates uninformative trajectories for reinforcement learning, but does not provide a recovery
strategy within the ongoing episode (Zou et al., 2026). To our knowledge, no existing framework maintains a belief
state with both continual consistency validation and online diagnosis and recovery from Belief Trapping.
We introduce Progression of States (PoS), a unified framework that integrates explicit belief modeling, consistency
validation, and trapping-aware recovery for long-horizon agent decision-making.
As illustrated in Figure 2, PoS
continually updates a world state of task-relevant Entities, States, and Relations, along with epistemic and achievement
gaps that specify what the agent still needs to learn or accomplish. The agent selects actions from the current belief,
while a Belief Sentinel validates updates for internal consistency and support from interaction evidence. PoS also
assesses whether belief evolution supports continued task progress using three measures: gap persistence, progress
stagnation, and belief recurrence. It then performs factorized trapping diagnosis along two dimensions, agent dynamics
and blocked gap type, and composes corresponding recovery constraints to recover from Belief Trapping.
We evaluate PoS using three LLM backbones on ALFWorld and LOCA-Bench for execution tasks and RCA-100
and ClinDiag for diagnosis tasks. PoS achieves the highest overall performance on all four benchmarks with each
backbone, with relative gains over the strongest baseline reaching 22.68% on ALFWorld and 37.89% on RCA-100
joint accuracy. Ablation studies demonstrate the importance of consistency validation and trapping recovery beyond
explicit belief construction. Further analyses examine trapping patterns and recovery effectiveness, robustness to
context growth, and the tradeoff between token consumption and task performance.

Related Work
We organize prior approaches to long-horizon context management according to the decision context they expose to
the agent: memory as context and belief as context.
2.1
Memory as Context
ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) provides a foundational framework for LLM agents, conditioning each action on the growing
raw interaction trajectory. Compression-based methods instead summarize accumulated context into compact forms:
ReadAgent (Lee et al., 2024) constructs retrievable gist memories, ACON (Kang et al., 2025) optimizes guidelines
for history compression, and SUPO (Lu et al., 2026) jointly learns periodic summarization and tool-use policies.
Other methods impose structure on the retained context: HiAgent (Hu et al., 2025) organizes working memory around
subgoals, COMPASS (Wan et al., 2026) maintains concise progress briefs across reasoning stages, and PACE (Wei
et al., 2026) adjusts historical granularity according to its predicted relevance to the next action. Other approaches
make context management itself an agent-controlled operation: MemGPT manages information across in-context and
external memory through an operating-system-inspired hierarchy (Packer et al., 2023). CaT (Liu et al., 2026) exposes
context compression as a callable tool over a structured workspace, AgentFold (Ye et al., 2026) performs proactive
multi-scale folding of historical trajectories, and Sculptor (Li et al., 2026) provides explicit tools for actively curating
the working context. Although these methods improve evidence accessibility and context efficiency, the resulting
context remains a transformed record of historical evidence rather than a consistent estimate of the current world.
2.2
Belief as Context
A parallel line of work studies how LLMs represent the current state of an evolving world (Gurnee and Tegmark,
2024). Mechanistic studies probe the world-state abstractions encoded by LLMs (Li et al., 2024) and show that state
tracking of reasoning is itself a learned computation whose underlying mechanism affects systematic generalization (Li
et al., 2025). Agent frameworks further externalize parts of this computation (Tang et al., 2024). QuBE (Kim et al.,
2024) constructs a task-relevant belief through question answering; StateAct (Rozanov and Rei, 2025) maintains a
chain of states throughout task execution; and ReflAct (Kim et al., 2025) grounds action selection in reflection over
the current state relative to the goal. More structured approaches maintain particle beliefs over latent states and
goals through hypothesis trees and Bayesian filtering (Tang et al., 2026), recursively update natural-language belief
summaries (Lidayan et al., 2025), or combine causal graphs with state machines as explicit belief (Luo et al., 2026).
LongHorizon-Harness (Ma et al., 2026) externalizes task state as audited records of requirements, artifacts, and facts,
preserving verified progress through a Manage-Execute-Audit loop. However, it relies on independently verifiable
intermediate results and is therefore less suitable when meaningful progress consists of uncertain inferences rather
than observable outcomes. Beyond belief construction, recent work has also begun to address failure modes related
to Belief Trapping. T3 (Zou et al., 2026) detects excessive belief deviation and truncates uninformative trajectories
during reinforcement learning, while CGDP (Kausik et al., 2026) uses an exhaustion gate to terminate unproductive
context gathering. These approaches address stalled progress through truncation or termination, without attempting to
restore progress during inference. In contrast, PoS maintains and validates a task-conditioned belief and uses factorized
recovery to redirect subsequent actions.

Methodology
PoS comprises three components: Belief Modeling constructs a task-conditioned belief (Section 3.1), Belief-Guided
Interaction selects actions and validates belief updates (Section 3.2), and Trapping-Aware Recovery detects and recovers
from Belief Trapping (Section 3.3).

benchmarks, where the agent interacts with the environment to acquire discriminative evidence, update competing
hypotheses, and ultimately identify the most plausible diagnosis.
Baselines.
We adopt ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) as the common harness for context-management baselines, using
its simple reasoning–action loop to keep the interaction protocol consistent while varying the context supplied for
action selection. Raw Trajectory directly provides the complete, chronologically ordered action–observation history.
ACON (Kang et al., 2025) and PACE (Wei et al., 2026) manage the growing context through compression and
adaptive retention, respectively. HiAgent (Hu et al., 2025) organizes completed interactions around subgoals and
maintains a hierarchical working memory. LongHorizon-Harness (Ma et al., 2026) maintains an audited task state and
coordinates long-horizon execution through a Manage–Execute–Audit architecture. Together, these baselines cover
raw history, compressed and adaptively retained context, hierarchical working memory, and explicitly maintained task
state. LongHorizon-Harness retains its native control architecture, while the remaining baselines operate through the
common ReAct harness.
Implementation details.
We evaluate all methods with three backbone models: Qwen3.7-Plus, Kimi-K3, and
GLM-5.3. Within each backbone setting, all LLM components of PoS and all LLM-based components of the baselines
use the same evaluated backbone. All LLM calls use deterministic decoding with temperature 0. Complete benchmark
protocols and budgets, baseline implementations, and method-specific hyperparameters are provided in the appendix D.
4.1
Main Results
(1) Existing context management does not consistently improve upon raw trajectories.
Table 1 reveals substantial
variation in baseline performance across tasks and backbones. PACE remains competitive on ALFWorld and RCA-
100, but falls 24.57–28.19 points below Raw Trajectory on LOCA-Bench. LongHorizon-Harness performs well on
LOCA-Bench, where explicit requirements and generated artifacts support intermediate verification, but delivers less
consistent gains on RCA-100 and ClinDiag, as progress depends on refining uncertain diagnostic hypotheses rather
than producing directly verifiable artifacts. On ClinDiag with GLM-5.3, none of the context-management baselines
outperforms Raw Trajectory. These reversals suggest that additional compression and organization do not consistently
benefit modern LLMs with strong long-context capabilities, as information loss may offset the benefits of reducing
redundancy.
(2) PoS consistently outperforms existing methods across diverse tasks and backbones.
In contrast, PoS achieves
the highest overall performance on all four benchmarks under all three backbones. Compared with the strongest
baseline with the same backbone, PoS achieves relative gains of up to 22.68% on ALFWorld, 7.53% on LOCA-Bench,
7


37.89% on RCA-100 joint accuracy, and 11.31% on ClinDiag. The gains span both execution and diagnosis tasks,
indicating that they are not tied to a particular task structure. These results highlight the importance of maintaining
an accurate, task-conditioned belief about the current world state and unresolved requirements, beyond retaining and
organizing interaction history.
(3) Effective belief modeling requires maintaining both consistency and health.
Removing Consistency Validation
lowers performance across all four benchmarks to varying degrees, with losses reaching 14.93 points on ALFWorld
and 11.81 points on LOCA-Bench, compared with 0.33–0.66 points on ClinDiag. This pattern is consistent with the
greater risk of inconsistent belief updates when complex interactions require the agent to reconcile changing states
and observations across steps. Removing Trapping Diagnosis and its associated recovery also degrades performance
across all four benchmarks, including losses of 4.85–6.79 points on RCA-100 and 2.65–3.97 points on ClinDiag.
Recovery complements validation by redirecting actions toward unresolved requirements when continued interaction
no longer advances the task. Together, these results demonstrate the value of maintaining both belief consistency and
task progress, beyond constructing an explicit representation.

Figure 4 compares task success on LOCA-Bench as environment descriptions grow from 8K to 256K across three
backbones. Although performance generally declines with context length, ACON shows a more gradual decline than

potential information loss. Meanwhile, PoS maintains broadly stable performance from 96K to 256K, exceeding the
strongest baseline by 10.67–16.00 points at 256K. By integrating new observations into an existing world state and
unresolved requirements, PoS grounds each decision in the current task conditions rather than requiring the agent
to reconstruct them from an increasingly long interaction history. This incremental maintenance reduces repeated
interpretation of dispersed or outdated observations, while consistency validation and progress monitoring help prevent
unsupported updates and stalled interactions from persisting. Together, these results suggest that effective context
management should balance reducing historical redundancy with maintaining a reliable and actionable understanding
of the current world.

5
Conclusion
In this work, we showed that explicit belief construction alone is insufficient for reliable long-horizon decision-making.
We proposed PoS, a framework that constructs task-conditioned belief states and continually maintains them through
consistency validation and factorized recovery from Belief Trapping. Experiments across four benchmarks and three
LLM backbones demonstrate consistent improvements in execution and diagnosis, together with resilience to context
growth. Although PoS uses more tokens, the resulting gains demonstrate the potential of investing inference-time
computation in belief maintenance. Reducing this overhead while preserving the gains in agent performance is an
important direction for future work.

B.2
Belief Trapping Detection and Recovery
Step-level progress assessment.
For diagnostic tasks, 𝑐𝑡(𝑥) ∈[0, 1] denotes the confidence assigned to a goal-
relevant inferred State or Relation. These values are maintained independently without normalization across records.
The progress measure in Eq. 4 aggregates absolute confidence changes between consecutive validated beliefs, so both
increases and decreases contribute to the progress label when the aggregate change exceeds 𝜖. For execution tasks, the
Belief Sentinel evaluates progress relative to the active gap using the criteria in Section 3.3.
Window-based health estimation.
Trapping detection begins once 𝐾validated transitions are available. At each
step 𝑡, historical world states within W𝑡are projected onto the same current active gap Δ𝑡, selecting relevant records
through their existing relations. The comparison underlying 𝑑𝑊includes confidence changes even when descriptive
content remains unchanged. When the active gap changes, the existing window is retained and its historical world states
are projected onto the new gap. The resulting persistence, stagnation, and recurrence signals are combined according
to Eq. 5, with trapping detected when H𝑡≤𝜃𝐻.
Factorized trapping diagnosis.
Once trapping is detected, the Static, Cycle, and Drift conditions are evaluated
in order. If none is satisfied, PoS uses a generic recovery prompt rather than assigning a trapping pattern. The blocked
17


gap type is selected by comparing persistence across nonempty gap sets. When persistence is equal, diagnostic tasks
prioritize epistemic gaps, whereas execution tasks prioritize achievement gaps.
Recovery constraint composition.
The diagnosed pattern and selected gap type are incorporated into a recovery
prompt together with the relevant Entities and the active gap. The resulting constraints address both the unproductive
behavior and the unresolved requirement, and guide subsequent action selection while the active gap remains unchanged.
After each validated transition, PoS recomputes belief health and releases the constraints when H𝑡> 𝜃𝐻. Otherwise,
it updates the diagnosis and corresponding recovery constraints.
B.3
Design Rationale for Belief Trapping
Choice of trapping signals.
The three signals capture complementary aspects of unproductive interaction. Gap
Persistence ties the assessment to requirements that remain unresolved, while Progress Stagnation measures the absence
of recorded task progress. Belief Recurrence complements these signals by identifying repeated belief configurations
that may occur despite positive step-level progress labels. Behavioral statistics such as action repetition or interaction
count do not directly establish whether the task is advancing. Repeated tool calls may acquire relevant information,
whereas different actions may leave the same requirement unresolved. Likewise, confidence levels alone do not indicate
progress, as high confidence can persist during stalled investigation and low confidence can accompany useful evidence
gathering. The selected signals instead connect unresolved requirements with changes and recurring patterns in the
maintained belief.
Aggregation of trapping signals.
We design the health score to capture stagnation or recurrence associated with
persistent unresolved requirements, without treating gap persistence alone as sufficient evidence of trapping. Let

C.3
Interactive Evaluation Protocols
All methods interact with each benchmark through the same benchmark-specific interface. The context-management
baselines operate within the shared ReAct harness, while LongHorizon-Harness retains its native Manage–Execute–
Audit control structure.
• ALFWorld. The agent receives a natural-language goal and an initial textual observation. At each step, it
executes one command from the environment’s admissible action set and receives the resulting observation.
A case succeeds when the environment confirms that the goal conditions have been satisfied; otherwise, it
terminates when the action budget is exhausted.
• LOCA-Bench. The agent receives a task instruction and a case-specific set of MCP tools for inspecting and
modifying the task environment. Each tool call constitutes one environment action, and its result becomes the
next observation. The agent invokes the “claim_done” tool after completing the task, after which the official
evaluator determines success; an uncompleted case terminates upon budget exhaustion.
• RCA-100. The agent initially receives the incident metadata, while metrics, logs, traces, events, alerts, and
system topology must be acquired through diagnostic tools. Each tool call constitutes one environment action.
The agent terminates the case by calling “submit_root_cause” with a fault type and a root-cause entity. We
evaluate the two submitted fields separately and report joint accuracy when both are correct; cases without a
valid submission before the action budget are treated as failures.
• ClinDiag. The agent initially observes only the patient’s initial presentation. It acquires additional evidence by
asking one specific question per action about medical history, physical examination, laboratory tests, radiographic
tests, or other diagnostic procedures. The case terminates when the agent calls “submit_diagnosis”, and the
submitted diagnosis is evaluated against the reference diagnosis by the benchmark judge. Cases without a valid
submission before the action budget are treated as failures.

D
Implementation and Reproducibility Details
D.1
Backbone and Agent Configuration
We evaluate all methods with three backbone models: Qwen3.7-Plus, Kimi-K3, and GLM-5.3. The model is accessed
through the DashScope OpenAI-compatible API (https://dashscope.aliyuncs.com/compatible-mode/v1);
no fixed API snapshot is available. For each backbone setting, all LLM-based components of PoS and baselines use the
same backbone as the corresponding baseline agents. We do not use a stronger auxiliary model for belief construction,
validation, or trapping-aware recovery. The Task Agent selects its action from the current task-conditioned belief state,
which serves as its decision context.
The only model fixed independently of the evaluated backbone occurs in the medical diagnosis benchmark ClinDiag.
Its provider and judge are treated as part of the benchmark environment rather than as components of the evaluated
21


agent. To keep this environment identical across backbone settings, both are implemented with Qwen3.7-plus for
all methods and backbones. All agent-side model calls use deterministic decoding with temperature 0. We set the
maximum generation length to 512 tokens for ALFWorld, where each interaction primarily requires selecting an
environment action, and to 4096 tokens for LOCA-Bench, RCA-100, and ClinDiag.
D.2
Baseline Selection
We select baselines to cover representative forms of decision context while remaining applicable under a common
cross-domain evaluation setting. Raw Trajectory serves as the uncompressed reference and exposes the complete
interaction history directly to the Agent. Among memory-based approaches, ACON (Kang et al., 2025), PACE (Wei
et al., 2026), and HiAgent (Hu et al., 2025) represent complementary strategies based on history compression, adaptive
context granularity, and hierarchical subgoal-oriented working memory, respectively. We further include LongHorizon-
Harness (Ma et al., 2026) as a closely related state-oriented baseline because it explicitly maintains an audited task state
across long-horizon execution. Together, these methods span raw history, compressed and selectively retained context,
hierarchical memory, and explicit task-state maintenance, while remaining adaptable to the same four evaluation
environments and backbone settings.
We do not attempt to exhaustively benchmark every method discussed in Section 2, as several approaches differ
substantially in their training assumptions, control architecture, or task-specific state representation. For example,
SUPO (Lu et al., 2026) jointly optimizes summarization and tool-use policies through reinforcement learning, while
CaT (Liu et al., 2026) and AgentFold (Ye et al., 2026) rely on learned policies for actively manipulating the working
context. Several belief-oriented methods are likewise specialized to particular reasoning structures or task families.
In particular, Graph of States (Luo et al., 2026) is designed for abductive reasoning and couples belief representation
with a causal graph and a state machine that explicitly constrain hypothesis evolution, making its representation closely
tied to diagnosis-style tasks rather than a common decision context that can be applied unchanged across all four
benchmarks. Similarly, Tru-POMDP (Tang et al., 2026) assumes structured hypotheses and belief-space planning
for uncertain task planning. We therefore prioritize baselines that provide representative and practically comparable
alternatives to PoS under a unified cross-domain evaluation protocol.
D.3
Baseline Implementation
Shared evaluation protocol.
We evaluate all baselines through the same benchmark adapters, tool interfaces,
task instructions, termination protocol, and environment-action budgets described in Appendix C. All LLM-based
components follow the backbone configuration described in Appendix D.1. Because different policies naturally induce
different interaction trajectories, the methods do not receive identical observations; instead, they share the same
evidence-acquisition interface and differ in how the evidence produced by their own interactions is represented and
exposed for subsequent decision making. Raw Trajectory, ACON, PACE, and HiAgent are integrated into a common

Material implementation differences.
The original baselines were developed for different environments and agent
protocols, so their end-to-end runners cannot be directly applied to all four benchmarks. We preserve the defining
context-management mechanism of each method while adapting its interaction layer to the common benchmark inter-
faces. All methods receive the same task-independent background information, tool documentation, and termination
instructions. Method-specific prompts contain only the instructions required to construct or operate the corresponding
context representation. When an original response format is incompatible with the benchmark tool interface, we
provide format demonstrations containing only interface-level examples and no evaluated cases or target answers.

splits for learning such benchmark-specific compression policies, while optimizing them on evaluation cases would
conflate inference-time context management with task-specific training.

PACE.
PACE retains the interaction history in an external chunk store and exposes each chunk at an adaptive granu-
larity determined by its predicted relevance to the next decision (Wei et al., 2026). We preserve its four representation
levels, relevance-based granularity selection, chronological ordering, and glimpse mechanism. Because the repository
linked by the paper was inaccessible during our implementation, we reproduce the method from its published equations,
default settings, and Algorithm 1. The original BGE-M3 encoder is replaced with text-embedding-v4 for relevance
scoring, while the evaluated backbone generates the textual detailed and brief representations.
HiAgent.
HiAgent organizes working memory around Agent-generated subgoals (Hu et al., 2025). The trajectory
associated with the active subgoal is retained in full, whereas completed subgoals are represented by summaries and
their original trajectories remain available for retrieval. Since the official implementation is built on AgentBoard, we
port the subgoal-based working-memory mechanism into the shared benchmark interface and convert its interaction
protocol to the common structured action format. Summary-length and folding parameters are adapted at the benchmark
level and then fixed across all cases and backbone models.
LongHorizon-Harness.
LongHorizon-Harness differs from the other baselines in that it defines a complete Manage–
Execute–Audit control architecture rather than only a context representation (Ma et al., 2026). At each round, the
Manager constructs a bounded subtask from the audited task state, the Executor interacts with the environment to
complete it, and the Auditor verifies the resulting state before verified requirements, artifacts, and facts are propagated
to the next round. We base our implementation on the official LongHorizon-Harness codebase and preserve its native
Manager–Executor–Auditor workflow and audited task-state representation. We only adapt the benchmark-facing tool
interfaces and action formats so that the method can operate through the same evaluation interfaces as the other
23


baselines. Any Auditor operation that queries the environment is counted against the same environment-action budget
as an Executor action; purely internal Manager, Executor, or Auditor LLM calls are treated as computational overhead.

Fine-Grained Main Results
Table 9 expands the aggregate results in Table 1 into task categories and diagnostic dimensions. This breakdown
reveals which task requirements benefit from explicit belief maintenance, how validation and recovery contribute to
different aspects of performance, and where challenges remain.
(1) Explicit belief particularly benefits state tracking and diagnostic disambiguation.
A key benefit of explicit
belief is that observations from different steps jointly inform an updated understanding of the current world. By
maintaining this understanding together with unresolved gaps, PoS connects what the agent has already established to
what its next action must accomplish. This is particularly useful when successful execution depends on preserving state
changes across actions, or when diagnosis requires combining several signals to distinguish competing hypotheses. The
concentration of ALFWorld gains in Transform supports this interpretation, with Qwen3.7-Plus success increasing
from 56.00% to 81.33%, while Place matches the strongest baseline at 97.56%. Our RCA-100 trace analysis further
shows how belief integrates “request patterns” and “resource-utilization” signals around an identified entity, allowing
the agent to rule out failure types that conflict with the accumulated observations. Most failure-type corrections occur
in cases where Raw Trajectory already identifies the correct entity but misclassifies its failure, including 14 cases with
Kimi-K3 and 16 with GLM-5.3. Together with the trace analysis, this highlights how explicit belief consolidates failure
characteristics around an entity, while the Active Gap directs further investigation toward evidence that distinguishes
competing hypotheses.

Explicit belief construction

receives correct information but lacks the medical knowledge needed to interpret it. Even an accurate representation
of the current World State is insufficient when the agent lacks the execution skills or domain knowledge required to
complete the task. Addressing these limitations requires improvements in tool-use competence and domain-specific
reasoning alongside belief maintenance, rather than relying on context management alone.

Case Study
We use RCA-100 case t024 with Kimi-K3 to illustrate PoS’s trapping diagnosis and recovery. The agent observes
abnormal inventory-service behavior but remains unable to distinguish database waiting from JVM-side processing
despite continued investigation.
PoS identifies “Static Stagnation” with a persistent epistemic gap and combines
constraints against ineffective repetition with a requirement to seek evidence that distinguishes these explanations. The
agent subsequently obtains CPU observations and integrates them with the earlier GC evidence, revising its belief and
ultimately submitting the correct diagnosis. The case below traces this process from the unresolved diagnostic question
to targeted recovery and belief revision.

Limitations and Future Directions
Two limitations motivate future work on the efficiency of belief maintenance and the reliability of knowledge-dependent
reasoning in PoS.
(1) Cost of explicit belief maintenance.
The performance gains of PoS come with additional token and computational
costs.
Its natural language form structured Belief States require repeated text processing and generation during
maintenance and subsequent reasoning. Repeatedly expressing and revising beliefs in language introduces redundancy
and may cause information omission or semantic distortion. Future work could explore task-conditioned latent world-
state representations that encode decision-relevant information more compactly and support incremental updates
without repeatedly expanding the state into text. The key is to encode information about the current world state in a
latent representation with high information density that can be efficiently propagated and updated within the model.
We aim to preserve an accurate understanding of the current world state while substantially reducing the token and
computational costs of belief maintenance.
26


(2) Dependence on domain and task knowledge.
Although PoS receives external observations through tools, it does
not systematically integrate external domain-knowledge retrieval into belief construction and reasoning. Its ability
to identify relevant evidence and interpret observations therefore depends largely on the backbone model’s existing
knowledge. Insufficient knowledge may lead to omitted facts or incorrect causal and diagnostic judgments, even when
the resulting Belief State is internally consistent. Future work could incorporate domain-knowledge retrieval into
belief updates and evidence interpretation, helping the agent determine what information to retain and how it supports
or contradicts competing explanations. By incorporating relevant domain knowledge, we aim to improve the accuracy
of Belief States and the reliability of subsequent reasoning in specialized tasks.
