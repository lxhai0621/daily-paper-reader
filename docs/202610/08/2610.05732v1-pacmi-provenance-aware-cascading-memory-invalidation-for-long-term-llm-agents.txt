Abstract 

LLM agents rely on long-term memory to retain and reuse information when performing tasks over long horizons. Exist-ing methods provide limited support for handling memories that become outdated as new observations or domain evi-dence arrive. Such outdated memories may remain seman-tically relevant, continue to affect dependent records, and retain value as historical evidence. This calls for two capa-bilities: dependency tracking to identify downstream effects and historical preservation to retain useful past records. We propose Provenance-Aware Cascading Memory Invalidation (PACMI ), a framework that represents memories and new ev-idence in a provenance graph with typed dependency edges. PACMI assigns records to a four-state validity lattice, propa-gates validity changes to dependent memories, and uses the resulting states for retrieval and stale-premise detection. We also introduce a diagnostic benchmark with 100 cases and 300 queries across five domains. The evaluation separates node-, context-, and answer-level performance. PACMI achieves the highest final-answer accuracy on this benchmark, and its paired difference from the strongest baseline is significant under an exact McNemar test. The premise checker achieves perfect precision, recall, and F1 on the controlled query distri-bution. Cascading propagation primarily improves memory-state correctness: removing it increases final-answer errors from 3 to 11, but the paired difference does not reach the 0.05 significance threshold. Code and data will be made publicly available. 

## 1 Introduction 

LLM agents (Zhang et al. 2026b) can employ external tools to complete tasks progressively in a long-horizon. Through au-tonomous decision-making process, they have been widely applied to personalized assistance (Park et al. 2023), task automation (Yao et al. 2023), and multi-step problem solv-ing (Shinn et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024). These agentic tasks often require LLM agents to retain and reuse informa-tion across sessions rather than solve each task from scratch. Long-term memory has therefore become an important com-ponent of agent systems, supporting persistent user informa-tion (Zhong et al. 2023; Chhikara et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2026c; Yao and Zheng 2026), past interactions and task out-comes (Packer et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023; Lu et al. 2025a, 2026, 2025b), and accumulated experience for future tasks. Equipped with long-term memory, existing memory-augmented agents are mainly designed to improve memory representation and information utilization. Text-based and vector-indexed methods store past information as retrievable records and retrieve or summarize relevant content when needed (Lewis et al. 2020; Chhikara et al. 2025). Temporal methods organize memories over time to preserve evolving information (Rasmussen et al. 2025). Graph-based methods link related entities, facts, events, or experiences to support structured access and multi-hop retrieval (Edge et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2024; Gutiérrez et al. 2024). Recent systems fur-ther connect related notes and evolve memory structures to improve consolidation and long-term reuse (Xu et al. 2025; Lv et al. 2026). Although these methods improve memory storage, organization, and utilization, they pay limited at-tention to whether stored memories remain valid after new evidence arrives. 

Agent memory changes over time . New observations may update a user’s location, preference, goal, or plan. New do-main evidence may also conflict with previously stored in-formation. However, LLM agents often fail to identify such invalid memories, especially under implicit updates (Chao et al. 2026; Tao et al. 2026). Repeated LLM-based rewrit-ing may further introduce errors (Zhang et al. 2026a; Yao, Zheng, and Li 2026). Semantic relevance alone cannot solve this problem, because a stale memory may remain highly relevant and still be retrieved. 

Stale memories are not always useless . They may no longer support current decisions, but may remain valid as historical records. They may also affect downstream claims, assump-tions, or recommendations. For example, if a user moves from Sydney to San Francisco, the Sydney record should not sup-port current local recommendations. Related memories, such as restaurant suggestions or commuting assumptions, should also be re-evaluated. However, they may still be useful for historical queries. Stale-memory management therefore re-quires both dependency tracking and historical preservation. We propose PACMI , which represents prior memories and new evidence as nodes in a memory provenance graph with typed dependency edges. Given new evidence, PACMI classifies evidence–memory relations, identifies directly re-placed records, propagates validity along dependency edges into a four-state lattice, and then performs validity-aware retrieval and stale-premise checking before response gener-ation. Scope and assumptions. We state up front what this paper does and does not establish, because it determines how our numbers should be read. (A1) The dependency edges of the pre-update graph are given by the benchmark annotation. We study propagation and retrieval given a provenance graph; we do not study graph induction from raw memory text. (A2) 

Each case presents a single new-evidence item over a fixed two-hop dependency chain and six prior memories. (A3) 

Consequently, in this benchmark the ground-truth validity labels are a deterministic function of graph topology. Metrics that score downstream state assignment therefore verify that propagation is implemented correctly; they are not evidence of comparative advantage, and we do not present them as such (see the Experiments section). These assumptions make the benchmark diagnostic rather than naturalistic. We argue this is the right first step for a problem that no existing benchmark isolates, and we specify in the Limitations section exactly which experiments would remove each assumption. 

## 2 Related Work 

Long-term memory for LLM agents. Generative Agents maintain a memory stream of observations and reflections for long-horizon behavior (Park et al. 2023); Reflexion stores verbal feedback as episodic memory (Shinn et al. 2023); MemGPT treats memory management as virtual context with explicit tiers (Packer et al. 2023); MemoryBank and Long-Mem explore persistent user memory and long-context reuse (Zhong et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023); Mem0 extracts, con-solidates, and retrieves salient memories at scale (Chhikara et al. 2025). These systems target storage, consolidation, and retrieval. PACMI instead targets whether stored memories remain valid once new evidence arrives. 

Graph-structured memory, stale memory, and evalua-tion. GraphRAG and LightRAG show that graph index-ing captures dependencies that flat chunks cannot (Edge et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2024); HippoRAG supports as-sociative multi-hop retrieval (Gutiérrez et al. 2024); Zep uses a temporal knowledge graph for agent memory (Ras-mussen et al. 2025); A-MEM links new memories to re-lated historical notes (Xu et al. 2025); All-Mem maintains a topology-structured bank with non-destructive evolution (Lv et al. 2026). These improve organization and retrieval but do not track validity under evolving evidence. In par-allel, STALE evaluates whether agents recognize invalid memories, including state resolution and premise resistance (Chao et al. 2026), and MemConflict defines memory va-lidity as query-conditioned fitness-for-use (Tao et al. 2026); both focus on direct conflicts between old and new infor-mation. Broader benchmarks—LoCoMo (Maharana et al. 2024), LongMemEval (Wu et al. 2024), and MemoryAgent-Bench (Hu, Wang, and McAuley 2025)—cover recall, tem-poral reasoning, updates, forgetting, and conflict resolution, but none asks whether a system can identify the downstream 

memories affected by stale evidence, or distinguish current, historical, and invalidated use of the same record. 

Dependency-directed belief revision. Tracking which conclusions depend on which premises, and retracting them when a premise fails, is a classical AI problem. Truth mainte-nance systems record justifications and perform dependency-directed retraction: a JTMS relabels dependent nodes when a justification becomes unsupported (Doyle 1979), and an ATMS maintains labels over assumption sets so that multi-ple contexts coexist (de Kleer 1986). AGM belief revision axiomatizes the accompanying principle of minimal change (Alchourrón, Gärdenfors, and Makinson 1985). The database analogue is provenance and incremental view maintenance, where a base-tuple change invalidates exactly the derived views that depend on it (Gupta and Mumick 1995; Cheney, Chiticariu, and Tan 2009), with PROV-DM providing a stan-dard vocabulary for such dependencies (Moreau and Missier 2013). PACMI is, in this lineage, a truth maintenance system over natural-language agent memory. Three differences drive the design. First, the relation between new evidence and can-didate memories is expressed in natural language and must be inferred by an LLM under deterministic constraints. In the current benchmark, however, the pre-update dependency edges are supplied by annotation under assumption A1; in-ducing these edges from raw memory text remains future work. Second, labels are not binary IN/OUT: an agent must rank rather than admit or reject, so validity is a graded state coupled to retrieval scoring. Third, retraction is not deletion: a superseded record remains the correct answer to an explic-itly historical query, so invalidation must be non-destructive. To our knowledge this connection has not been made explicit in the agent-memory literature, and we make it here both to position the contribution and to indicate that the symbolic-TMS setting supplies a natural upper bound for future work. 

## 3 Problem Formulation 

Given newly observed evidence, the goal is not only to re-trieve relevant memories, but to determine which remain valid, which are replaced, and which downstream memories or decisions are affected. 

Memory provenance graph. Let Mt =

{m1, m 2, . . . , m n} denote the memories held before time step t, each with textual content, timestamp, source, and optional metadata. Rather than treating memories as independent records, we represent agent memory as a provenance graph Gt = (Vt, E t), where Vt contains prior-memory and new-evidence nodes and Et contains typed dependency edges. Prior-memory nodes store textual records that may describe facts, plans, recommendations, claims, historical notes, or previous decisions; these se-mantic roles are annotations, not runtime node classes. The evaluated pre-update graph contains supports and used-by 

edges; after relation classification PACMI may add an 

updates or contradicts edge from the new evidence to an accepted direct target. 

Task. At step t + 1 the agent receives evidence et+1 and query q, and must produce (Gt+1 , S t+1 , C q ): the updated graph, a validity-state assignment over memory nodes, and the retrieved context used for generation. This decomposes into three sub-goals: identify records directly replaced by or in conflict with the new evidence; trace dependency edges to the downstream records affected by them; and construct a context Cq that prioritizes appropriate evidence, reducing stale reuse while retaining history when the query is explicitly historical. 

Validity lattice. We use four validity states, ordered from weaker to stronger non-current status; from left to right, their eligibility for current-oriented retrieval decreases: active < needs-verification 

< historical-only < superseded .

Active records support current reasoning directly. Super-seded records state an older value that newer evidence has replaced. Needs-verification records depend on changed evidence and are admissible but discounted. Historical-only records are retained primarily for explicitly histori-cal queries. The ordering is not a convention: it is exactly the ordering of the query-dependent retrieval biases used at inference time ( +0 .12 , +0 .04 , −0.10 , −0.22 for current-oriented queries; see Appendix A), and it induces the "keep the stronger state” rule used when multiple propagation paths reach the same node. The state assignment is non-destructive throughout: original text is preserved, so a superseded record remains available as historical evidence. 1

Running example. Consider a hotel-booking scenario, fol-lowing the implicit-conflict setting of STALE (Chao et al. 2026). Evidence e1 supports memory m1, which supports claim c1, which is used by decision d1. The agent later ob-serves e2: Hotel X is sold out on January 10. The relation between e2 and m1 is classified as update , so m1 moves from active to superseded; propagation along supports moves 

c1 to needs-verification, and along used-by moves d1 to historical-only. The related hard negative m2 stays ac-tive because it does not depend on the superseded record. Standard retrieval may still return m1 and c1 and produce an outdated recommendation; PACMI’s validity-aware ranking instead prioritizes the new evidence and active memories. ## 4 Method 

Overview. PACMI separates local semantic judgment from global validity propagation. It inserts new evidence, classifies evidence–memory relations with an auxiliary LLM under deterministic constraints, accepts directly replaced records, propagates edge-dependent states, and constructs a validity-aware context before generation. 

Relation-aware conflict detection. Candidate ranking combines embedding similarity, token-level overlap, and matching semantic slots; exact weights are in Appendix A. PACMI retains up to six candidates; because each evaluated case has six prior memories, ranking orders but does not prune candidates in this benchmark. For each candidate the auxiliary LLM predicts duplicate , refine , update , contradict ,

temporally-compatible , or unrelated , with a reason. Deter-ministic slot-, stability-, and temporality-aware constraints then normalize these predictions. Only update and contra-dict can create a direct target. A candidate with an incom-ing supports or used-by edge from another prior memory is treated as a downstream record rather than a direct target; its effect is handled by propagation. This split is what keeps local classification from having to reason about dependency structure. 

Cascading invalidation and repair. From the accepted di-rect targets, PACMI performs breadth-first traversal over out-going dependency edges. In the evaluated benchmark, each accepted direct target is an update target and becomes super-seded; the transition map is supports →needs-verification and used-by →historical-only. When several paths reach one node, the stronger state in the lattice wins. Unreached nodes remain active. Repair is non-destructive: PACMI records relation-judgment and cascade-invalidation opera-tions while preserving the original text for traceability and historical retrieval. 

Validity-aware retrieval and premise checking. After propagation, PACMI ranks entries by semantic relevance, re-cency, slot match, new-evidence status, and query-dependent state biases. Current-oriented queries favor active and needs-verification records; historical queries instead fa-vor historical-only and superseded records. The top five entries, rendered with state and slot labels, form Cq .The premise checker consumes the inferred graph states ,not merely the retrieved context: it contrasts superseded prior records against the new evidence and active records. ## 5 Experiments 

We ask four questions. RQ1: how does PACMI compare with retrieval-based, revision-based, and graph-memory base-lines on direct stale-node identification, context quality, stale reuse, and final answers? RQ2: does cascading invalida-tion identify downstream affected memories without over-invalidating valid information? RQ3: how do validity-aware retrieval, premise checking, and historical preservation affect final accuracy and resistance to stale premises? RQ4: how do individual components and query types affect performance? 

5.1 Benchmark 

Existing long-term memory benchmarks evaluate factual re-call, temporal reasoning, or direct conflicts, with limited cov-erage of cases where new evidence invalidates not only a directly related memory but also downstream claims or de-cisions. We therefore construct a stale-memory dependency benchmark: 100 cases and 300 queries across five domains, each case with six prior memories, one new-evidence item, and three queries covering a current state, a stale premise, and a historical state. The evaluated dependency structure is the two-hop chain M1

5.2 Baselines and Setup 

Vector RAG uses semantic top-k retrieval, and Recency-RAG adds a recency score. LLM-Only Update locally clas-sifies and rewrites candidate memories before cosine re-trieval. CUPMem-style , adapted from STALE (Chao et al. 2026), supersedes records that directly state a replaced single-valued slot and admits previous values for histori-cal queries, but does not propagate downstream effects. A-MEM-style constructs semantic links and expands linked notes without explicit validity propagation (Xu et al. 2025). All methods use the same final-answer model, embedding model, and temperature-zero decoding; model, context-size, retrieval, and method-specific settings are in Appendix A. 

5.3 Metrics 

We evaluate node identification, retrieved context, and final answers separately; formal definitions are in Appendix B. Di-rect Stale-Node Recall (DR) and Affected-Node Recall (AR) are case-macro recalls over the ground-truth direct and down-stream sets. Over- and Under-Invalidation (OI, UI) are the fractions of cases whose predicted non-current set contains a node outside ground truth, or misses one inside it. Stale-in-Context (SIC) is the fraction of retrieved entries that are stale or affected, and Current Valid-Evidence Recall (CVER) is the fraction of active prior memories plus new evidence that is retrieved; both are averaged over current-oriented queries only, since superseded records may be appropriate evidence for a historical query. At the answer level, Final Accuracy 

follows the annotated expected behavior, Stale Reuse records whether an answer treats a directly stale memory as current, 

Premise Resistance is accuracy in correcting the stale as-sumption in Q2, and Q3 accuracy operationalizes historical preservation. A fixed deepseek-v4-pro judge applies the same temperature-zero rubric to all methods. 

Statistical analysis. We report percentile 95% confidence intervals from 10,000 case-level bootstrap resamples with a fixed seed of 20260728. All three queries belonging to a sampled case are resampled together, while structural metrics retain their original case-level definitions. For prespecified paired binary comparisons, we use two-sided exact McNemar tests. 

How to read the node-level metrics. By assumption A3, the ground-truth validity labels in this benchmark are a deter-ministic function of graph topology, and by A1 the topology is supplied as input. AR, UI, and recall-at-depth therefore certify that propagation is implemented correctly and that direct-target detection feeds it the right seeds; they do not 

certify a comparative advantage over a method that also had the graph. We report them for completeness and for repro-ducibility of the pipeline, and we base no claim of superiority on them. The claims in this paper rest on the context-level and answer-level metrics, the prespecified paired significance tests, and the separately reported intrinsic premise-detector evaluation. 5.4 Main Results 

Table 1 gives the comparison. Three observations matter, and only the first is a clean win. First, at the answer level PACMI reaches 0.99 final accu-racy against 0.90 for the strongest baseline, A-MEM-style, corresponding to 3 versus 29 errors over 300 paired queries. The difference is statistically significant under a two-sided exact McNemar test ( p = 2 .56 × 10 −6). The gap is concen-trated entirely in Q2: both methods answer the current-state and historical queries at ceiling, so this benchmark’s dis-criminative power lives primarily in stale-premise queries. Second, PACMI does not dominate on retrieval: A-MEM-style attains higher current valid-evidence recall (0.88 versus 0.83), because link expansion pulls in more valid context than validity-aware ranking and because A-MEM-style has a larger maximum expanded context. PACMI is better on stale-in-context (0.34 versus 0.47), i.e. it admits less out-dated material, but it pays for that in coverage. Third, stale reuse rounds to 0.00 for both PACMI and A-MEM-style, so on that metric we tie rather than lead. The one clearly harmful design is destructive rewriting. LLM-Only Update matches PACMI on direct stale-node re-call (1.00) but collapses on historical queries (0.42), because rewriting a memory in place destroys the record that a histor-ical query needs. This reproduces, in a controlled setting, the degradation reported for continuously LLM-updated mem-ory (Zhang et al. 2026a), and it argues for non-destructive invalidation independently of any propagation mechanism. 

Error analysis. PACMI’s three errors occur after success-ful premise detection and primarily reflect answer-generation failures. In one case, the new-evidence node is absent from the retrieved top-five context, although the premise-correction block still supplies the current fact; the remaining errors reflect insufficient grounding or incomplete execution of the expected answer behavior. LLM-Only Update’s 58 historical-query errors primarily reflect loss of earlier values under in-place rewriting. Appendix I reports representative cases from both sets. 

5.5 Ablation 

Table 2 disables one component at a time. The largest answer-level drops occur when premise checking is removed (Final 

0.99 → 0.83 , Premise 1.00 → 0.80 ) or historical preserva-tion is removed (Final 0.99 → 0.77 , Hist. 1.00 → 0.36 ). On the 100 Q2 queries, PACMI makes 3 errors whereas the

This ablation removes both the conflict detector and the premise-aware answer prompt, so the re-sult measures the combined premise-checking module rather than the detector alone. Removing provenance use or cascading traversal drives af-fected recall to 0.00 and under-invalidates every case, while raising stale-in-context from 0.34 to 0.37 and 0.41, respec-tively. Their final-accuracy changes are much smaller: re-moving provenance produces 6 additional errors, and remov-ing cascading produces 8 additional errors over 300 queries. For PACMI versus w/o Cascading Invalidation , the error counts are 3 versus 11 and the two-sided exact McNemar test gives p = 0 .0574 , which does not reach the 0.05 sig-nificance threshold. This non-significant result does not es-tablish equivalence; it indicates that the current sample does not provide sufficient evidence for an answer-level cascading effect. 

Intrinsic premise-detector performance. We evaluate the premise checker’s structured conflict decision inde-pendently of final-answer generation, treating Q2 as the pos-itive class and Q1/Q3 as negatives. Across the 300 queries, the checker produces 100 true positives, 200 true negatives, no false positives, and no false negatives, yielding precision, recall, and F1 of 1.00. This result is conditional on the cur-rent controlled benchmark: the checker receives facts already separated by inferred graph states into outdated and current sets, Q2 directly assumes an outdated value, and Q3 explic-itly asks about the past. It should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of perfect premise detection in unrestricted long-context settings. 

Why cascading has limited answer-level effect. Each case contains six prior memories with a context budget of five, and PACMI retrieves the new-evidence node in 98.7% of query contexts. The answer model therefore usually sees both the new evidence and the affected records even when downstream state labels are removed. Cascading propaga-tion provides correct state assignment and auditability, but its paired final-accuracy difference is not significant at this scale ( p = 0 .0574 ). Whether an answer-level gap emerges under larger memory stores or deeper dependency chains remains an open empirical question. 

5.6 Computational Cost 

A mechanism that buys accuracy with inference is only in-teresting if the exchange rate is stated, so Table 3 reports it. Relation classification is a single batched call scoring all candidates at once, so PACMI matches the other update-aware methods at update time; the difference is the per-query premise check, which the baselines do not have. That check is skipped without an LLM call when no superseded record exists, but every case here contains one, so the bound is tight and PACMI issues four times the auxiliary calls of the next most expensive method. This is the correct denomina-tor for our main result: 0.09 final accuracy over A-MEM-style for 300 extra calls, all at query time. Deployments that update often and query rarely therefore pay almost noth-ing, whereas query-dominated deployments should gate the premise check—for instance on whether the retrieved context contains any non-active record—rather than run it uncondi-tionally. We measured calls, not wall-clock latency or tokens.

## 7 Conclusion 

We formulated stale-memory resolution as dependency-aware validity management, gave a four-state validity lat-tice with typed-edge propagation, and released a diagnos-tic benchmark that separates memory-management behavior from final-answer behavior. On that benchmark PACMI at-tains the highest final accuracy, with a significant paired im-provement over the strongest baseline. The graph-state-based premise checker achieves perfect precision, recall, and F1

on the controlled query distribution, while non-destructive invalidation prevents the historical-query collapse observed under in-place LLM rewriting. Cascading propagation deliv-ers correct downstream state assignment and auditability, but its paired final-answer difference is not statistically signifi-cant at the evaluated memory scale. Larger memory stores, deeper and branching dependency structures, graph-quality degradation studies, and human or cross-family evaluation are needed to determine whether these state-level benefits translate into broader answer-level gains.

## D Intrinsic Evaluation of the Premise Detector 

The premise checker emits one structured conflict deci-sion for each query. We evaluate this decision independently of final-answer generation, using the benchmark query-type annotation as ground truth: Q2 is the positive class because it contains a stale premise, while Q1 and Q3 are negative because they ask about the current state and historical state, respectively. All 100 Q2 queries are classified as conflicts, while all 100 Q1 and 100 Q3 queries are classified as non-conflicts. The detector therefore has no false positives and no false neg-atives on this benchmark. This result is conditional on the evaluated pipeline: the checker receives facts already sepa-rated by the inferred graph states into outdated and current sets, Q2 directly assumes an outdated value, and Q3 ex-plicitly asks about the past. The result demonstrates correct premise-conflict classification on the current controlled dis-tribution, not perfect detection in unrestricted or naturalistic long-context settings. 

## E Statistical Protocol 

Each method is run once at temperature zero, so the re-ported uncertainty is over benchmark cases rather than over inference seeds. For every numeric cell reported in Ta-bles 1 and 2, we compute a percentile 95% confidence interval from 10,000 case-level bootstrap resamples using seed 20260728 . Each bootstrap replicate samples 100 cases with replacement, and all three queries belonging to a sam-pled case move together. Structural quantities retain their original case-level definitions within each replicate. Method-inapplicable cells marked “–” are not bootstrapped. For the three reported paired comparisons, we use a two-sided exact McNemar test on the discordant paired outcomes. The comparisons are PACMI versus A-MEM-style on fi-nal accuracy over all 300 queries, PACMI versus w/o Cas-cading Invalidation on final accuracy over all 300 queries, and PACMI versus w/o Premise Checking on Q2 accuracy over the 100 stale-premise queries. We use the conventional 

α = 0 .05 threshold and report the full discordant counts rather than only the significance decision. A non-significant result is not interpreted as evidence of equivalence. The three exact p-values are reported without multiplic-ity adjustment. They should therefore be interpreted as per-comparison evidence rather than as family-wise error-controlled decisions.

## H Detailed Ablation Results 

Over- versus under-invalidation. Among methods that make an explicit invalidation decision, none over-invalidates an active memory in the current benchmark. This is a prop-erty of the benchmark rather than of the methods: because every downstream node of a directly replaced memory is annotated as non-current, the benchmark contains no node that is dependency-connected to a stale record yet should remain active, so the over-invalidation metric cannot be ex-ercised. The observable difference therefore lies entirely in under-invalidation: LLM-Only Update misses at least one non-current node in 68% of cases, and CUPMem-style in all cases, because neither propagates downstream effects. 

Analysis by query type. Current-state queries measure whether stale information is appropriately handled when producing the current answer; stale-premise queries mea-sure whether the system identifies and corrects an invalid assumption; historical queries measure whether past infor-mation is preserved and retrieved under the correct temporal context. PACMI obtains 1.00, 0.97, and 1.00 respectively. Its observed advantage in the reported comparison is con-centrated on stale-premise queries. CUPMem-style preserves historical answers but performs less reliably on stale-premise queries under the main evaluation setting. Within PACMI, the w/o Premise Checking variant retains the same retrieval and graph states but removes both premise-conflict detec-tion and the premise-aware correction block: Q2 accuracy falls from 0.97 to 0.50, while Q1 and Q3 are unchanged. This ablation demonstrates the importance of the combined premise-checking module but does not isolate detection from answer-side prompting. 

## I Representative Error Analysis 

The analysis script enumerated all three PACMI final-answer errors and all 58 LLM-Only Update errors on Q3. We se-lected two representative examples from each set to illustrate the observed failure patterns across cases and domains. The quantitative error counts are computed from the logs; the mechanism descriptions below are qualitative interpretations of the corresponding retrieved contexts, rewritten memories, final answers, and judge reasons. 

PACMI: case_0022/Q2 . The query assumes that the user’s current home is London, whereas the new evidence states that the current city is San Francisco. PACMI’s premise checker correctly identifies London as outdated and returns San Francisco as the current fact. Although N1 is absent from the top-five retrieved context, the premise-correction block still supplies the current fact to the answer generator. The generated answer rejects London as current but fails to state San Francisco. This is therefore best interpreted as a generation or instruction-following failure under reduced retrieval support, rather than as a pure premise-detection failure or a pure retrieval-coverage failure. 

PACMI: case_0072/Q2 . The query assumes a tax-sensitive-income investment profile, whereas the current pro-file is diversified growth. PACMI retrieves N1 and correctly identifies and repairs the premise, but the generator proceeds to give a generic diversified-growth recommendation. The expected behavior requires acknowledging that the memory contains no specific allocation or fund recommendation for the new profile. This is a generation-grounding failure rather than a detection or retrieval failure. 

LLM-Only Update: case_0001/Q3 . The historical query asks which profession preceded data scientist. LLM-Only Update rewrites the original profession memory from accountant to data scientist. The previous value is therefore no longer available, and the final answer incorrectly reports that the earlier profession is unspecified. This illustrates de-structive loss of historical evidence under in-place rewriting. 

LLM-Only Update: case_0021/Q3 . The historical query asks which city preceded Singapore. LLM-Only Up-date rewrites the original home city from Berlin to Singa-pore, after which the system can no longer recover Berlin as the historical answer. ## K Prompt Templates 

PACMI contains three LLM-driven system components: rela-tion classification, premise checking, and answer generation. The evaluation additionally uses an LLM-based answer-level judge. Relation classification, premise checking, and answer judging require structured JSON output, whereas answer generation returns plain text. All calls use temperature = 0 and a task-specific system message. Angle-bracketed fields are populated at runtime. Line wrapping, example items, and dynamically rendered lists are normalized below without changing the instructional content. 

Relation Classification Prompt. The relation classifier uses deepseek-v4-flash . Its system message is: “You are a strict memory-relation classifier. Return JSON only.” 

1 You are a memory relation classifier. 

2 Task: classify the relation between the new evidence and each old memory. Return JSON only. 

34 Allowed labels: DUPLICATE, REFINE, UPDATE, CONTRADICT, TEMPORALLY_COMPATIBLE, UNRELATED. 

56 Definitions: 

7 - UPDATE: the new evidence directly replaces an old current fact in the same slot. 

8 - CONTRADICT: the new evidence directly conflicts with an old current fact in the same slot. 

9 - REFINE: the new evidence adds detail without invalidating the old memory. 

10 - TEMPORALLY_COMPATIBLE: both memories can be true at different times. 

11 - DUPLICATE: both memories express nearly the same fact. 

12 - UNRELATED: no substantive relation. 

13 14 Constraints: 

15 - Use UPDATE or CONTRADICT only when the old memory itself is the current fact replaced by the new evidence. 

16 - Treat markers such as current, active, on file, latest saved, still lists, still says, and still treats as assertions of a current fact. If new evidence explicitly says now/current/changed/ updated and gives a different value in the same single-value slot, classify that old current fact as UPDATE or CONTRADICT, not TEMPORALLY_COMPATIBLE or REFINE. 

17 - Use TEMPORALLY_COMPATIBLE only when the old memory is explicitly historical, using wording such as previous, earlier, former, used to, was previously, or historical. An older timestamp by itself does not make a current-fact statement historical. 

18 - Stable user preferences, output-format preferences, general rules, reusable checklists, templates, and advice style should not become stale merely because aprofession, location, project, or tool changed. 

19 - If an old memory is a plan, draft, recommendation, or historical record based on an old fact, prefer TEMPORALLY_COMPATIBLE or REFINE; downstream impact is handled separately by graph propagation.
