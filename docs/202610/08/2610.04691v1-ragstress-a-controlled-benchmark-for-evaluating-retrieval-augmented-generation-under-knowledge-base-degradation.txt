ABSTRACT 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is typically evaluated under the implicit assumption that the underlying knowledge base (KB) is clean, leaving the behaviour of RAG systems under realistic KB degradation poorly characterised. We introduce RAGS TRESS , a controlled evaluation benchmark for stress-testing RAG systems under systematic KB corruption. The benchmark pairs four naturalistic corruption types (factual corruption, numeric typo, relevance poisoning, contradiction injection) with three severity levels (subtle, moderate, obvious) over a single-KB, metadata-filtered experimental design built from 57 MMLU subjects and 182,546 documents. Across 52,500 model–question– condition evaluations, RAGS TRESS reveals that clean retrieval can mask robustness differences, semantic-fidelity corruptions are substantially more harmful than signal-utility perturbations, no-retrieval accuracy does not predict corrupted-retrieval robustness, and mixed-KB accuracy should not be treated as worst-case robustness. We document the benchmark’s intended use, supported claims, and limitations, and provide an artifact bundle including generation scripts, corruption prompts, metadata schema, and evaluation code. RAGS TRESS is intended as a controlled stress test for RAG robustness under KB corruption, not as a general model leaderboard. 

1 Introduction 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [1] grounds large language models (LLMs) in an external knowledge base (KB), giving them access to up-to-date information, reducing hallucination, and extending the effective knowledge of smaller models [2]. The leverage it provides can be substantial: in our benchmark, a clean KB raises the accuracy of an 8B-parameter model from 42 .3% to 98 .6% on a multi-domain evaluation, a gain no amount of parameter scaling could plausibly deliver. The same model, however, drops to 27% once those KB documents contain factual corruption, well below its no-retrieval baseline. The same mechanism that makes RAG effective also makes it fragile. This fragility is poorly served by current RAG evaluation. Benchmarks typically assume the retrieval corpus is clean, but real-world KBs accumulate errors long before any adversary intervenes—a data-quality problem long studied in the knowledge discovery community: data-entry mistakes, document versioning conflicts, OCR noise in ingestion pipelines, outdated content that has not been pruned. At generation time the model implicitly arbitrates between its parametric prior and the retrieved context, and the quality of that arbitration decides whether corrupted documents are discounted or faithfully reproduced. How much this matters, for which corruption types, and how it should be evaluated are still open questions. Prior work on RAG robustness splits into adversarial poisoning, knowledge conflict, and noisy retrieval, reviewed in Section 2; none treats corruption type and severity as joint evaluation variables on a fixed retrieval pipeline. We address this gap with RAGS TRESS , a controlled evaluation benchmark for stress-testing RAG under systematic KB corruption. 2 Related Work 

RAG and its evaluation. RAG [1] augments an LLM with a non-parametric document index, typically retrieved by dense search [4], and successive surveys [2] identify retrieval quality as the dominant driver of downstream accuracy. Retrieval grounds generation partly to curb hallucination, a pervasive LLM failure mode [5]; yet almost all RAG evaluations assume the retrieval corpus is clean. A growing line of work tries to close that gap, and our benchmark sits in a specific corner of it: naturalistic KB corruption, treated as an evaluation variable rather than an attack objective. 

Knowledge-base poisoning and RAG security. The most active strand of prior work studies adversarial attacks on the KB. PoisonedRAG [6] injects optimised passages and achieves more than 90% attack success; Phantom [7] uses trigger-based backdoors; BadRAG [8] shows that a small number of adversarial passages suffices; Zhong et al. [9] inject adversarial passages that transfer across queries; the single-injection attack of Zhang et al. [10] improves stealth and feasibility; Zhang et al. [11] benchmark 13 attacks against 7 defences; and RobustRAG [12] provides certifiable robustness via an isolate-then-aggregate defence. As evaluation protocols, these works adopt the attacker’s perspective and treat corruption as binary (clean or poisoned), optimised for worst-case attack success rather than the naturalistic, non-adversarial KB degradation practitioners encounter; our benchmark instead treats corruption type and severity as controlled evaluation variables. For a broader map of this threat-and-defence landscape see the recent survey of Mu et al. [13]. Table 2 (Appendix A) positions RAGS TRESS against the closest prior work on these axes. 

Knowledge conflicts and context faithfulness. A second strand examines how LLMs arbitrate when retrieved context contradicts parametric memory [14]. Longpre et al. [15] find that models tend to over-rely on parametric memory under entity-substitution counterfactuals, and Xie et al. [16] show that models can be either context-following or prior-stubborn depending on how the conflict is framed; both studies use narrow entity substitutions on earlier-generation models. Neeman et al. [17] report that training models to predict parametric and contextual answers separately improves robustness to such conflicts, and Shi et al. [18] demonstrate that LLMs are easily distracted by irrelevant context in arithmetic reasoning, early evidence that context utility, not only content truthfulness, affects downstream accuracy and motivates the semantic-fidelity vs. signal-utility distinction we formalise in Section 3. Goyal et al. [19] further show that instruction finetuning can worsen a model’s reliance on context, evidence that context–memory arbitration is a trained behaviour that varies by system, consistent with the cross-model heterogeneity we observe. ClashEval [20] extends this line to graded context vs. parametric conflicts on current-generation models and is closest in spirit to our severity axis; our benchmark revisits the same questions with a broader corruption taxonomy. 

Robustness of RAG to noisy retrieval. A third strand studies what happens when retrieval simply returns low-quality or off-topic documents. Zeng et al. [21] show that misleading retrievals can push RAG below its no-retrieval baseline; Wu et al. [22] find that not all noise degrades performance, and some noise types can even help; and Cuconasu et al. [23] report that irrelevant documents can improve generation in the right positions. NoMIRACL [24] evaluates hallucination under non-relevant retrieval across 18 languages, and Bogavelli et al. [25] measure LLM perturbation consistency across formats and languages in enterprise settings. These studies characterise what noise does, but do not vary KB-corruption type and severity jointly on a common retrieval pipeline.

RAG evaluation benchmarks. Existing benchmarks address the evaluation question from different angles. RGB [26] evaluates four RAG abilities (noise robustness, negative rejection, information integration, and counterfactual ro-bustness); CRAG [27] offers a large-scale factual-QA benchmark spanning diverse domains and question types; and RAGAS [28] provides component-level reference-free metrics for faithfulness, answer relevance, and context relevance. Closest in structure is QE-RAG [29], which crosses error types with severity rates, but it perturbs the query rather than the knowledge base, so it measures robustness to user-side input noise rather than to KB degradation. None of these treats KB-corruption type, severity, and mixed- versus type-filtered retrieval as jointly varied evaluation variables in a single controlled design. Our primary contribution is of this evaluation-design kind, complementing rather than competing with the attack, defence, and metric literatures surveyed above.

4 Evaluation Design 

Figure 1 summarises the RAGS TRESS evaluation framework. A single vector KB stores both clean MMLU-derived reference documents and their corrupted variants, generated along four corruption types at three severity levels. Experimental conditions (clean, per-type × severity, and unfiltered) are realised at query time via metadata filters, so every system queries the same index through the same retriever. The remainder of this section states what the framework is intended to support, the assumptions it rests on, and how each condition is implemented. 

4.1 Intended Use and Assumptions Intended use. RAGS TRESS is a controlled stress test for RAG robustness under KB corruption, not a model leaderboard. It supports claims about (i) accuracy loss under four corruption types, (ii) severity effects, (iii) whether parametric accuracy predicts corrupted-retrieval robustness, and (iv) mixed-KB versus type-isolated worst cases. It is not intended for raw factual-recall comparison, retriever evaluation, or multi-hop or agentic RAG. 

Assumptions. The design rests on three assumptions. (A1) Metadata-filtered retrieval from a single KB is methodolog-ically equivalent to running each condition against a separate index, while better controlling for embedding-distribution and retrieval-score-calibration confounds. (A2) Exact-match MCQ scoring eliminates LLM-as-judge variance, attribut-ing differences to KB content; findings may not transfer directly to open-ended generation. (A3) The retriever (top-k=5 

dense search) and prompt are fixed across all conditions and models, so observed differences reflect KB content and model behaviour, not retrieval or prompt-engineering variation. 4.2 Single-KB, Metadata-Filter Design 

Maintaining one KB per condition introduces confounds from index construction, embedding distribution, and document count. Instead, we build a unified KB containing every document version (clean and all corrupted variants), each tagged with structured metadata. At query time, a server-side metadata predicate restricts retrieval to the active condition’s document subset, giving identical retrieval behaviour across conditions. 

4.3 Experimental Conditions via Metadata Filtering 

We define 15 experimental conditions, each enforced by a metadata predicate at query time. Baseline (no retrieval) measures the model’s parametric knowledge. Clean only (is_corrupted=false ) establishes the RAG upper bound. 

Unfiltered applies no predicate, so corrupted documents outnumber clean ones 12:1 in the KB and account for ≈79% 

of retrieved chunks, simulating an unaudited KB. The remaining 12 type × severity conditions each restrict retrieval to a single corruption type and severity level, isolating the effect of each perturbation class. The full condition table is in Appendix D. 

5 Benchmark Construction 

Construction proceeds in three stages: (1) converting MMLU items into retrievable knowledge documents (the clean corpus); (2) generating corrupted variants via an LLM-based perturbation pipeline; and (3) ingesting clean and corrupted documents, with their metadata, into a single vector KB that supports the conditions in Section 4.3. 

5.1 Dataset: MMLU as a Knowledge-Base Corpus 

We ground the study in the Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) benchmark [3], which spans 57 academic subjects across STEM, humanities, social sciences, and professional domains. 

Document conversion. Each MMLU item (question stem, four choices, correct label) is rewritten into a short reference-article-style document mirroring an FAQ entry, keeping the ground truth explicit and machine-verifiable. A representative example is in Appendix C. 

Corpus scale. We use the test split of all 57 MMLU subjects, covering STEM (18), Humanities (13), Social Sciences (12), and Other (14). With one clean and 12 corrupted variants per item (Section 5.2), the full KB contains 182,546 documents .

5.2 Perturbation Method Corruption taxonomy. We instantiate the four corruption types defined in Section 3, factual corruption, numeric typo, relevance poisoning, and contradiction injection, at three severity levels, giving 12 corrupted variants per document. 5.4 Representativeness of the Synthetic Corruptions 

Because the corrupted documents are LLM-generated rather than harvested from a live corpus, we make explicit what real-world degradation each type is designed to stand in for, and where the analogy stops. Each corruption type mirrors a distinct, documented failure mode of production knowledge bases: factual corruption models stale or mis-entered content that remains fluent and authoritative (e.g. an outdated value never pruned after a policy change); numeric typo 

models data-entry and OCR errors in figures, a dominant error class in ingested tables and scanned documents; relevance poisoning models versioning drift and topic-adjacent clutter that accumulates when documents are appended but never curated; and contradiction injection models multi-source KBs that ingest conflicting statements of the same fact. The severity axis operationalises how conspicuous the degradation is, from edits a human reader would likely miss to overt errors. We quantify one facet of representativeness directly: under the neutral prompt the corruptions do measurably shift model behaviour in the predicted direction (semantic-fidelity types overturn 25–72% of clean-answerable answers; §6.3), confirming they are not inert artifacts. Three boundaries of the analogy should be read alongside these mappings, and are revisited in the limitations of §6.3. First, all corruptions come from a single generator (Claude 3 Haiku), so absolute rates may carry a generator signature; because two evaluated systems are Claude-family, a generator-swap study would isolate this confound, which we leave to future work. Second, severity is an LLM-assigned operational label, not a human-validated perceptual scale; we therefore treat severity as a controlled knob rather than a claim about human-perceived conspicuousness. Third, documents are derived from benchmark items rather than a naturally occurring corpus, trading ecological validity for machine-verifiable ground truth. We accordingly frame RAGS TRESS as a controlled stress test whose value is comparative (how systems and corruption types rank relative to one another under identical conditions), not a forecast of absolute failure rates on any particular deployed KB.

6.3 Findings and Analysis 

We organise the results around five findings, unified by the theme that RAG accuracy depends on how the model arbitrates between parametric knowledge and retrieved evidence, a property that current evaluation protocols do not isolate. 

Finding 1: Clean retrieval saturates accuracy. Clean retrieval compresses a 44pp capability gap into a 1.4pp accuracy band, masking heterogeneity that corruption later reveals. All five models reach 98.6–100.0% with clean context (Table 1), despite no-retrieval baselines ranging from 42.3% to 86.7%. The uplift is largest for the weakest model (Llama 3.1 8B, +56 .3pp) and sizeable even for mid-baseline systems (DeepSeek R1, +22 .8pp from 77.1%), showing that a clean KB can lift models at every capability level to a near-ceiling band that is itself too narrow to distinguish them. The same context-reliance, however, becomes a failure mode under corruption. 

Finding 2: Semantic corruption is far more harmful than utility noise. Corruption type matters more than severity: the gap between factual corruption and relevance poisoning ranges from ∼25pp to nearly 70pp across systems, dwarfing the within-type gap between subtle and obvious variants ( ≤4pp). Finding 3: No-RAG accuracy does not predict corrupted-retrieval robustness. A model with 4.5pp more paramet-ric knowledge can be 45pp less robust under corruption, indicating that corrupted-retrieval arbitration is a distinct capability axis. Qwen3-32B outscores Claude Opus 4.6 at baseline (83.1% vs. 78.6%) yet collapses to 29.9% under factual corruption, while Opus stays at 74.7% (Appendix Table 6). DeepSeek R1 sharpens the same point in another direction: despite one of the lower baselines (77.1%), it reaches near-ceiling clean-KB accuracy (99.9%) yet suffers a large semantic-corruption drop (factual corruption collapses to 50.4%, −49 .5pp from clean), on par with Claude Sonnet 4 despite a sizeably lower parametric baseline. With five models spanning proprietary, reasoning-tuned, and open-weight systems this remains illustrative; a broader sweep would test generality. Quantifying with the resilience gap (Eq. 3): ΓFACT (Opus , Qwen ) = 45 .1 and ΓCONTRA (Opus , Qwen ) = 47 .4. The robustness divide dwarfs the 4.5pp baseline difference. 

Finding 4: Moderate contradictions can be more damaging than obvious ones. The most dangerous corruption is not the most extreme one—a pattern invisible under binary clean-vs.-poisoned protocols. Contradiction injection shows a non-monotonic, U-shaped severity pattern for four of five models (Table 1): Claude Sonnet 4 drops to 41.1% at moderate severity, versus 55.7% (subtle) and 56.1% (obvious), a 15.0pp dip whose 95% CI excludes zero; Qwen3-32B, Llama 3.1 8B, and DeepSeek R1 (59.7% vs. 67.1/68.1%) follow the same shape, though their mod − obv gaps straddle zero in some cases and warrant further replication. Only Claude Opus 4.6 shows a near-flat severity profile for contradiction. Severity labels are LLM-assigned and lack human validation, so this U-shape may partly reflect generator behaviour; even so, stress tests should include plausible contradictions, not only extreme ones. 

Finding 5: Mixed KBs are not equivalent to worst-case corruption. Mixed-contamination robustness and worst-case corruption robustness are distinct properties; collapsing them reverses system rankings. Unfiltered accuracy stays well above type-filtered worst cases for every model (Appendix Table 6): Claude Opus 4.6 drops only 4.7pp from clean, DeepSeek R1 drops 8.6pp, Claude Sonnet 4 drops 17.0pp, Qwen3-32B drops 18.4pp, and Llama 3.1 8B drops 23.9pp. When clean evidence is still retrievable, models partially recover, so unfiltered accuracy should not be reported as worst-case sensitivity. These findings expose a core tension in RAG: the same context-reliance that makes RAG powerful makes it fragile. Clean retrieval lifts smaller models to near-ceiling accuracy, yet semantic corruption erases that gain and can push performance below random choice. Scope and limitations. The benchmark is scoped to MCQ for scoring reliability; open-ended and multi-hop QA are natural extensions the metadata-filtered design supports. KB documents are derived from benchmark items, trading ecological validity for controlled ground truth. All corruptions are produced by a single generator (Claude 3 Haiku); since two evaluated models are Claude-family, a generator-swap ablation would quantify the same-family confound. Severity labels are LLM-assigned without human validation (see F4 caveat). The evaluated systems use different serving interfaces, so differences should be read as system-level rather than purely parameter-level. MMLU items likely appear in the models’ pre-training data, so no-retrieval baselines partly reflect memorisation; this inflates absolute baselines but leaves the comparative findings intact, since all conditions share the same questions and pipeline. Controlled partial corruption (varying the corrupted fraction in top-k) and targeted adversarial injections are complementary future directions. 

7 Conclusion and Future Work 

We presented a controlled evaluation of how knowledge-base corruption affects retrieval-augmented generation. Across five models, four corruption types, and three severity levels, clean retrieval brings every model close to ceiling accuracy, but corrupted retrieval exposes large differences in how models arbitrate between retrieved evidence and parametric knowledge. The dominant failure modes are semantic: factual corruption and contradiction injection are far more damaging than numeric typos or relevance poisoning. We further find that no-retrieval accuracy does not reliably predict corrupted-retrieval robustness, and that moderate contradictions can be more damaging than obvious ones. These results argue that RAG evaluation should measure not only whether retrieval helps under clean conditions, but also whether the model can reject fluent, relevant, and misleading evidence. The metadata-filtered framework is reusable: new corruption types, severity levels, or retrieval configurations can be added without rebuilding the index. Future work should extend this evaluation in three directions. First, move beyond MMLU-style multiple choice toward open-ended QA, citation-heavy generation, multi-hop reasoning, and naturally occurring enterprise or web documents, in line with broader RAG evaluation settings [1, 2]. Second, study how retrievers, rerankers, embedding models, and chunking strategies interact with corruption, building on prior work on noisy and misleading retrieval [21]. Third, evaluate defences, retrieval filtering, contradiction detection, provenance tracking, abstention, and robust aggregation, under the same controlled protocol, connecting this benchmark to prior work on RAG poisoning, knowledge conflicts, and robust RAG defences [6, 8, 11, 12, 15]. All artifacts are publicly released or pledged for DOI-archived release upon publication (Section 1, footnote; Appendix F.8).

B Corruption Type Details 

This appendix expands on the four corruption types and their severity calibration, supplementing the summary in Section 5.2. For each type we describe the perturbation, the failure mode it simulates, and how severity is operationalised in the generation prompt. 

Factual corruption. The correct answer label is replaced with a different, plausible-but-wrong option. The question stem and the four choices are left unchanged, so the document remains internally coherent but asserts a false fact. This simulates the most dangerous class of KB error: a document that looks authoritative and well-formed yet contains a wrong answer. Severity controls the plausibility of the substituted answer—subtle corruptions choose the most confusable distractor, while obvious corruptions choose a clearly inferior one. 

Numeric typo. A realistic transcription error is introduced into a numeric value within the correct answer, using one of three patterns: a missing digit ( 100 → 10 ), an extra digit ( 100 → 1000 ), or a digit transposition ( 123 → 132 ). These simulate OCR errors, copy-paste mistakes, or unit-conversion errors common in automated ingestion pipelines. Severity controls the magnitude of the introduced error. 

Relevance poisoning. Two to three sentences of plausible but off-topic content are appended to the document. The original correct answer is preserved, so the document is not factually wrong, but the injected material can distract the retriever (by shifting the embedding away from the query) or the reader (by introducing irrelevant context that competes for attention). Severity controls the topical distance of the injected sentences from the original subject. 

Contradiction injection. A contradicting statement is appended that explicitly asserts a different answer is correct, directly conflicting with the original. Unlike factual corruption, which silently replaces the answer, contradiction injection creates an intra-document conflict : the document simultaneously supports two incompatible answers, testing whether the model can detect and resolve internal inconsistencies in retrieved context. Severity controls the assertiveness and apparent authority of the contradicting claim—subtle contradictions hedge with qualifiers, while obvious ones make bold, unqualified assertions. 

Generation and evaluation prompts. All corruptions are produced by Claude 3 Haiku from type-specific prompt templates (one per corruption type, with severity encoded in the prompt instruction). The evaluator uses a neutral answering prompt that does not instruct the model to flag inconsistencies or distrust context, so robustness differences reflect default arbitration behaviour. Full generation and answering prompt templates, together with the retrieval configuration, are released in the artifact bundle described in Sections 5.2–5.3.

is_corrupted metadata flag. • The benchmark is designed for evaluation and stress-testing, not for teaching models to generate misinforma-tion. • We do not release full adversarial prompts that could be directly weaponised for KB-poisoning attacks; we release the corruption type descriptions and representative examples.
