Abstract 

Recent advancements in large language models have introduced new capabilities for reason-ing over structured data, particularly through program-aided tools that can analyze tables. However, many existing methods address single-table scenarios or assume that the rel-evant tables are already provided. In prac-tice, users often issue complex data exploration queries over entire databases, where relevant information may be distributed across multiple relations. In this work, we introduce DBRAG, a retrieval-augmented generation framework tailored for multi-table question answering. DBRAG first retrieves candidate tables using an offline table index, enriches their summaries with query-relevant rows, and uses an LLM to rerank the candidates. A program-aided rea-soner then selects the required tables and exe-cutes operations over their full contents, keep-ing the initial prompt context compact. Ex-periments on the Spider, GeoQuery, and ATIS datasets used in this study demonstrate im-provements in table retrieval and multi-table question answering. 

1 Introduction 

Tabular data structures are a widely used data model for storing information in various domains. In real-world applications, these tables often con-tain complex, interrelated data with relational de-pendencies. When interpreted, relational data can lead to improved decision-making, enhanced oper-ational efficiency, and the discovery of new hidden insights (Fey et al., 2024). Given the inherent com-plexity of structured query languages, such as SQL, using natural language can lower the barrier for data exploration (Li et al., 2024). Consequently, table question-answering (QA) has emerged as an important research area focused on developing sys-tems that can understand natural language queries and extract relevant insights from tabular data (Jin et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2025). Although significant progress has been made in single-table QA scenarios (Liu et al., 2022; Herzig et al., 2020; Nan et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2021), the more challenging and practical task of multi-table QA remains relatively underexplored. Multi-table QA involves answering questions that require the integration of information from multiple tables, ne-cessitating sophisticated reasoning and advanced data fusion techniques (Pal et al., 2023). This challenge is further compounded by the complex relationships between tables, such as identifying joinable keys or overlapping attributes, particularly when such relationships lack a well-structured ex-plicit relational schema, as is often the case in web tables and data lakes (Cafarella et al., 2008; Chap-man et al., 2020). Many existing studies assume that the relevant tables are already provided (Chen et al., 2024b; Pal et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). In an open-domain table QA setting, the system must instead retrieve them from a larger corpus. This requires consid-ering both query-table relevance and table-table relationships: a table with limited direct relevance may still be needed to connect complementary in-formation (Chen et al., 2024a). Large language models (LLMs) have demon-strated remarkable proficiency in various natural language processing tasks (Minaee et al., 2024). Their adaptation to QA via retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has led to substantial perfor-mance gains (Lewis et al., 2020). In the RAG framework, relevant information is first efficiently retrieved from a pre-indexed large corpus and used to augment the query provided to the LLM, thereby mitigating issues such as hallucinations and out-dated knowledge. Despite the effectiveness of the RAG framework in single-table QA contexts (Chen et al., 2024b), its application to multi-table QA remains under-explored. The challenges of multi-table QA stem from several factors. First, multi-table QA requires 1arXiv:2610.07622v1 [cs.IR] 6 Oct 2026 the system to reason across multiple tables, often necessitating different types of joins and aggrega-tions. Second, the sheer volume of tables in a typ-ical database complicates the retrieval process, as the system must identify not only the main tables relevant to a query but also any auxiliary tables needed to connect and augment the data. Third, each table can vary significantly in size, both in the number of columns and rows. For instance, data warehouses often contain wide tables with billions of rows. These challenges, combined with the lim-itations of current LLM context windows, make the direct application of LLMs to multi-table QA non-trivial, thereby necessitating novel approaches. To tackle the aforementioned challenges, we pro-pose a novel methodology that extends the RAG framework to encompass the entire database. Our approach decomposes the problem of multi-table QA into two critical subtasks, multi-table retrieval 

and multi-table reasoning . ➀ In the retrieval step, we adopt a two-pronged approach to identify the relevant tables in a scalable manner. First, table search quickly selects a subset of tables relevant to the query from the entire database using a table index. Next, we enrich the tables of interest by augmenting them with query-relevant rows. The collected context is then fed into an LLM to se-lect the most promising tables. ➁ In multi-table reasoning , we propose a novel reasoning protocol designed to answer the natural language question given the retrieved tables. Specifically, we design a multi-table-aware Chain-of-Thought prompting technique combined with a program-aided reasoner that executes operations over the retrieved tables and returns the final answer. Our experiments on multi-table QA datasets demonstrate that DBRAG not only improves the retrieval of relevant tables but also enhances the reasoning task, resulting in higher overall QA performance in the evaluated settings. In summary, we make the following contributions: 

⋆ We introduce a RAG approach for multi-table question answering in settings where the rele-vant tables must be retrieved from a large table corpus. 

⋆ We propose DBRAG, a novel framework that decomposes multi-table QA into two subtasks: a two-stage retrieval strategy that leverages table indexing and context enrichment, and a multi-table reasoning protocol that integrates Chain-of-Thought prompting with program-aided execu-tion. 

⋆ We demonstrate through comprehensive experi-ments that DBRAG improves both table retrieval and reasoning performance, resulting in effective end-to-end QA execution.

2 Related Work 

In this section, we provide a brief overview of retrieval-augmented generation and then discuss the most pertinent works on table QA, highlighting both the inspiration and the novelty of our contri-butions. 

LLMs and RAG. Large language models have revolutionized natural language processing with their advanced text comprehension and reasoning capabilities. However, they are not designed to handle domain-specific knowledge, rapidly chang-ing information, private data, or long-tail entities. These limitations result in issues such as hallu-cinations (Huang et al., 2025), spurious explana-tions (Camburu, 2020), and inconsistencies (Elazar et al., 2021; Adiwardana et al., 2020). Recent tech-niques have improved reasoning through prompting methods, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022) for step-by-step reasoning, and Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023) for selecting the most consistent answer across sampled reasoning paths. To overcome these challenges, recent research has focused on injecting domain-specific knowl-edge into language models by integrating external sources and leveraging domain corpora. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has proven effective thanks to a two-stage retrieve-and-read framework, in which the retriever quickly extracts relevant data from large corpora (Gao et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2024). By incorporating accurate, up-to-date infor-mation, RAG can enhance the LLM’s response per-tinence, reduce hallucinations, and produce more reliable, contextually relevant outputs. 

Language Models for Table Understanding. 

Numerous studies have focused on pretraining table-specific language models (LMs) for down-stream tasks. Building on the success of masked language modeling, TAPAS (Herzig et al., 2020) introduces a BERT-like architecture enhanced with positional embeddings to encode table-specific 2information. During pretraining, the model re-constructs masked table elements. In contrast to TAPAS’s reconstruction approach, TAPEX (Liu et al., 2022) pretrains a language model to execute SQL operations on tables, motivated by the intu-ition that accurate SQL execution reflects robust table comprehension. To advance LM capabilities in table retrieval, Herzig et al. (2021) develop a dense retriever leveraging contrastive learning, pre-training, and negative sample mining. By employ-ing TAPAS as the base encoder, they demonstrate that dense retrieval outperforms traditional meth-ods like BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) in open-domain single-table retrieval. 

LLMs for Single-table QA. Ye et al. (2023) de-velop a method that enables an LLM to construct sub-evidences and sub-questions to answer natural language questions over large tables. Their ap-proach employs in-context learning by providing a small set of manually crafted examples to guide table deconstruction and reasoning. Chen et al. (2024b) introduce a RAG framework for single-table QA, retrieving relevant schema and cells to address scalability and context length limitations when feeding large tables to the LLM. Inspired by the effectiveness of reasoning chains in textual con-texts, Wang et al. (2024) propose using an LLM agent to dynamically plan table operations based on prior steps, forming an iterative reasoning pro-cess. They demonstrate that enabling LLMs to generate a reasoning chain improves performance in single-table QA. 

Multi-table QA. While single-table QA has gar-nered research attention, studies on multi-table QA remain limited. Pal et al. (2023) make a pioneer-ing contribution by pretraining the TAPEX model on a multi-table dataset with SQL queries and tab-ular answers, followed by fine-tuning on natural language questions. However, their method as-sumes the relevant tables are already provided to the model and does not address scenarios where flattened table representations exceed the model’s maximum sequence length. Chen et al. (2024a) in-troduce a method focused on multi-table retrieval, solving a mixed-integer program that jointly opti-mizes table-query relevance and table-table rele-vance. This approach adapts to databases lacking key-foreign-key constraints. It uses flattened table representations for coarse-grained retrieval, while downstream question answering relies on SQL gen-eration and execution. 

3 Methodology 

3.1 Motivation and Overview 

As mentioned in the introduction, directly present-ing all tables to an LLM can be expensive and time-consuming. Additionally, methods that pass com-plete table information often run into context length limitations, especially with large tables. To make matters worse, users issuing ad hoc queries often do not know which tables are relevant or what the schema entails. To overcome these challenges and limit the number of tables presented to the LLM, we propose an approach that employs a multi-stage ranking mechanism for efficiency and mitigates context length issues by summarizing the selected tables. Additionally, we leverage a prompting tech-nique that facilitates Chain-of-Thought-style multi-table reasoning, allowing the LLM to interact pro-grammatically with the tables. Figure 1 shows an overview of our proposed framework, DBRAG. In the following, we begin by formally defining the setup, followed by descriptions of each component.

3.3 Multi-table Retrieval

First, an efficient encoder is used to perform coarse-grained ranking, identifying a broad subset of top-K tables from the corpus. 4 Experiments 

We evaluate our method and baselines focusing on two key tasks: multi-table retrieval and multi-table reasoning. 

Datasets. Existing tabular QA benchmarks have largely focused on either limited-domain scenar-ios or single-table contexts. To address this gap, MTQA (Pal et al., 2023) introduced a multi-table QA dataset derived from the cross-domain Spider dataset (Yu et al., 2018), which spans tables from 138 distinct domains. Their work adapts the origi-nal text-to-SQL pairs by executing the SQL queries to obtain tabular answers and excluding examples with errors or empty results. In our experiments, we also use the MTQA versions of GeoQuery (GeoQ; Zelle and Mooney, 1996) and ATIS (Price, 1990; Dahl et al., 1994), two smaller multi-table QA datasets built on databases of United States geography and flight booking systems, respectively. Appendix A details the methodology for construct-ing the database corpora and the queries. It also includes a summary of the key statistics of each dataset. 

Experimental Setup and Parameters. In our method, DBRAG, we utilize GPT-3.5-turbo 1 as the backbone LLM. For efficient dense retrieval, we employ OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small 2

as the encoder fenc . Specifically, we set the coarse-grained table budget to K = 50 and the fine-grained table budget to M = 5 , a design choice that balances precision and recall across diverse user queries. Unless otherwise specified, the number of Row Context examples added to the table sum-maries is set to L = 2 . Additionally, all the LLM-based experiments are run with a self-consistency parameter of 5, where we take the majority vote from five independent runs.

We evaluate several varia-tions of our method to analyze the contribution of each component. • DBRAG (Name and Schema): Modifies the fine-grained summary so that it includes only the table name and the table schema. • DBRAG (Random Rows): Modifies the fine-grained summary to include the table name, table schema, and random rows from the table. Specif-ically, we use Recall@5 as the metric to evaluate retrieval performance. Recall@5 measures the pro-portion of required tables retrieved among the top five candidates, averaged across queries. For a fair evaluation, we retrieve the top M = 5 tables from each method as the set of retrieved tables.

4.2 Multi-table Reasoning Performance 

Next, we turn our attention to evaluating the end-to-end performance of multi-table QA encompass-ing retrieval and reasoning capabilities. In the following, we use the set of retrieved tables Rq

obtained using DBRAG during the multi-table re-trieval phase for the three datasets, and feed them to several baselines. 

Baselines. We compare our method against the following baselines: • ReadTable (Chen et al., 2024b): This is a com-mon baseline that embeds the entire table in the prompt and asks the LLM to solve the problem using that data. However, if the table exceeds the model’s context length, the question is marked as a failure. • MTQA (Pal et al., 2023): A multi-table reason-ing baseline that pretrains the TAPEX model on a multi-table dataset containing SQL queries and tabular answers, and then fine-tunes it on natural language questions. 

Ablations. We compare our method with the fol-lowing variations: • DBRAG (Name and Schema): This setup re-stricts the table summaries to include only the table name and schema, without context rows. • DBRAG (w/o Selection): Our approach that per-forms program-aided reasoning without the table-selection step (Section 3.4). • DBRAG (Random Rows): Here, the relevant rows in the table summaries are replaced with random rows. 

Evaluation Metrics. The performance of multi-table reasoning is measured by comparing the ground truth answer table derived from the answer SQL query with the table generated by the model. Following Pal et al. (2023), we employ metrics that determine if the returned table’s features precisely match those of the ground truth answer. The evaluation metrics assess table similarity at different levels of granularity. Table EM measures whether the returned table exactly matches the tar-get table, requiring both row and column ordering to be identical; since many queries do not depend on ordering, additional metrics are used. Row EM 

assesses how many rows in the returned table ex-actly match those in the target table, ignoring row order but requiring exact value ordering within each row. Similarly, Column EM evaluates how many columns in the returned table match those in the target table, disregarding column order while still requiring exact value ordering within each column. Finally, Cell EM provides the most relaxed evalua-tion by measuring how many individual cells match exactly between the two tables. For Row EM, Column EM, and Cell EM, we report the macro versions of these metrics to miti-gate the bias introduced by queries returning large tables. Statistics about table size and distribution are provided in Appendix A.

4.3 Varying Table and Row Context Budget 

Finally, we evaluate the impact of table and row context budgets on both table retrieval and reason-ing performance on the Spider dataset. In the re-trieval setup (Fig. 2(a)), we vary the coarse-grained ranking budget K, which determines the number of retrieved tables provided to the LLM for fine-grained reranking. Our results indicate that increas-ing K generally improves retrieval performance for both approaches, and importantly, the setup us-ing relevant row context mostly outperforms that using random row context. For the reasoning setup (Fig. 2(b)), we vary the number of rows L included in the table summary. The experiment reveals that while increasing L initially enhances answer accu-racy, performance reaches a plateau beyond which additional rows do not consistently improve per-formance. This aligns with the intuition that only a few rows are needed to grasp the structure and semantics of tabular information. These findings underscore the effectiveness of compact table sum-maries in the evaluated setting.

5 Conclusions 

We propose DBRAG, a retrieval-augmented gener-ation framework tailored for multi-table question answering. DBRAG overcomes the challenges of complex multi-table relationships by decomposing the task into efficient table retrieval and multi-table reasoning. Our approach leverages a two-stage retrieval strategy, first retrieving candidate tables from the Table Index and then reranking them using query-relevant row context, followed by multi-table chain-of-thought prompting and program-aided ex-ecution for precise reasoning. Extensive experi-ments on multi-table QA datasets demonstrate im-provements in both table retrieval and end-to-end QA performance. Our results validate DBRAG as an effective solution for complex, multi-table database question answering. Future work will explore further optimizations and extend support for heterogeneous data integration such as semi-structured data and knowledge graphs. 86 Limitations 

Building and maintaining the Row Index can be computationally expensive for databases with bil-lions of records. More efficient index construction and maintenance strategies, including sampling approaches potentially drawing on the frequency-based cell selection used by Chen et al. (2024b), could help address these challenges. We leave these issues, along with dynamic schema updates and production deployment, for future work. While the queries in the datasets involve multiple tables, their answers can be obtained by executing SQL queries. In real-world scenarios, however, users may pose more complex queries, such as queries for open-ended exploration or interpreta-tion of trends in the data, which may require analyt-ical reasoning beyond SQL-based operations (Ma et al., 2023). The capability of DBRAG to handle such advanced queries remains unexplored in this paper. Additionally, real-world large databases often present challenges such as noisy data, inconsisten-cies, and missing information (Hai et al., 2023). Our method has not been evaluated in these set-tings, where the model would need to navigate and address potential flaws in the data itself. Finally, our comparisons cover a limited set of baselines for multi-table retrieval and reasoning. We do not include comparisons with advanced solvers which could provide further insights into the performance of our approach.

B Evaluation Metrics Details 

This section complements the reasoning metrics described in Section 4.2. For Row EM, Column EM, and Cell EM, precision is the fraction of fea-tures (rows, columns, or cells) in the returned table that exactly match a feature in the target table, and 

recall is the fraction of features in the target table that are exactly matched in the returned table; the F1-score is their harmonic mean. Pal et al. (2023) compute these scores by pooling features across all returned and target tables in the test set (i.e., micro-averaging), so a few queries with very large returned or target tables can dominate the results. We instead compute precision and recall for each query, average them across queries (i.e., macro-averaging) so that every query contributes equally, and report F1 as the harmonic mean of the macro-averaged precision and recall. 11 C DBRAG Prompts
