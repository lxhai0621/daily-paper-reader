ABSTRACT 19 

Fragmented metadata in spatial omics archives has rendered large volumes of 20 

multimodal molecular –histological data inaccessible as ‘dark data’. Here, we 21 

introduce SpatialDataAgent, an agentic workflow for autonomous spatial omics data 22 

curation, combining schema -constrained evidence evaluation with a self -refining 23 

standardization agent. Applied to a decade of GEO records, SpatialDataAgent 24 

identified 769 paired H&E –spatial transcriptomics (ST) datasets, representing a 6.4 -25 

fold scale expansion over existing manually curated baselines. Within the 26 

benchmarking window, the framework achieved a 141% increase in high -confidence 27 

Class A paired datasets. We further assembled a recent high -confidence subset into 28 

HESRT, a standardized datalake containing 29.2 million spots/cells, establishing a 29 

blueprint for evidence -grounded autonomous curation of multimodal biomedical 30 

archives .31

Integrating spatial omics data with their paired histological images (e.g., Hematoxylin and Eosin, 33 

H&E) provides a comprehensive view of tissue architecture and is fundamental to training next -34 

generation spatial foundation models 1–6. Although public archives such as Gene Expression 35 

Omnibus (GEO) contain thousands of spatial datasets, severe metadata fragmentation has 36 

rendered the majority of these multimodal pairs inaccessible as ‘dark data’, i.e., datasets that 37 

nominally exist in public archives but lack the standardized metadata necessary for 38 

computational reuse. This challenge is widely recognized across biomedical domains 7,8 . While 39 

agentic AI has recently been deployed to automate downstream spatial biology analysis and 40 

hypothesis generation (e.g., SpatialAgent 9), the field still lacks autonomous frameworks for 41 

upstream data readiness. Given the exponential growth of the data volume (doubling roughly 42 

every 14.4 months, as shown in Extended Data Fig. 1), exhaustive manual curation can no longer 43 

scale to produce analysis -ready repositories (Fig. 1a, top). Here, we introduce SpatialDataAgent, 44 

an agentic workflow that integrates autonomous dataset identification, evidence grading, and 45 

physical alignment under an intent -driven framework. By exploring the GEO records across 10 46 

years with SpatialDataAgent, we generated HESRT (H&E Spatially Resolved Transcriptomics), 47 

a standardized multimodal datalake comprising 29.2 million spots (Extended Data Fig. 2). The 48 

SpatialDataAgent -recovered cohort expands upon existing manually curated counterparts, 49 

delivering a 141% increase in dataset coverage across sequencing techniques. (Visium, Visium 50 

HD, Xenium, CosMx, BMKMANU). 51 

Traditional curation workflows 10 –12 require researchers to manually navigate web portals and 52 

nested repository files to verify the co -existence of omics and histology data (Fig. 1a, right). This 53 

process is critical, given standard keyword -based scraping frequently conflates true spatial 54 

datasets with single -cell RNA -seq or multiplexed immunofluorescence assays. While recent 55 

agentic systems have achieved progress in autonomously curating one -dimensional sequence 56 

repositories via global metadata scanning (e.g., the SRAgent framework powering 57 

scBaseCount 13 ), they face limitations when confronted with multimodal spatial datasets. They 58 

often lack the parsing capabilities to extract critical imaging metadata from nested, unstructured 59 

attachments, and cannot enforce the strict cross -assay isolation required to prevent systemic 60 

image -to -matrix misalignments in complex, multi -arm studies. SpatialDataAgent addresses these 61 

limitations through a hierarchical triage funnel (Fig. 1a, bottom right; Extended Data Fig. 3) that 62 

operationalizes evidence -grounded curation. Following keyword -based filtering and initial large 63 

language model (LLM) prescreening, the framework executes a strict evidence -grading stage 64 

(Fig. 2a, left; Extended Data Fig. 4a) to classify each dataset into three evidence -grounded tiers: 65 

Class A (high -confidence H&E -ST pairing), Class B (probable), and Class C (rejected). 66 

Rather than relying on superficial metadata, the system integrates multi -source archival evidence 67 

and operates under a strict Zero -Inference Policy (i.e., evidence -grounded classification that 68 

permits judgments solely on explicit, traceable textual evidence), featuring a Cross -Assay 69 

Isolation protocol. This architectural design is intended to prevent the false validation of 70 

transcriptomic data using histological evidence from unrelated experimental arms. Such 71 

evidence -grounded and schema -constrained approaches have been increasingly adopted in recent 72 

agentic systems for scientific data handling 14,15 . A quantitative failure mode analysis confirms 73 

the rigor of this policy, with the Evidence Reviewer assigning 577 candidates to Class C, 74

Dataset identification alone does not guarantee its usability. Public ST data suffer from severe 103 

structural heterogeneity (Fig. 2b) and undocumented affine transformations, frequently resulting 104 

in misaligned histopathological whole slide images (Fig. 2c; Extended Data Fig. 7). To bridge 105 

this gap autonomously, we engineered the Evolutionary Standardizer (Fig. 1b; Fig. 2a, right), a 106 

self -refining agentic standardization module 17,18 . Instead of relying on static parsing scripts, the 107 

agent iteratively infers dataset -specific spatial -to -image transformations using structural and 108 

alignment feedback. Alignment quality is assessed via an Objective Scorer that quantifies the 109 

consistency between transcriptomic coordinates and histological tissue regions. Validated 110 

through synthetic perturbation experiments, this scorer flags physical alignment discrepancies 111 

(ROC AUC = 0.82; Extended Data Fig. 6a, b). This optimization loop executes iteratively until 112 

the dataset satisfies a predefined Global Alignment Score threshold, yielding a standardized, co -113 

registered data bundle (Fig. 1b, bottom right; Fig. 2d). 114

We deployed this standardizer on a recent subset of 69 Class A datasets to generate the HESRT 115 

datalake, aligning 745 tissue samples with an empirical success rate of >95%. Per -sample 116 

technical quality metrics confirmed robust standardization across capture spots, molecular 117 

sensitivity, and imaging precision (Extended Data Fig. 9). A cross -datalake quality comparison 118 

reveals that HESRT provides superior data density. At the sample level, the agent -standardized 119 

HESRT data exhibits a median spot capacity 2.4 -fold higher than the combined STimage -1K4M 120 

and HEST -1k 19 (a large -scale, high -quality Visium benchmark of 1,229 H&E -ST profiles) 121 

datasets, while maintaining comparable molecular depth (median UMI per spot = 4k in HESRT 122 

vs 5k in combined manual sets; a 0.8 -fold ratio; see Fig. 2e). Notably, HESRT delivers 2.1 times 123 

more ultra -high -resolution samples and 1.8 times the total histological information volume 124 

compared to existing repositories (Extended Data Fig. 10). The framework demonstrated broad 125 

tissue and organism coverage (Extended Data Fig. 2a) and successfully processed emerging, 126 

ultra -high -resolution platforms including Visium HD and Xenium. The generation of this 127 

specialized datalake supports the feasibility of agentic standardization for complex, real -world 128 

spatial omics archives. 129 

We also noted several limitations of this framework. First, SpatialDataAgent’s recall is 130 

constrained by the scope of its initial keyword filtering strategy. For example, GSE158730, the 131 

only data in STimage -1K4M but missed by SpatialDataAgent, lacked canonical ST terms within 132 

its GSE -level metadata, rendering it undetectable to keyword -based filters. Incorporating sample -133 

level (GSM) scanning may recover such entries in future iterations. Second, while the Evidence 134 

Grading achieves high precision, manual verification of the 170 uniquely identified datasets 135 

identified by SpatialDataAgent confirmed 85.0% as true H&E -ST paired datasets (17/20), 136 

indicating that a subset of ‘dark data’ remains challenging to resolve from textual evidence 137 

alone. Finally, while our heuristic Objective Scorer (Spillover Rate) is effective for standardizing 138 

the majority of datasets, it is a pragmatic approximation. The 2D mask -based heuristic remains 139 

vulnerable to complex geometric anomalies, showing limited robustness against severe shear 140 

deformations, undocumented non -orthogonal coordinate rotations, or perfectly symmetric tissue 141 

samples (where mirror inversions may bypass background detection). To achieve a more 142 

generalized and robust visual quality inspection, we explored utilizing frontier vision -language 143 

models (VLMs) 20 . However, these models exhibited high hallucination rates when reasoning 144 

over complex histopathological overlays, yielding an effective filtration rate of <20%. This 145 

observation is consistent with documented challenges of frontier VLMs when applied to 146 

specialized scientific and pathological imaging tasks 21 –23 . Fully robust, vision -based autonomous 147 

quality control therefore remains an open challenge for the field. 148 

SpatialDataAgent demonstrates that autonomous, evidence -grounded agentic reasoning can 149 

perform robustly compared to manual curation in both scale and recall, establishing a practical 150 

framework for transitioning biocuration from static rule -based scripting to autonomous data 151 

recovery. Beyond its immediate application to broad H&E -ST pairing, the architecture of 152 

SpatialDataAgent is designed to be adaptable across related biomedical data -curation tasks (Fig. 153 

1c). Because the framework is intent -driven, its target specification is externalized into the 154 

research intent, search strategy, and judging schema rather than hard -coded into the pipeline, 155 

allowing SpatialDataAgent to be adapted to the identification, verification, and harmonization of 156

METHODS 164 

1. Overview of the SpatialDataAgent framework 165 

SpatialDataAgent is an intent -driven agentic framework designed to recover and harmonize 166 

multimodal spatial omics data from public archives. The framework contains two operational 167 

modules. The first is a hierarchical retrieval -and -triage module that identifies candidate public 168 

records and assigns evidence -grounded dataset classes. The second is a self -refining 169 

standardization module that resolves dataset -specific spatial -to -image registration discrepancies 170 

and produces co -registered spatial transcriptomics (ST) matrices and histological images. 171 

The workflow begins with a user -specified research intent, which is translated into a search 172 

strategy and evidence -evaluation criteria. Candidate records are retrieved from public repository 173 

metadata, routed through sequential filtering and evidence review stages, and then classified 174 

according to the explicit strength of evidence for physical H&E –ST pairing. High -confidence 175 

datasets are subsequently passed to the standardization module, where spatial coordinate systems 176 

and histology images are harmonized under automated quality control. 177 

2. Data source and candidate retrieval 178 

The production -scale retrieval scan was performed on GEO Series records deposited between 179 

2016 and 2026. For each record, the retrieval module considered repository -level metadata 180 

including series titles, summaries, organism annotations, sample titles, and supplementary -file 181 

descriptors when available. The initial retrieval strategy was designed for high recall rather than 182 

high precision, because the goal of the first stage was to capture potentially relevant spatial 183 

omics records while allowing downstream evidence review to remove false positives. 184 

Candidate records were retrieved using broad spatial -omics and platform -related terms, together 185 

with contextual transcriptomic and histology -pairing signals. These signals included core spatial 186 

transcriptomics platform names, general spatial gene -expression terminology, and metadata 187 

patterns suggestive of paired histological imaging. Records passing the initial relevance screen 188 

were retained for LLM -assisted prescreening. 189 

The retrieval stage reduced 216,510 GEO records to 3,771 candidate records for further 190 

evaluation. 191

3. LLM -assisted prescreening 192 

Candidate records from the retrieval stage were evaluated by an LLM -assisted prescreening 193 

module. The input to this stage consisted of concise repository metadata, including the series 194 

accession, title, description, and supplementary -file descriptors. The prescreening task was 195 

intentionally lenient: each candidate was assigned a preliminary verdict of likely relevant, 196 

uncertain, or unlikely relevant. 197 

Candidates judged likely relevant or uncertain were retained for detailed evidence review, 198 

whereas candidates judged unlikely relevant were excluded from subsequent high -precision 199 

classification. This prescreening stage retained 1,808 candidates for evidence grading, including 200 

990 likely relevant candidates and 818 uncertain candidates. 201 

4. Evidence bundle construction 202 

For each candidate retained after prescreening, SpatialDataAgent constructed a multi -source 203 

evidence bundle. The evidence bundle integrated repository -level series metadata, available 204 

sample -level metadata, supplementary -file descriptors, and linked publication evidence when 205 

available. Linked publications included abstracts or summary evidence from PubMed, PMC, 206 

preprint servers, or other repository -associated publication identifiers. 207 

The purpose of the evidence bundle was to aggregate the explicit textual and file -level evidence 208 

needed to determine whether a spatial transcriptomics assay and histological image were 209 

physically paired within the same experimental arm. Evidence from different experimental arms 210 

within the same repository record was treated separately to avoid cross -assay contamination. 211 

5. Evidence grading and dataset classes 212 

The final evidence -grading stage assigned each candidate to one of three classes. 213 

Class A denotes high -confidence physical H&E –ST pairing. A Class A assignment required 214 

explicit evidence that spatial transcriptomics data and histological imaging were paired, co -215 

registered, or otherwise physically linked within the same sample or experimental arm. 216 

Class B denotes probable pairing. Class B was assigned when evidence supported spatial omics 217 

with morphological or imaging context but lacked sufficient explicit evidence for high -218 

confidence H&E –ST pairing. 219 

Class C denotes rejection. Class C was assigned when the record represented a non -spatial assay, 220 

lacked histological evidence, contained only unrelated imaging information, or failed cross -assay 221 

consistency checks. 222 

To reduce false -positive classifications, evidence grading was governed by a Zero -Inference 223 

Policy. Under this policy, positive classifications could only be made from explicit, traceable 224 

evidence rather than implicit assumptions. A Cross -Assay Isolation Protocol was also applied: 225

6. Statistical analysis of triage consistency 230 

To evaluate consistency across the triage pipeline, we compared prescreening decisions with 231 

final evidence -graded classes. A chi -square test of independence was applied to the contingency 232 

matrix of prescreening categories versus final Class A/B/C outcomes. In addition, an odds ratio 233 

was calculated to quantify whether candidates prescreened as likely relevant were more likely to 234 

be validated as Class A rather than rejected as Class C, compared with candidates prescreened as 235 

uncertain. 236 

Candidates designated as likely relevant during prescreening showed a 17.2 -fold higher odds of 237 

final validation as Class A rather than rejection as Class C compared with uncertain candidates. 238 

The chi -square test confirmed strong dependence between prescreening and final evidence 239 

grading. 240

temporal window used by the reference catalog. Recall was calculated by comparing recovered 244 

GEO Series accessions against the reference accessions. 245 

Within the benchmarking window, SpatialDataAgent recovered 119 of 120 reference datasets 246

8. Evolutionary standardization 260 

Dataset identification alone does not ensure downstream usability, because public spatial omics 261 

records frequently contain heterogeneous coordinate formats, non -standard directory structures, 262 

missing registration metadata, and inconsistent image resolutions. To address this problem, we 263 

applied the Evolutionary Standardizer to a recent high -confidence subset of Class A datasets. 264 

The standardization module takes as input raw repository files containing spatial expression 265 

matrices, spatial coordinate information, histological image files, and associated metadata when 266 

available. The target output is a standardized co -registered bundle containing a harmonized 267 

spatial expression matrix, an aligned histological image, and quality -control evidence 268 

documenting successful registration. 269 

The standardization module operates as an iterative self -refinement process. At each iteration, 270 

the module inspects the available file structure and metadata, infers a candidate spatial -to -image 271 

transformation, generates a standardized output, and submits the output to objective alignment 272 

quality control. Outputs failing quality control are returned for additional refinement. Outputs 273 

passing the quality criterion are compiled into standardized co -registered data bundles. 274 

This standardization process was applied to a recent subset of 69 Class A datasets, yielding 745 275 

processable tissue samples in HESRT. 276 

9. Objective alignment quality control 277 

Standardized outputs were evaluated using an Objective Scorer designed to quantify physical 278 

consistency between transcriptomic coordinates and histological tissue regions. The scorer 279 

assessed whether spatial coordinates projected to plausible tissue -containing regions of the 280 

histological image and flagged outputs with gross physical misalignment. 281 

To validate the robustness of this quality -control strategy, we performed synthetic perturbation 282 

experiments on manually verified aligned samples. Perturbations represented common classes of 283 

real -world spatial registration failure, including translation, rotation, scaling error, mirroring, and 284 

coordinate disruption. Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to evaluate whether 285 

the Objective Scorer could distinguish aligned samples from perturbed negative controls. The 286 

scorer achieved an ROC AUC of 0.82 in this validation setting. 287 

10. Cross -datalake quality comparison 288 

The standardized HESRT datalake was compared with existing manually curated H&E –ST 289 

resources, including STimage -1K4M and HEST -1k. Comparisons were performed at the sample 290 

level using three major dimensions: spot capacity, molecular depth, and histological image 291 

resolution. Spot capacity was measured as the number of transcriptomic observations per sample. 292 

Molecular depth was measured using median UMI counts per spot. Histological image resolution 293 

was quantified by total image pixel count. 294
