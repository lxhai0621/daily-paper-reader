Abstract 

Knowledge graphs store large numbers of relations efficiently, but they remain weak at 

representing a quieter difficulty: the meaning of a concept often shifts with the domain in 

which it is used. A triple such as ⟨Apple, instance-of, Company ⟩ may be acceptable in one 

setting while being misleading or unusable in another. In most current systems, domain 

information is attached as metadata, qualifiers, or graph-level organization. These 

mechanisms help with filtering and provenance, but they usually do not alter the formal status 

of the assertion itself. 

This paper argues that domain should be treated as part of knowledge representation rather 

than as supplementary annotation. It introduces the Domain-Contextualized Concept Graph 

(CDC), a framework in which domain is written into the relation and interpreted as a modal 

world constraint. In the CDC form ⟨C, R@D, C' ⟩, the marker @D identifies the world in 

which the relation is licensed to hold. Formally, the relation is interpreted through a domain-

indexed necessity operator, □_D, so that truth, inference, and conflict checking are all scoped 

to the relevant world. 

This move has three immediate consequences. First, ambiguous concepts can be 

disambiguated at the point of representation rather than deferred to later processing. Second, 

invalid or ill-formed assertions can be challenged against the domain they invoke. Third, 

structurally comparable relations across domains can be connected through explicit cross-

domain predicates rather than ad hoc alignment alone. The paper develops this claim through 

a Kripke-style semantics, a compact predicate system, a Prolog reference implementation, 

and mappings to RDF stores, OWL ontologies, and relational databases. 

The contribution of the paper is therefore not another context tag, but a representational 

reinterpretation of domain itself. The central claim is that many practical failures in 

knowledge systems begin when domain is treated as external to the assertion. CDC addresses 

that problem by giving domain a structural and computable role inside the representation. 

Keywords: Knowledge representation, Modal constraints, Context-aware reasoning, 

Cognitive modeling, Logic programming. 1.1 The Problem: three fields, one unresolved assumption 

Knowledge graphs have transformed the storage and retrieval of structured information 

(Hogan et al., 2021). Systems such as Google's Knowledge Graph, DBpedia (Auer et al., 

2007), and Wikidata (Vrande čić & Kr ötzsch, 2014) show the practical power of triples and 

graph-based schemas at scale. Yet the efficiency of the triple model conceals a persistent 

weakness: a triple says nothing about the world in which it is supposed to be interpreted. 

When DBpedia records (Apple, instance-of, Company), a human reader may infer the 

intended domain without difficulty. The formal structure cannot enforce that reading, cannot 

determine when Apple should be treated as a fruit, and cannot prevent one reading from 

contaminating another. This is not missing information, it is missing structure. The 

representation does not encode the conditions under which meaning holds. 

The same difficulty appears wherever concepts cross domains. A researcher comparing 

artificial neural networks with biological neural systems encounters a representational gap: in 

one ontology a neural network is an algorithmic model, in another the brain is a biological 

organ. The structural relation may be intellectually productive, but the knowledge graph does 

not know how to preserve it without manual alignment (Euzenat & Shvaiko, 2013). Historical 

concepts create the same pressure. Dalton, Thomson, Bohr, and contemporary physics do not 

simply add detail to one unchanged object; they reframe what "atom" means. Standard 

ontologies address this by replacing definitions or splitting the concept into separate entities 

such as Atom_Dalton and Atom_Bohr (Flouris et al., 2008). What is lost is the ability to 

represent continuity and difference together. 

The problem extends beyond knowledge graphs. OWL and description logics adopt an open-

world assumption with unary semantics: a class assertion holds globally, with no native 

construct for expressing that an assertion is valid only within a particular context (Baader et 

al., 2007). This is not a deficiency of OWL, it is its design premise: serve ontological 

consistency and decidable reasoning, not context-sensitive knowledge. But in practice, it 

becomes a hard constraint against alignment with complex domains such as medicine, 

engineering, and law. The limitation has motivated two decades of extensions :

(1)contextualized ontologies (Bozzato et al., 2018), modular ontologies (Grau et al., 2008), 

named graphs (2) none of which makes context a structural parameter of the inference unit 

itself. 

ICD-11, the WHO's global standard for health information (~85,000 entities), manifests the 

same problem at industrial scale. A single disease must be classified simultaneously along 

anatomical, etiological, pathological, and functional axes. The WHO's own technical 

documentation acknowledges that the ontology layer "was not completed and remains an 

opportunity for future development" (Chute et al., 2022). The root cause: OWL's subClassOf 

relation does not distinguish why an entity belongs to a parent class. Streptococcal 

Pneumonia is simultaneously a respiratory disease (anatomical axis), a bacterial infection 

(etiological axis), and a streptococcal disease (pathogen axis). But the formalism treats these 

as three undifferentiated parent-class memberships. Multiple inheritance becomes ambiguous 

polyhierarchy, with no mechanism to scope classification to a specific axis. These are not three separate problems. They are the same problem in three settings. 

1.2 The shared root cause 

Knowledge graphs, OWL ontologies, and medical classification systems share an assumption 

so foundational it has never been explicitly stated: inference relations are global. The relation 

is_a has one meaning, everywhere. The domain in which an assertion holds — the semantic 

world that licenses the relation — lives outside the representation: in query-language 

WHERE clauses, in application code, in the human user's head. The inference engine does 

not know which world it is operating in. 

A common response is to attach more context: qualifiers, metadata, named graphs, 

provenance records. These devices are useful — they improve documentation, support 

filtering, and help organize data. But in most systems the core assertion remains formally 

untouched. The triple still stands as if it were globally valid, while the domain appears 

alongside it as supplementary explanation. 

The distinction this paper develops is therefore the distinction between a label and a

constraint. A label indicates how an assertion is intended to be read. A constraint determines 

whether the assertion is licensed to stand at all. Domain, we argue, belongs to the second 

category. If domain is built into the relation as a structural constraint — as part of the 

predicate arity, not as external annotation — then it becomes part of what the representation 

means and part of how subsequent inference must proceed. The system cannot ignore it, just 

as a function call cannot ignore one of its arguments. 

1.3 Theoretical foundations 

CDC is grounded in two complementary traditions. 

Cognitive-linguistic foundation. Humans do not store concepts as universal definitions; 

instead, we understand them through domain-dependent frames (Fillmore, 1982): "bank" in 

finance activates a frame of money, accounts, and transactions, while "bank" in geography 

activates a frame of rivers, erosion, and terrain. This is not lexical ambiguity; it is how 

cognition works (Barsalou, 1982; Gärdenfors, 2000). CDC operationalizes this through a

three-level isomorphic mapping between cognitive frames, linguistic context markers, and 

computational domain specifications (Goldberg, 2006; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980). 

Modal logic foundation. Since Kripke (1963), the insight that propositions can hold relative 

to possible worlds has been central to logic. The necessity operator □ asserts that a

proposition holds in all accessible worlds. McCarthy (1993) proposed formaliz ing contexts as 

first-class logical objects. Fagin et al. (2004) demonstrated that modal frameworks can 

capture epistemic states in multi-agent systems. But within KR, these modal approaches have 

remained theoretical; no prior work has embedded modal world constraints directly into the 

representational layer of a knowledge graph as computable structural elements. CDC fills this 

gap. 

1.4 Contributions This paper makes four contributions, each demonstrated through concrete examples before 

being formalized: 

Modal constraint interpretation. We show that CDC's domain annotation @D functions as a

modal necessity operator □_D — not merely analogously, but with formal consequences for 

what can be asserted, inferred, and rejected within a knowledge representation. 

Representation-level power. We demonstrate that @D enables capabilities at the 

representational layer that traditional KR cannot achieve: falsification of ill-formed assertions, 

a priori disambiguation, cross-domain structural transfer, and multi-path constraint locking. 

Constraint transferability. We formalize the property that any computational system 

consuming CDC representations automatically inherits the semantic constraints of @D —

making the representation an active constraint interface rather than passive data. 

Computational validation. We provide a Prolog reference implementation, side-by-side query 

comparison with RDF and Wikidata demonstrating CDC's structural advantages, and case 

studies — including ICD-11 respiratory diseases — validating the framework's computability 

and practical value. 

This paper focuses on the representational foundations of CDC: what @D means, why it 

constitutes a constraint rather than a label, and what representational properties follow from 

this interpretation. Questions of computational complexity, decidability guarantees, and 

optimized inference algorithms over CDC structures — while important — constitute a

distinct line of investigation that builds upon the representational semantics established here. 

2. Related Work 

2.1 Knowledge graph systems 

DBpedia (Auer et al., 2007) and Freebase (Bollacker et al., 2008) established the paradigm of 

fixed ontological schemas. Wikidata (Vrande čić & Kr ötzsch, 2014) introduced qualifiers —

temporal or situational annotations attached to statements. But qualifiers do not change the 

logical status of assertions; they are metadata about statements, not structural 

constraints within them. Schema.org (Guha et al., 2016) provides web-optimized structured 

vocabulary but is designed for search-engine interoperability, not general-purpose knowledge 

representation. 

2.2 Ontology engineering 

Upper ontologies such as SUMO (Niles & Pease, 2001), Cyc (Lenat, 1995), and BFO (Arp et 

al., 2015) seek universal categories applicable across all domains. They provide formal rigor 

but sacrifice adaptability: when a concept must mean different things in different disciplines, 

universal categories become a straitjacket. Domain-specific ontologies, such as SNOMED 

CT and Gene Ontology (Smith et al., 2007), deliver rich internal structure but resist cross-disciplinary integration. CDC positions itself as a cross-domain integration layer. It does not 

replace domain ontologies, but connects them through explicit modal constraints. 

2.3 Cognitive science foundations 

Gärdenfors' conceptual spaces (2000) model concepts as regions in multidimensional quality 

spaces. Fillmore's frame semantics (1982) proposes that meanings are interpreted relative to 

conceptual frames. Barsalou (1982) and Medin & Shoben (1988) demonstrate that human 

cognition systematically adapts categorization to situational context. CDC operationalizes 

these insights: each domain specification defines a cognitive frame within which concepts 

acquire specific meanings. 

2.4 Logic-based representation 

Description Logic (DL) provides the semantic foundation of OWL ontologies (Baader et al., 

2003; Horrocks et al., 2003) and emphasizes decidable reasoning under well-defined 

constraints. CDC ’s □_D operator is formally compatible with DL ’s existing modal ext ensions. 

Epistemic DLs already incorporate modal operators for knowledge and belief, such as the 

epistemic operator K in DL-Lite, and temporal DLs use modal operators for time-indexed 

assertions. CDC ’s contribution is not a new logical formalism but a specific 

operationalization that treats the domain specification as a Kripke-style world index within an 

otherwise standard relational structure. A CDC assertion is_a(C, C’, D) can be read as a DL 

axiom C ⊑_D C’, a subsumption that holds within world D. This compatibility means CDC 

does not require abandoning DL-based infrastructure; it extends that infrastructure with 

explicit world indexing. 

RDF named graphs (Carroll et al., 2005) extend triples to quads for provenance tracking and 

statement grouping. RDF-star, in the forthcoming RDF 1.2, enables annotations on individual 

triples through quoted triple syntax. RDF-star can in fact represent the same surface structure 

as CDC, namely a triple annotated with a domain specification. The distinction is semantic 

rather than syntactic: RDF-star annotations carry no formal semantics that participate in 

inference. Transitive closure, attribute inheritanc e, and prerequisite chains do not 

automatically respect quoted-triple annotations unless each inference rule is manually 

extended to check them. CDC ’s @D carries modal necessity semantics, □_D, that structurally 

constrain reasoning. The same notation, with out the modal interpretation, is a label; with it, it 

is a constraint. CDC treats domains as semantic worlds that directly participate in logical 

inference, rather than as containers or annotations. 

2.5 Modal logic in knowledge representation 

Kripke's possible-world semantics (1963) provides the framework for necessity and 

possibility relative to accessible worlds. McCarthy (1993) proposed contexts as formal 

objects. Situation calculus (McCarthy & Hayes, 1969) models how facts change across 

situations. Fagin et al. (2004) demonstrated modal reasoning about knowledge in multi-agent 

systems. 

These approaches share CDC's concern with context-dependent truth. But they have 

remained theoretical tools which is used to analyze knowledge representations, not 

to constitute them. Description Logics incorporated limited modal features (temporal 

extensions), but no prior work has made modal world constraints a first-class, computable component of the knowledge graph representation itself. This is CDC's distinctive 

contribution: @D is not a theoretical analysis tool applied to representations — it is part 

of the representation.

2.6 Context-aware knowledge representation 

The absence of a formal solution has not prevented deployment. It has produced a catalog of 

workarounds, each addressing a symptom while leaving the root cause intact. ICD-11's 

Foundation Layer permits multiple parent classes without distinguishing which classification 

axis each parent represents — users are expected to filter manually. Postcoordination encodes 

multi-axis classification as string concatenation of base codes and qualifier codes, producing 

combinatorial complexity that users must navigate by memorizing coding rules. Hospital 

EMR systems hard-code axis logic in application layers — "respiratory department queries 

return only anatomical classifications" — producing solutions that work but cannot be reused, 

adapted, or maintained when classification axes change. SWRL rules patch OWL reasoning 

with external logic, coupling tightly to the ontology version and becoming unmaintainable at 

scale. And in many cases, the multi-axis semantics are simply written in natural language 

documentation: human-readable, machine-opaque. 

These workarounds share a pattern. Each moves the domain information further from the 

inference engine: from the representation (where it could constrain reasoning) to application 

code, to coding conventions, to documentation, to the clinician's memory. The result is that knowledge systems operate globally while humans perform domain scoping manually —

precisely the separation this paper addresses. 

3. The CDC structure 

3.1 Formal definition 

Definition 1 (CDC Triple). A CDC triple is a four-tuple: 

τ = ⟨c, r, c', d⟩

where c ∈ C (source concept), r ∈ R (relation predicate), c' ∈ C (target concept), and d ∈ D

(domain specification). 

Notation: c —{r@d} → c' , or equivalently r(c, c', d). 

Definition 2 (Domain Specification). A domain specification d ∈ D is a structured string: 

d := dimension | dimension@d 

Examples: 'Physics', 'Physics@Quantum_Mechanics', 'HighSchool@Math@Calculus', 'Stude 

nt_Zhang@Grade10'. 

Critical property: Domains are defined on demand, not from a fixed taxonomy.

Design principles: Specificity (fine-grained enough to disambiguate, not so fine as to

natural), Context-Sensitivity (capture the most relevant distinguishing context). 

3.3 Representation in practice 

CDC allows divergent categorizations to coexist without contradiction: 

is_a(Apple, Fruit, 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy'). is_a(Apple, Company, 'Business@Technology_Industry'). 

is_a(Neural_Network, Computational_Model, 'CS@ML'). 

analogous_to(Neural_Network, Biological_Brain, 'CS@ML', 'Biology@Neuroscience'). 

is_a(Neural_Network, Function_Approximator, 'Math@Optimization'). 

is_a(Neural_Network, Philosophy_Topic, 'Philosophy@Mind'). 

This is the surface structure. The next section reveals what makes it powerful. 

3.4 Migration path: From existing representations to CDC 

CDC does not require rebuilding knowledge from scratch. Any existing knowledge 

representation — whether a traditional knowledge graph (RDF triples), an OWL ontology, a

relational database, or a structured document — can be migrated to CDC through a

straightforward transformation:  

> 

Existing entities become concepts C 

> 

Existing relations become relation predicates r 

> 

Existing namespaces, categories, or contextual tags become domain specifications D

The mapping is direct: 

Traditional triple: ⟨Apple, instance-of, Company ⟩

CDC triple: ⟨Apple, is_a, Company, 'Business@Technology_Industry' ⟩

Hierarchical domains (e.g., 'Physics@Quantum_Mechanics') can be constructed from 

existing taxonomy structures, category hierarchies, or namespace prefixes. Cross-domain 

relations like analogous_to can be added incrementally without disrupting existing data. 

This migration path means CDC is not a replacement that requires discarding legacy 

knowledge — it is an augmentation layer that wraps existing representations with modal 

world constraints. An RDF triple store can be converted to CDC by mapping named graphs to 

domain specifications. An OWL ontology can be wrapped by treating its namespace as a

domain. A relational database can gain CDC semantics by adding a single domain column to 

its fact tables. 

The cost of adoption is therefore not "rebuild your knowledge base" but "add one field to 

your existing data and gain modal constraint semantics." This low-invasive upgrade path is 

a deliberate design property: CDC is meant to be layered on top of existing infrastructure, not 

substituted for it. No existing triples are modified or discarded; no schemas are restructured; 

no queries are invalidated. The only change is the addition of a domain parameter — and any 

system that ignores this parameter simply recovers the original, unscoped behavior. CDC is 

therefore backward-compatible by construction: the worst case of ignoring @D is exactly the 

status quo. 

4. The power of @D: Domain as modal world constraint 

This is the core of the paper. We do not merely define CDC's modal interpretation —

we demonstrate its power through concrete examples, then formalize what makes that power 

possible. 4.1 @D Is a Constraint, Not a Label 

In standard Kripke semantics (Kripke, 1963; Chellas, 1980), a modal frame is a tuple ⟨W, 

R_acc ⟩ where W is a set of possible worlds and R_acc is an accessibility relation. The 

necessity operator □ asserts that a proposition holds in all accessible worlds. 

CDC operationalizes this framework: 

Definition 3 (Modal Domain Interpretation). Each domain specification d ∈ D defines a

possible world w_d ∈ W. The CDC assertion 

R@D(C, C') ≡ □_D R(C, C') 

states that relation R between C and C' necessarily holds within the world defined by domain 

D.

This is not a notational convenience. It has teeth. Consider what happens when a relation 

violates the laws of its declared world: 

Demonstration: representation-level falsification 

Suppose someone asserts: 

causes(Thunder, Dark_Clouds, 'Meteorology') 

In a traditional knowledge graph, this triple is syntactically valid and will be stored without 

objection. No structural mechanism prevents the encoding of a reversed causal claim. 

In CDC, the @Meteorology annotation is not a passive tag — it is □_Meteorology, a

necessity operator that imports the constraints of the meteorological world. Within 

□_Meteorology, the established causal chain is: 

Moisture_Accumulation —{causes@Meteorology} → Cloud_Formation —

{causes@Meteorology} → Charge_Separation —{causes@Meteorology} → Lightning —

{accompanies@Meteorology} → Thunder 

The proposed assertion reverses this chain. Under □_Meteorology, the causal direction from 

Thunder to Dark_Clouds is inconsistent with the world's constraint structure. The assertion 

is rejected — not by a separate rule engine, not by a human reviewer, but by the modal 

semantics of the representation itself. 

This is the difference between a label and a constraint. A label says "this fact is about 

meteorology." A constraint says "this fact must be consistent with meteorology — and it 

isn't." 

No traditional knowledge graph, including Wikidata with qualifiers and RDF with named 

graphs, can achieve this. They can annotate a triple with a domain tag, but they cannot reject 

a triple because its content violates the tagged domain's semantic laws. CDC can, because 

@D is not metadata. It is a modal operator. 

4.2 A priori disambiguation: Context resolves before reasoning begins Traditional knowledge graph pipelines handle polysemy through a multi-stage process: entity 

linking, word sense disambiguation, context resolution — each a separate module with its 

own error propagation. CDC eliminates this entire pipeline. 

Demonstration: Disambiguation as structural property 

The concept "Apple" is ambiguous. In a traditional KG, disambiguation requires either URI 

differentiation (Apple_fruit vs. Apple_company) or post-hoc context resolution. In CDC: 

□_Medicine : Apple → Fruit → Nutritional_Component □_Business : Apple → Company →

Technology_Sector 

The disambiguation is already complete at the moment of representation . When a query 

enters the CDC knowledge base scoped to @Medicine, the world w_Medicine is activated, 

and within that world, "Apple" necessarily denotes a fruit. There is no ambiguity to resolve, 

no module to invoke, no error to propagate. The @D operator has pre-selected the semantic 

pathway. 

Now consider a richer example. The same mathematical problem — computing the area of a

triangle with sides a=5, b=6, c=7 — can be approached through multiple methods. In CDC, 

each method lives in its own world: 

Known_Conditions —{apply@Geometry [Heron's Formula]} → Semi-perimeter → Area 

Known_Conditions —{apply@Trigonometry [Law of Cosines]} → Angle_C → Area 

Known_Conditions —{apply@Analytic_Geometry [Coordinate Placement]} → Coordinates 

→ Area Known_Conditions —{apply@Linear_Algebra [Cross Product]} → Vector_Product 

→ Area 

Each path is locked to its domain world . The Geometry path uses only geometric primitives; 

the Linear_Algebra path uses only vector operations. There is no risk of a hybrid step that 

mixes geometric theorems with algebraic identities in an invalid way — each □_D constrains 

the inference vocabulary to the tools legitimate within that world. 

This is a priori constraint: the disambiguation and path-locking happen at representation time ,

not at query time. By the time a reasoning engine touches the data, the semantic scope has 

already been narrowed. 

4.3 Cross-domain structural transfer: When worlds are compatible 

Perhaps the most striking power of CDC is its ability to support cross-domain structural 

transfer — not as an ad hoc analogy, but as a formal consequence of modal compatibility. 

Demonstration: Topology preservation across worlds 

Consider a narrative about a grandmother raising a grandchild after family tragedy. CDC 

extraction produces a structured semantic graph:

↓ generates

Now replace the core domain with @Writing_Pedagogy — a teacher struggling to teach

Core domain: @Writing_Pedagogy 

@Professional_Responsibility (bearing pressure, persisting through difficulty) 

↓ supports 

@Instructional_Investment (grading, tutoring, resource building) 

↓ manifests

The topological structure is preserved exactly. The six-node chain Responsibility → Care 

→ Character → Discipline → Qualities → Impact is invariant; only the domain-specific 

content at each node changes. This is not coincidence — it is a consequence of the modal 

framework. 

In modal terms, the transfer succeeds because: 

◇(Intergenerational_Caregiving ∧ Writing_Pedagogy) : structural_alignment is satisfiable 

The two worlds are modally compatible : their constraint structures do not contradict when 

evaluated jointly. The shared topology (responsibility-driven care → character formation →

quality emergence → social impact) is a structural invariant that survives domain substitution. 

This is the computational realization of analogy. Traditional KR systems require manual 

alignment between ontologies to establish cross-domain correspondence. CDC achieves it 

structurally: if two domain worlds share compatible topologies, their contents are 

automatically transferable. The analogous_to@D ₁↔ D₂ predicate formalizes this: 

analogous_to(Atom, Solar_System, 'Physics@Atomic', 'Astronomy@Planetary'). 

analogous_to(Neural_Network, Brain, 'CS@ML', 'Neuroscience@Cognition'). 

Each such assertion is a claim that ◇(D ₁ ∧ D₂) is satisfiable — that there exists a structural 

alignment between the two worlds. 

4.4 Formal properties 

Having demonstrated the power of @D, we now formalize the properties that make it 

possible. 4.4.1 Modal domain separation 

Theorem 1 (Modal Domain Separation). Let M = ⟨W, R_acc, V⟩ be a Kripke model where 

each domain d ∈ D corresponds to a distinct world w_d ∈ W. Then for any concept c, relation 

r, and target concepts c' ₁ ≠ c' ₂:

□_D ₁ r(c, c' ₁) ∧ □_D ₂ r(c, c' ₂) ∧ c' ₁ ≠ c' ₂ ∧ D₁ ≠ D₂ is consistent. 

Proof. Since D₁ ≠ D₂, the assertions are evaluated in distinct worlds w_D ₁ and w_D ₂. The 

valuation function V assigns truth values independently per world: V(w_D ₁) ⊨ r(c, c' ₁) and 

V(w_D ₂) ⊨ r(c, c' ₂). Because w_D ₁ ≠ w_D ₂, the two valuations do not interact, and no 

contradiction arises even when c' ₁ ≠ c' ₂. □

This theorem is not a trivial observation that "different namespaces don't conflict." It is 

a formal guarantee rooted in modal semantics : the consistency of divergent 

categorizations is a theorem of the underlying logical framework, not an ad hoc design choice. 

It specifies precisely when assertions can interact (via ◇ possibility checks for cross-domain 

reasoning) and when they cannot (by default, due to world independence). 

Example: 

is_a(Apple, Fruit, 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy'). 

is_a(Apple, Company, 'Business@Tech_Sector'). 

These coexist because □_Biology and □_Business define independent worlds. Within each 

world, the categorization is necessary; across worlds, no contradiction arises. 

4.4.2 Constraint transferability 

Property 1 (Constraint Transferability). Let S be any computational system that consumes 

CDC representations as input. If S preserves the structural integrity of CDC triples (i.e., it 

reads ⟨C, R@D, C' ⟩ as a unit rather than discarding D), then S's computations are 

automatically scoped to the world w_D defined by the domain specification. 

This property has a profound consequence: CDC representations are not passive data 

awaiting interpretation by an external engine. They are active constraint carriers that 

shape the behavior of any system that consumes them. Whether the consuming system is a

Prolog inference engine, a SPARQL query processor, or any other computational framework, 

the @D annotation imposes semantic boundaries that the system inherits structurally. 

The difference from metadata-based approaches is fundamental. Metadata must be explicitly 

queried and interpreted by each consuming system: the system must "know" to check the 

domain qualifier and adjust its behavior accordingly. Modal constraints embedded in the 

representation itself require no such explicit interpretation — they are the representation. A

system that reads is_a(Apple, Fruit, 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy') is already operating within 

□_Biology, whether or not it has been programmed to "handle domain information." 

This is why CDC achieves what labels cannot: the constraint is in the structure, not in the 

instructions to the reader. 

4.4.3 Cross-domain reasoning as modal possibility 

The cross-domain predicates receive precise modal semantics: Analogy (structural correspondence between worlds): 

analogous_to(C ₁, C₂, D₁, D₂) ≡ ◇(D ₁ ∧ D₂) : structural_alignment(C ₁, C₂)

There must exist an accessible configuration where both worlds can be jointly evaluated and 

structural correspondence holds. 

Fusion (constructing composite worlds): 

fuses_with(C ₁, C₂, C_new, D₁⊕D₂) ≡ □_{D ₁⊕D₂} integrates(C ₁, C₂, C_new) 

A new world is created in which the constraints of both parent worlds are simultaneously 

satisfied.

CDC's expressive power arises not only from its modal domain structure but also from a

concise, semantically orthogonal relation predicate system. 

5.1 Design philosophy 

Four principles guide the relation system: 

Compact Core. Approximately twenty predicates — compared to Wikidata's 9,000+ 

properties. Conceptual parsimony with sufficient expressiveness. 

Semantic Orthogonality. Each relation encodes a distinct semantic function. No redundancy, 

no overlap. 

Formal Specification. Every predicate has defined arity, algebraic properties (transitivity, 

symmetry, reflexivity), and computational complexity. 

Cross-Domain Support. Unlike traditional systems restricted to intra-domain reasoning, 

CDC includes first-class predicates that operate across domain worlds. 

5.2 Structural relations 

is_a@D — Taxonomic classification. Transitive, Asymmetric. All inferences are scoped by 

□_D: 

% Transitive closure within a world 

is_a_transitive(X, Z, D) :- is_a(X, Y, D), is_a(Y, Z, D). 

% Attribute inheritance within a world 

has_attribute(X, Attr, D) :- is_a(X, Y, D), has_attribute(Y, Attr, D). 

The domain parameter D ensures that inheritance chains never leak across worlds: an 

attribute inherited under □_Biology cannot contaminate reasoning under □_Business. 

part_of@D — Mereological relation. Transitive, Asymmetric. Represents part-whole 

hierarchies scoped to specific worlds. 

has_attribute@D — Property association. Neither transitive nor symmetric. 

5.3 Logical relations 

requires@D — Prerequisite relation. Transitive, Asymmetric, Acyclic. Within world w_D, 

prerequisite chains are computed recursively: 

prerequisite_chain(X, Z, D) :-

requires(X, Y, D), prerequisite_chain(Y, Z, D). 

prerequisite_chain(X, Y, D) :- requires(X, Y, D). cause_of@D and enables@D — Causal and facilitative dependencies. The causal 

falsification power demonstrated in Section 4.1 derives directly from the modal scoping of 

cause_of: a causal claim must be consistent with the laws of world w_D. 

contrasts_with@D — Oppositional relation. Symmetric, Non-transitive. 

5.4 Cross-domain relations 

analogous_to@D ₁↔ D₂ — Structural analogy between worlds, formalized as ◇(D ₁ ∧ D₂). 

Symmetric. 

analogous_to(Atom, Solar_System, 'Physics@Atomic', 'Astronomy@Planetary'). 

analogous_to(Neural_Network, Brain, 'CS@ML', 'Neuroscience@Cognition'). 

fuses_with@D ₁⊕D₂ — Conceptual integration creating composite worlds. □_{D ₁⊕D₂}. 

fuses_with(User_Experience, Technical_Feasibility, 

Product_Design, 'UX+Engineering').

6. Reference implementation 

CDC is not a theoretical proposal awaiting implementation. The modal constraints formalized 

in Section 4 are fully executable: the Prolog reference implementation in this section 

translates every □_D assertion into a computable predicate, and the case studies in Section 8

demonstrate real inference over real data structures. The gap between the formal framework 

and its computational realization is zero — every property claimed in this paper can be 

verified by running the provided code. 

6.1 Prolog as modal constraint checker While CDC is substrate-agnostic, Prolog serves as a natural reference implementation for a

specific reason (Kowalski, 1974): each Prolog predicate directly realizes a world-scoped 

modal assertion .

The rule: 

is_a(X, Y, Domain) :- ... 

corresponds to the modal axiom: "within world Domain , if conditions φ hold, then is_a(X, Y) 

necessarily holds." Prolog's resolution mechanism automates derivation within each world. 

The domain parameter in every predicate is not an optional annotation — it is the 

computational realization of □_D. 

However, CDC is not inherently tied to Prolog. The same model can be instantiated in RDF 

triple stores with domain-qualified predicates, in SQL with domain tables and joins, or in 

property graph databases supporting multi-context edges. Section 7 demonstrates this 

substrate independence through side-by-side comparison. 

6.2 Core predicates 

% Structural relations (world-scoped) 

:- dynamic is_a/3. 

:- dynamic part_of/3. 

:- dynamic has_attribute/3. 

% Logical relations (world-scoped) 

:- dynamic requires/3. 

:- dynamic cause_of/3. 

:- dynamic enables/3. 

% Cross-domain relations (multi-world) 

:- dynamic analogous_to/4. 

:- dynamic fuses_with/4. 

% Context relations 

:- dynamic context_value/3. 

:- dynamic strategy/3. 

6.3 Inference rules 

All inference is automatically scoped to world boundaries: 

% Transitive closure (stays within world w_D) 

is_a_star(X, Y, Domain) :- is_a(X, Y, Domain). 

is_a_star(X, Z, Domain) :-

is_a(X, Y, Domain), is_a_star(Y, Z, Domain). 

% Prerequisite chains (stays within world w_D) 

requires_star(X, Y, Domain) :- requires(X, Y, Domain). 

requires_star(X, Z, Domain) :-

requires(X, Y, Domain), requires_star(Y, Z, Domain). all_prerequisites(Target, Domain, Prereqs) :-

findall(P, requires_star(Target, P, Domain), Prereqs). 

6.4 Query examples 

% Taxonomic ancestors within world w_math@algebra 

?- is_a_star(quadratic_function, Supertype, 'math@algebra'). 

% Prerequisite chain within world w_highschool 

?- all_prerequisites(calculus, 'highschool', Prereqs). 

% Cross-domain analogy (modal possibility check) 

?- analogous_to(neural_network, BioConcept, 'ai@ml', BioDomain). 

7. Representational comparison: CDC vs. traditional approaches 

Before presenting case studies, we demonstrate concretely what CDC's @D achieves that 

traditional approaches cannot — not through theoretical argument, but through side-by-side 

query comparison. The key claim is: CDC's domain constraint provides structural 

pruning at the representation layer, with negligible storage overhead. 

7.1 Storage overhead: One string field 

At the storage level, CDC adds exactly one field to each triple: a domain specification string. 

The following table compares the per-triple storage cost: 

Representation Triple Structure Fields Overhead vs. RDF 

RDF Triple ⟨s, p, o⟩ 3 baseline 

RDF Named Graph ⟨s, p, o, g⟩ 4 +1 graph URI 

Wikidata Qualifier ⟨s, p, o⟩ + qualifier list 3 + variable +N qualifier pairs 

CDC ⟨c, r, c', d⟩ 4 +1 domain string 

CDC's overhead is identical to RDF Named Graphs: one additional field per triple. It 

is less than Wikidata qualifiers, which attach variable-length key-value pairs. The 

representational cost is trivial. The question is what this one field buys you. 

7.2 Query comparison: The same question, three representations Consider a knowledge base containing information about "Apple" in both biological and 

business contexts. A user queries: "What is Apple?" 

7.2.1 In RDF (No domain) 

# RDF: no domain scoping — returns everything 

SELECT ?type WHERE {

:Apple rdf:type ?type .

}

Result: {Fruit, Company} — mixed, unscoped. The system returns both answers with no 

mechanism to prefer one. The consuming application must implement its own disambiguation 

logic. 

To scope the query, the application must know in advance which ontology to query, or add 

post-hoc filtering: 

# RDF: manual filtering by ontology graph 

SELECT ?type WHERE {

GRAPH <http://bio.example.org> {

:Apple rdf:type ?type .

}

}

This works — but the scoping is in the query , not in the representation . The data itself carries 

no constraint. Every application that queries this data must independently implement domain 

filtering. 

7.2.2 In wikidata (qualifiers) 

# Wikidata: qualifier-annotated statement 

Apple — instance of — Fruit 

[context: Biology] 

[subfield: Plant Taxonomy] 

Apple — instance of — Company 

[context: Business] 

[subfield: Technology Industry] 

To retrieve the biology-scoped answer: 

SELECT ?type WHERE {

wd:Apple p:P31 ?statement .

?statement ps:P31 ?type .

?statement pq:P642 wd:Q420 . # "of" qualifier = Biology 

}

This is more expressive than bare RDF. But qualifiers are annotations about statements , not 

structural constraints. The critical difference:  

> 

Qualifiers cannot reject an inconsistent assertion. If someone adds Apple — causes —

Cancer [context: Biology], the qualifier [context: Biology] does not and cannot 

evaluate whether this causal claim is valid within biology. It simply tags it.  Qualifiers do not participate in inference. Transitive closure, attribute inheritance, and 

prerequisite chains do not automatically respect qualifier values — each inference 

rule must be manually extended to check qualifiers.  

> 

Qualifiers are invisible to basic queries. A query SELECT ?type WHERE { wd:Apple 

wdt:P31 ?type } (using the "truthy" shorthand) ignores qualifiers entirely, returning 

the unscoped result. 

7.2.3 In CDC 

% CDC: domain is structural 

is_a(apple, fruit, 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy'). 

is_a(apple, company, 'Business@Technology_Industry'). 

Query: 

?- is_a(apple, What, 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy'). 

What = fruit. 

One query. One result. No post-hoc filtering. No qualifier parsing. No application-level 

disambiguation. The domain constraint is in the data , so the query result is pre-scoped. 

Now the critical test — transitive inference :

% Knowledge base 

is_a(apple, fruit, 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy'). 

is_a(fruit, plant_product, 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy'). 

is_a(plant_product, organic_matter, 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy'). 

is_a(apple, company, 'Business@Technology_Industry'). 

is_a(company, corporation, 'Business@Technology_Industry'). 

% Query: all ancestors in biology world 

?- is_a_star(apple, Ancestor, 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy'). 

Ancestor = fruit ;

Ancestor = plant_product ;

Ancestor = organic_matter. 

The transitive closure automatically stays within the biology world . It never 

returns corporation — not because a filter removed it, but because corporation does not exist 

in world w_Biology. The domain parameter in the inference rule is_a_star(X, Z, Domain) :-

is_a(X, Y, Domain), is_a_star(Y, Z, Domain) ensures that every step of the chain respects the 

same □_D constraint. 

In RDF, achieving the same result requires either (a) maintaining entirely separate graphs and 

knowing which graph to query, or (b) adding FILTER clauses to every inference step. In 

Wikidata, qualifier-aware transitive closure requires custom SPARQL property paths that 

check qualifiers at each hop — a feature not natively supported.

7.5 Interoperability: Bidirectional CDC ↔ RDF Mapping 

CDC is designed to interoperate with existing Semantic Web infrastructure, not replace it. 

The following bidirectional mapping rules define a lossless round-trip between CDC four-

tuples and RDF Named Graph quads: 

CDC → RDF Named Graph: 

CDC: r(C, C', D) 

RDF: GRAPH <urn:cdc:domain:D> { C r C' }

Every CDC domain specification maps to a named graph URI. The graph groups all 

assertions that hold within world w_D. Standard SPARQL engines can query the result 

without modification. 

RDF Named Graph → CDC: 

RDF: GRAPH <g> { S P O }

CDC: P(S, O, extract_domain(g)) 

The graph URI is parsed into a domain specification. If the graph URI encodes hierarchical 

structure (e.g., <urn:bio:plant_taxonomy>), it maps to a hierarchical domain string 

('Biology@Plant_Taxonomy').

The mapping is lossless in the RDF →CDC direction: no information is lost. In the 

CDC →RDF direction, the modal constraint semantics (□_D) are preserved only if the 

consuming system treats named graphs as inference boundaries — which standard SPARQL 

does not do by default, but which CDC-aware query extensions can enforce. 

This bidirectional mapping means CDC can be deployed incrementally within existing 

RDF/SPARQL infrastructure: legacy data is imported via the RDF →CDC mapping, gains 

modal constraint semantics, and can be exported back to standard RDF quads for 

interoperability with non-CDC systems. The semantic enrichment is additive — existing 

tooling continues to work; CDC-aware tooling additionally benefits from domain scoping. 

8. Case studies 

We present three proof-of-concept applications. Each demonstrates a specific power of @D 

that cannot be replicated by traditional approaches. 

8.1 Education: Domain-Scoped Personalization 

Scenario: An online platform teaches programming to students with diverse backgrounds. 

CDC representation: 

% Concept in world w_cs@fundamentals 

is_a(function, programming_concept, 'cs@fundamentals'). 

% Learner profiles define worlds 

context_value(student_alice, math_background, 'student@profile'). 

context_value(student_bob, design_background, 'student@profile'). 

% Strategies exist only in their respective worlds 

strategy(explain_function, use_formal_definition, 'math_background@cs'). 

strategy(explain_function, use_workflow_metaphor, 'design_background@cs'). 

% Cross-domain analogy 

analogous_to(function, machine, 'cs@programming', 'engineering@systems'). 

What @D buys here: Each learner's background defines a world. The 

strategy use_formal_definition exists only in □_math_background@cs — it is not a globally 

stored strategy that gets filtered at query time. A query strategy(explain_function, How, 

'design_background@cs') returns only use_workflow_metaphor without ever touching the 

math-background strategies. In a traditional KG, all strategies are stored globally, and the 

application must filter by checking metadata. 

Query demonstration: % "How should I explain 'function' to a design student?" 

?- strategy(explain_function, Method, 'design_background@cs'). 

Method = use_workflow_metaphor. 

% "What concept in engineering is analogous to 'function' in CS?" 

?- analogous_to(function, What, 'cs@programming', 'engineering@systems'). 

What = machine. 

8.2 Enterprise: Cross-department integration 

Scenario: Product, Engineering, and Design teams use different vocabularies. "User story" 

(Product) and "functional requirement" (Engineering) refer to structurally similar concepts in 

different department worlds. 

% Cross-department analogy 

analogous_to(user_story, functional_requirement, 

'product@requirements', 'engineering@specs'). 

% Composite world: both teams' constraints apply 

fuses_with(user_experience, technical_feasibility, 

integrated_product_spec, 'product+engineering'). 

% Conflict detection within composite world 

conflicts_with(real_time_sync, battery_efficiency, 

'product+engineering@mobile'). 

What @D buys here: The analogous_to predicate asserts that the two department worlds are 

structurally compatible (◇(product ∧ engineering) is satisfiable). The fuses_with predicate 

constructs a composite world □_{product+engineering} where both teams' constraints are 

simultaneously active — and within that world, conflicts_with can detect contradictions. A

traditional KG with two department ontologies would require a hand-built bridge ontology to 

achieve the same; CDC achieves it through world composition. 

8.3 Technical documentation: Version evolution 

Scenario: React framework evolves across versions; documentation must be version-specific 

without losing cross-version continuity. 

% Version-specific knowledge in separate worlds 

is_a(class_component, component_type, 'react@pre16.8'). 

is_a(functional_component, component_type, 'react@16.8+@hooks'). 

% Evolution across world boundaries 

evolves_to(class_component, functional_component, 'react@paradigm_shift'). 

% Cross-version analogy 

analogous_to(component_did_mount, use_effect, 

'react@pre16.8', 'react@16.8+@hooks'). 

% Version-specific recommendation 

if_then(mobile_app, use_lazy_loading, 'react@mobile@perf'). What @D buys here: A query about component_did_mount scoped 

to 'react@16.8+@hooks' returns nothing — because that concept does not exist in the hooks 

world. Instead, the analogous_to predicate directs the user to use_effect as its cross-world 

counterpart. In a traditional KG, both lifecycle methods would coexist in a flat namespace, 

and the system would need version-checking logic to determine which is current. 

Query demonstration: 

% "What is the hooks equivalent of componentDidMount?" 

?- analogous_to(component_did_mount, HooksEquivalent, 

'react@pre16.8', 'react@16.8+@hooks'). 

HooksEquivalent = use_effect. 

% "What component types exist in the hooks world?" 

?- is_a(What, component_type, 'react@16.8+@hooks'). 

What = functional_component. 

% Note: class_component is NOT returned — it exists in a different world. 

9. Analysis and Discussion 

9.1 Changes made by CDC 

The demonstrations in Sections 4 and 7 reveal a systematic capability gap. This gap is not a

matter of degree — it is architectural: 

Falsification at representation layer. No traditional KG can reject causes(Thunder, 

Dark_Clouds) based on its content. CDC rejects it because □_Meteorology imposes causal 

direction constraints. Knowledge quality becomes a structural property, not a procedural one. 

Zero-cost disambiguation. Traditional pipelines require entity linking → sense 

disambiguation → context resolution (three modules, compounding error rates). CDC's @D 

pre-selects the semantic pathway at representation time. The disambiguation is complete 

before any query is issued. 

Structural pruning without indexing tricks. CDC's domain parameter partitions the search 

space at the data level. Transitive closure within one domain world operates on N/K triples 

instead of N — a reduction that scales linearly with the number of domains. This is not an 

optimization applied to traditional KR; it is a consequence of the representation's structure. 

Automatic inference scoping. In CDC, the domain parameter in every inference rule 

(e.g., is_a_star(X, Z, D) :- is_a(X, Y, D), is_a_star(Y, Z, D)) ensures that chains never cross 

world boundaries unless explicitly bridged by cross-domain predicates. In RDF/SPARQL, 

inference scoping requires manual GRAPH clauses or FILTER statements at every step —

and forgetting one step produces silent cross-contamination. 

9.2 Representational overhead and adoption cost 

CDC adds one string field per triple. This is the same overhead as RDF Named Graphs and 

less than Wikidata qualifiers. The storage cost is negligible; the structural benefit is 

substantial. More importantly, CDC does not require any machinery beyond what traditional KR already 

uses. Prolog predicates gain a domain parameter; SPARQL queries gain a domain filter; SQL 

tables gain a domain column. The implementation complexity is minimal because the 

constraint is in the data , not in the engine . Any engine that respects predicate arity 

automatically respects domain scoping. 

The adoption model is low-invasive by design : existing data is preserved without 

modification, existing queries remain valid (they simply operate in an unscoped mode 

equivalent to the status quo), and the semantic upgrade is incremental — domains can be 

added to high-value triples first and extended over time. The bidirectional mapping with RDF 

Named Graphs (Section 7.5) further ensures that CDC-enhanced data can be exported back to 

standard formats for interoperability with non-CDC systems. This combination of properties 

— negligible overhead, backward compatibility, incremental adoption, and bidirectional 

interoperability — positions CDC as a candidate for standardization within existing Semantic 

Web infrastructure. 

9.3 Constraint transferability 

The @D constraint is not confined to Prolog. Because domain is a structural component of 

the tuple, rather than metadata attached externally, any system that reads CDC triples as four-

field units inherits the constraint automatically. For example, a SQL query with WHERE 

domain = 'Biology@Plant_Taxonomy' achieves the same world-scoping as a Prolog query 

with the domain parameter. Likewise, a SPARQL query over CDC data in an RDF quad store 

achieves it through the named graph mechanism. 

This constraint transferability means that CDC ’s representational power is not an artifact of a

particular inference engine. Rather, it is a property of the data structure itself. The constraint 

travels with the data: wherever the data goes, the scoping goes with it. 

9.4 Limitations and open challenges 

Domain specification ambiguity. First, domains are free-form strings without formal 

equivalence detection. Thus, ‘Biology@Plant_Taxonomy ’ and ‘Bio@Plants ’ might refer to 

the same world but are treated as distinct. In practice, mitigation strategies include meta-level 

domain ontologies, string similarity metrics, and community conventions. More importantly, 

a formal domain algebra, with subsumption, composition, and equivalence operators, is a

priority for future formalization. 

Scope of modal formalization. Second, this paper uses standard Kripke semantics with □ and 

◇. However, more expressive frameworks, including multi-modal logics, probabilistic modal 

extensions (e.g., is_a(X, Y, D, 0.85) for confidence-weighted assertions), and temporal modal 

operators for knowledge evolution, remain open directions. 

Scalability. Third, case studies involve 100 –1,000 triples. Accordingly, web-scale validation, 

including distributed CDC deployment, indexing strategies, and real-time inference over 

millions of triples across thousands of domains, remains to be empirically demonstrated, 

though the theoretical pruning analysis (Section 7.3) suggests favorable scaling properties. Relation completeness. In addition, the current 20+ relations cover common use cases. At the 

same time, probabilistic reasoning, quantification, and deontic modalities are outside the 

current scope but could be integrated as extended relation families. 

Empirical evaluation. Furthermore, the current work is primarily theoretical and proof-of-

concept. Therefore, systematic empirical evaluation, including benchmark datasets, baseline 

comparisons on established KR tasks, and user studies, is necessary to validate the practical 

benefits suggested by the theoretical analysis. 

Computational semantics. Finally, this paper establishes the representational semantics of 

@D as a modal constraint. Building on this foundation, a full computational treatment, 

including decidability analysis of domain-scoped inference, complexity bounds for cross-

domain reasoning, and optimized implementation strategies beyond the reference Prolog 

engine, is a natural next step that presupposes the representational foundation developed here. 

9.5 Future research directions 

Theoretical directions include formalizing a domain algebra with subsumption, composition, 

and similarity operators, developing probabilistic CDC variants, introducing temporal 

extensions for knowledge evolution tracking, and investigating the formal relationship 

between CDC ’s modal semantics and Description Logic ’s epistemic extensions. 

At the computational level, important next steps include distributed CDC storage and 

partitioning strategies, where domain-based partitioning is a natural fit, as well as graph-

database integration, visual editors and validation tooling, and RDF/OWL interoperability 

layers that map CDC four-tuples to named graph quads with semantic enhancement. 

In addition, CDC ’s four-tuple structure is directly serializable as RDF Named Graph quads 

with semantic enhancement, and its □ _D operator is formally compatible with Description 

Logic ’ s existing modal extensions (Section 2.4). Together, th ese properties suggest a path 

toward standardization within existing W3C infrastructure, potentially as a semantic layer 

specification that enriches named graphs with modal constraint semantics, without requiring 

changes to the underlying RDF data model or SPARQL query language. 

Correspondingly, future evaluation should include constructing benchmark datasets for 

domain-scoped knowledge representation, comparative evaluation against Wikidata qualifiers 

and RDF named graphs on disambiguation, inference scoping, and query efficiency tasks, 

and scalability testing at web scale. 

Finally, an important bridging direction is investigating how CDC ’s constraint transferability 

property interacts with non-symbolic computational systems, including large language 

models, where the @D annotation may serve as a natural constraint interface for structured 

generation and verification. This is a promising but empirically unvalidated direction. 

10. Conclusion 

This paper proposed the Domain-Contextualized Concept Graph (CDC) and established its 

semantic foundation through modal logic. The core insight is that the domain annotation @D is not metadata - it is a modal necessity operator ‚ñ° _D that determines the conditions under 

which relations hold. 

This single design decision - adding one string field to each triple - produces representational 

power that traditional knowledge graphs cannot achieve: 

Assertions that violate their declared domain's constraints are rejected at the representational 

layer, not by external rule engines. 

Disambiguation is a zero-cost structural property: @D pre-selects semantic pathways at 

representation time, eliminating the need for multi-module disambiguation pipelines. 

Transitive inference automatically respects domain boundaries, with a structural search space 

reduction proportional to the number of domains. 

Cross-domain reasoning is formalized as modal possibility (‚óá ), with explicit predicates for 

analogy and conceptual fusion - capabilities absent from RDF, Wikidata, and traditional 

ontology systems. 

The constraint travels with the data: any system that reads CDC four-tuples inherits the 

scoping, regardless of its internal architecture. 

The cost is one additional field per triple - the same as RDF Named Graphs, less than 

Wikidata qualifiers. The adoption path is non-disruptive: any existing knowledge base can be 

augmented with domain constraints without rebuilding from scratch. The benefit is a

paradigm shift: knowledge representation moves from passive fact recording to actively 

constrained semantic structure, where @D is the lock, not the name tag. The modal 

interpretation presented here addresses knowledge representation specifically; the broader 

principle - that context functions as a structural constraint rather than an annotation - may 

have implications well beyond this domain.

Appendix A: Migration Examples 

This appendix demonstrates that CDC's migration path (Section 3.4) is not hypothetical. We 

show concrete transformations from three real-world representation formats — an OWL 

medical ontology (ICD-11), a domain OWL ontology, and a relational database — to CDC. 

In each case, the transformation is mechanical: existing entities become concepts, existing 

relations become predicates, and existing organizational structures become domain 

specifications. 

A.1 ICD-11 Foundation → CDC 

The WHO's International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) is built on a

three-layer architecture: a Foundation semantic network (formalized in OWL), linearizations 

for specific use cases, and a content model specifying required attributes. The Foundation 

contains approximately 85,000 entities organized in polyhierarchies with multiple inheritance 

(a concept may have more than one parent). 

Source: ICD-11 Foundation (OWL) 

<!-- ICD-11 Foundation: Pneumonia hierarchy (simplified) --> 

<owl:Class rdf:about="http://id.who.int/icd/entity/CA40"> 

<rdfs:label>Pneumonia</rdfs:label> 

<rdfs:subClassOf rdf:resource="http://id.who.int/icd/entity/CA40-CA43"/> 

<!-- CA40-CA43 = "Pneumonia" block under Respiratory diseases --> 

</owl:Class> 

<owl:Class rdf:about="http://id.who.int/icd/entity/CA40.0"> 

<rdfs:label>Bacterial pneumonia</rdfs:label> <rdfs:subClassOf rdf:resource="http://id.who.int/icd/entity/CA40"/> 

</owl:Class> 

<owl:Class rdf:about="http://id.who.int/icd/entity/CA40.00"> 

<rdfs:label>Pneumonia due to Streptococcus pneumoniae</rdfs:label> 

<rdfs:subClassOf rdf:resource="http://id.who.int/icd/entity/CA40.0"/> 

<icd:hasCausingCondition rdf:resource="http://id.who.int/icd/entity/XN0CS"/> 

<!-- XN0CS = Streptococcus pneumoniae (Extension Code) --> 

</owl:Class> 

<!-- ICD-11 also supports post-coordination: --> 

<!-- Bacterial pneumonia + Severity:Severe + Laterality:Bilateral --> 

Problem with OWL-only representation: ICD-11's Foundation uses multiple inheritance 

— the same entity can appear under different parent hierarchies. For example, "Pneumonia 

due to Streptococcus pneumoniae" can be classified under:  

> 

Respiratory diseases (by anatomical site)  

> 

Bacterial infections (by etiology)  

> 

Infectious diseases (by mechanism) 

In OWL, these are represented as multiple rdfs:subClassOf axioms. But OWL does not 

distinguish why a classification holds — it cannot express "this is a respiratory disease when 

viewed from the anatomical perspective and a bacterial infection when viewed from the 

etiological perspective ." The classification context is implicit. 

CDC transformation: 

% Anatomical classification world 

is_a('CA40.00', 'CA40.0', 'ICD11@Respiratory@Anatomical'). 

is_a('CA40.0', 'CA40', 'ICD11@Respiratory@Anatomical'). 

is_a('CA40', 'Respiratory_Diseases', 'ICD11@Respiratory@Anatomical'). 

% Etiological classification world 

is_a('CA40.00', 'Bacterial_Pneumonia', 'ICD11@Infectious@Etiological'). 

is_a('Bacterial_Pneumonia', 'Bacterial_Infections', 'ICD11@Infectious@Etiological'). 

% Causal relation (world-scoped) 

cause_of('Streptococcus_pneumoniae', 'CA40.00', 'ICD11@Microbiology@Pathogenesis'). 

% Cross-classification analogy 

analogous_to('CA40.00', 'CA40.00', 

'ICD11@Respiratory@Anatomical', 

'ICD11@Infectious@Etiological'). 

% Same entity, different classification worlds — explicitly linked. 

% Clinical severity (world-scoped) 

has_attribute('CA40.00', severe, 'ICD11@Clinical@Severity'). 

has_attribute('CA40.00', bilateral, 'ICD11@Clinical@Laterality'). 

What CDC adds that OWL cannot express: 1. Classification context is explicit. OWL says "CA40.00 subClassOf CA40.0" without 

specifying which classification perspective makes this true. CDC says "CA40.00 is_a 

CA40.0 in the anatomical world " — the classification has a reason. 

2. Multiple inheritance becomes multi-world membership. Instead of one entity with 

multiple parents in a flat hierarchy, CDC represents the same entity existing in 

multiple domain worlds, each with its own complete and non-conflicting taxonomy. 

The analogous_to predicate explicitly bridges these worlds. 

3. Post-coordination gains modal scoping. ICD-11's post-coordination (combining 

codes like Pneumonia + Severe + Bilateral) maps to CDC's attribute system with 

explicit domain scoping. Severity is scoped to @Clinical@Severity; laterality 

to @Clinical@Laterality. Each attribute axis lives in its own world with its own valid 

value set. 

4. Linearization becomes world selection. ICD-11's linearization process — choosing 

which parent to use for a particular statistical classification — corresponds in CDC to 

selecting a domain world. The MMS linearization is one world; the Foundation is the 

union of all worlds. 

Transformation rule (mechanical): 

For each OWL axiom: C rdfs:subClassOf D

→ Determine classification context from hierarchy path 

→ Generate: is_a(C, D, 'ICD11@<context>') 

For each OWL axiom with multiple parents: 

C rdfs:subClassOf D1, C rdfs:subClassOf D2 

→ If D1 and D2 belong to different classification axes: 

is_a(C, D1, 'ICD11@<axis1>') 

is_a(C, D2, 'ICD11@<axis2>') 

analogous_to(C, C, 'ICD11@<axis1>', 'ICD11@<axis2>') 

For each OWL restriction: 

C hasCausingCondition P

→ cause_of(P, C, 'ICD11@<relevant_domain>') 

The 85,000 entities in ICD-11 Foundation can be systematically transformed using these 

rules. The domain specifications can be derived from ICD-11's existing chapter structure (28 

chapters → 28+ domain worlds), with sub-domains derived from block-level groupings.

</owl:Restriction> 

</rdfs:subClassOf> 

<rdfs:subClassOf> 

<owl:Restriction>

</owl:Restriction>

<rdfs:subClassOf>

</owl:Restriction> 

</rdfs:subClassOf> 

</owl:Class> 

<owl:Class rdf:about="#AmericanaPizza"> 

<rdfs:subClassOf rdf:resource="#NamedPizza"/>

</owl:Class> 

CDC transformation: 

% Taxonomic hierarchy 

is_a(margherita_pizza, named_pizza, 'Food@Pizza@Italian'). 

is_a(americana_pizza, named_pizza, 'Food@Pizza@American'). 

% Toppings as domain-scoped attributes 

has_attribute(margherita_pizza, mozzarella_topping, 'Food@Pizza@Toppings'). 

has_attribute(margherita_pizza, tomato_topping, 'Food@Pizza@Toppings'). 

has_attribute(americana_pizza, pepperoni_topping, 'Food@Pizza@Toppings'). 

% Origin — note: in OWL this is a flat hasValue restriction. 

% In CDC, it becomes a domain specification. 

has_attribute(margherita_pizza, italy, 'Food@Pizza@Origin'). 

has_attribute(americana_pizza, united_states, 'Food@Pizza@Origin'). 

% Cross-domain: nutritional perspective 

has_attribute(margherita_pizza, vegetarian, 'Food@Nutrition@DietaryCategory'). 

has_attribute(americana_pizza, non_vegetarian, 'Food@Nutrition@DietaryCategory'). 

% Cross-domain analogy: culinary vs. cultural perspective 

analogous_to(margherita_pizza, neapolitan_tradition, 

'Food@Pizza@Italian', 'Culture@Italy@Cuisine'). 

What CDC adds:  OWL restrictions are flat. hasTopping some MozzarellaTopping is a universal 

constraint — it doesn't distinguish whether you're asking about toppings, nutrition, or 

cultural significance. CDC scopes each attribute to a specific world.  

> 

OWL cannot represent "the same pizza means different things in different 

contexts." To a chef, Margherita is about ingredients (@Toppings). To a nutritionist, 

it's about dietary category (@Nutrition). To a cultural historian, it's about Neapolitan 

tradition (@Culture). OWL collapses all perspectives into one class definition; CDC 

preserves each. 

Transformation rule: 

rdfs:subClassOf (simple) → is_a(C, D, '<ontology_namespace>@<branch>') 

owl:Restriction + onProperty P + someValuesFrom V

→ has_attribute(C, V, '<ontology_namespace>@<property_domain>') 

owl:Restriction + onProperty P + hasValue V

→ has_attribute(C, V, '<ontology_namespace>@<property_domain>')

'Undergraduate');

CDC transformation: 

% Courses become concepts; department+level become domain 

is_a('CS101', programming_course, 'CS@Undergraduate'). is_a('CS201', data_structures_course, 'CS@Undergraduate'). 

is_a('MATH101', calculus_course, 'Math@Undergraduate'). 

% Prerequisites become world-scoped requires relations 

requires('CS201', 'CS101', 'CS@Undergraduate'). 

requires('CS201', 'MATH101', 'CS@Undergraduate@CrossDept'). 

% Now we can do things SQL cannot: 

% Cross-department analogy 

analogous_to('MATH101', 'CS101', 

'Math@Undergraduate', 'CS@Undergraduate'). 

% Both are "foundational first-year courses" in their respective worlds 

% Graduate-level recontextualization 

is_a('CS201', review_material, 'CS@Graduate'). 

% The same course is "data structures" for undergrads 

% and "review material" for graduate students — different worlds. 

Transformation rule: 

For each row in a fact table: 

→ Entity columns become concepts C, C' 

→ Relationship columns become relation predicates r

→ Category/department/type columns become domain specifications D

→ Generate: r(C, C', '<category>@<subcategory>') 

The key insight: relational databases already contain domain information in their category 

columns, foreign key structures, and table organization. They just don't use it as a constraint. 

Adding @D to the representation — which corresponds to adding a single domaincolumn —

activates this latent structure as a modal world constraint. 

Appendix B: CDC Query Templates and Interaction Protocols 

This appendix provides executable query templates that operationalize the modal constraint 

mechanisms described in Section 4. Each template corresponds to a specific theoretical 

property of CDC. 

B.1 Single-Chain Constrained Reasoning 

Theoretical basis: A priori disambiguation (Section 4.2). The □_D operator locks each 

reasoning step to a single domain world. 

Template: 

You are a CDC reasoning assistant. Answer strictly in the following format: 

Format: 

ConceptA --{relation@domain [basis]}--> ConceptB --{relation@domain [basis]}--> 

ConceptC 

Rules: 1. Every arrow must include an @domain annotation 

2. Every step must state its basis (theorem, reference, or established principle) 

3. If any step cannot be verified within the declared @domain, STOP and explain why 

Question: [user question] 

Example interaction: 

Question: A patient has fever 39 °C + severe headache + neck stiffness. 

Initial assessment? 

Response: 

Symptom_Cluster[fever + headache + neck_stiffness] 

--{classic_triad@Neurology [meningitis diagnostic criteria]}--> 

Suspected_Meningitis 

--{requires_workup@Emergency_Medicine [acute management protocol]}--> 

Lumbar_Puncture + Empiric_Antibiotics 

Verification: ✓ All steps within declared @domains 

Confidence: Steps grounded in established clinical criteria 

The @domain annotation at each step ensures that the reasoning chain stays within a

consistent clinical world. A step claiming --{folk_remedy@Neurology}--> would be rejected 

because folk remedies are not established within □_Neurology. 

B.2 Multi-Path Domain Comparison 

Theoretical basis: Domain Separation (Theorem 1). The same question can be answered 

through multiple □_D worlds, producing non-contradictory but distinct reasoning paths. 

Template: 

You are a CDC reasoning assistant. Analyze all possible reasoning paths: 

Question: [user question] 

Requirements: 

1. List at least 3 possible reasoning paths 

2. Each path uses CDC format with explicit @domain at every step 

3. Compare each path on: reliability, applicability, complexity 

4. Recommend the optimal path with justification 

Format: 

Path 1: A --{R1@D1}--> B1 --{R2@D2}--> C1 

Path 2: A --{R3@D3}--> B2 --{R4@D4}--> C2 

... 

Comparison: [table] 

Recommendation: [optimal path] because [reason] 

This template operationalizes Section 4.2's multi-path demonstration: the same mathematical 

problem solved through □_Geometry, □_Trigonometry, □_Analytic_Geometry, and 

□_Linear_Algebra produces four valid but distinct reasoning chains, each locked to its 

domain world. B.3 Causal Consistency Check 

Theoretical basis: Representation-level falsification (Section 4.1). The □_D operator can 

reject assertions whose causal direction is inconsistent with the declared domain's laws. 

Template: 

You are a CDC reasoning assistant. Answer strictly in CDC format: 

Format: 

ConceptA --{relation@domain [basis]}--> ConceptB 

Rules: 

1. Every arrow must include @domain 

2. Every step must state its basis 

3. CRITICAL: If any step — including its concepts, relation, or domain —

cannot be verified for consistency, accuracy, and causal direction 

within the declared @domain, you must STOP immediately and explain 

why the chain cannot continue. 

Question: [user question] 

Example interaction (falsification triggered): 

Question: Thunder causes dark clouds to appear. 

Response: 

I cannot construct this reasoning chain. 

The proposed assertion: 

Thunder --{causes@Meteorology}--> Dark_Clouds 

is inconsistent with □_Meteorology. Within the meteorological world, 

the established causal chain is: 

Moisture_Accumulation --{causes@Meteorology}--> Cloud_Formation 

Cloud_Formation --{causes@Meteorology}--> Charge_Separation 

Charge_Separation --{causes@Meteorology}--> Lightning 

Lightning --{accompanies@Meteorology}--> Thunder 

The proposed causal direction (Thunder → Dark_Clouds) reverses the 

established chain. STOPPED: causal inconsistency within declared domain. 

This template demonstrates the falsification power described in Section 4.1. The 

@Meteorology constraint does not merely label the assertion — it imports the causal laws of 

the meteorological world and rejects assertions that violate them. 

B.4 Notes on Template Usage 

These templates are not application-specific tools — they are executable specifications of 

CDC's theoretical properties : Template B.1 demonstrates that □_D locks reasoning chains to a single world (a priori 

constraint).  

> 

Template B.2 demonstrates that distinct □_D worlds produce non-contradictory 

parallel paths (Domain Separation).  

> 

Template B.3 demonstrates that □_D can reject ill-formed assertions at the 

representation level (falsification). 

Each template can be used with any general-purpose language model or rule-based system. 

The constraint behavior arises from the @D structure embedded in the query format, not from 

any special capability of the consuming system — a direct manifestation of the constraint 

transferability property (Section 4.4.2).
