Abstract 

Reducing hallucinations in Large Language Models (LLMs) is essential for improving the accuracy of data extraction from large text corpora. Current methods, like prompt engineering and chain-of-thought prompting, focus on individual docu-ments but fail to consider relationships across a corpus. This paper introduces Peer Context Outlier Detection (P-COD), a novel approach that uses the relationships between docu-ments to improve extraction accuracy. Our application do-main is in scientific literature summarization, where papers with similar experiment settings should draw similar conclu-sions. By comparing extracted data to validated peer informa-tion within the corpus, we adjust confidence scores and flag low-confidence results for expert review. High-confidence re-sults, supported by peer validation, are considered reliable. Our experiments demonstrate up to 98% precision in outlier detection across 6 domains of science, demonstrating that our design reduces hallucinations, enhances trust in automated systems, and allows researchers to focus on ambiguous cases, streamlining the data extraction workflows. 

# Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed the land-scape of automated information retrieval by enabling rapid synthesis of vast scientific literature. However, their propen-sity for generating hallucinations – plausible but incorrect information – presents a serious challenge, particularly in scientific research where accuracy is paramount. Hallucina-tions occur because LLMs generate responses based on sta-tistical patterns rather than verified facts, leading to unreli-able extractions that can mislead researchers. While LLMs provide significant improvements in effi-ciency and adaptability, their errors can cause issues when incorrect information is given in automated workflows. This is especially problematic in scientific literature, where data accuracy is crucial for future research. Without robust vali-dation mechanisms, LLM-driven knowledge extraction risks amplifying misinformation, making it difficult to distinguish between genuine scientific conclusions and errors. Several strategies have been developed to improve the accuracy of LLM outputs. One widely used approach is prompt engineering (Sahoo et al. 2024; White et al. 2023)

Another technique is Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al. 2022; Zheng et al. 2023), which encourages the model to reason step-by-step before making a final decision. While this method improves logical consistency, it does not inherently validate extracted data against external sources or flag potential inconsistencies within a corpus. It remains limited in its ability to cross-reference information across multiple documents. A more recent approach is Retrieval-Augmented Gener-ation (RAG) (He et al. 2024), which enhances LLMs by incorporating external knowledge from a retrieval system. This method grounds model outputs in authoritative sources, reducing hallucinations. However, RAG depends entirely on the completeness and reliability of its retrieval system. If the retrieved documents are incomplete, outdated, or lack ex-perimental data, RAG cannot ensure the correctness of ex-tracted scientific information. Despite these advancements, existing methods do not ac-tively compare extracted data against related scientific litera-ture. They fail to account for inter-document relationships –missing an important and useful idea for validation. Existing approaches do not validate whether an answer retrieved by the LLM is consistent with trends established by other docu-ments. This leads to a fundamental gap in reliability: models may produce plausible but incorrect outputs without mecha-nisms to detect inconsistencies across multiple sources. The absence of cross-document validation means that re-searchers must manually verify extracted data, reducing the efficiency of automated extraction systems. Without a sys-tem that can flag anomalies given the context of similar literature, erroneous information can go unnoticed, poten-tially skewing scientific analyses and conclusions. To ensure trust in LLM-generated data, a method is needed that cross-checks extracted values against similar studies. To address this gap, we introduce Peer Context Out-lier Detection (P-COD) , a novel approach that enhances extraction accuracy by leveraging inter-document relation-ships. Unlike traditional methods that process each docu-ment in isolation, P-COD evaluates extracted data in the context of semantically related and peer-reviewed papers. The intuition is that papers summarizing experiments with similar conditions should conclude with similar results. Our approach allows for confidence scoring, where extracted data points are evaluated based on alignment with existing knowledge, anomaly detection, where numerical inconsis-tencies between related papers are flagged for review, and human-in-the-loop validation, where only low-confidence results require manual assessment. P-COD functions as an advanced form of outlier detec-tion (Anusha et al. 2021; Hodge et al. 2004) but incorpo-rates additional layers of validation. Standard outlier detec-tion primarily relies on statistical measures such as Z-score analysis, Mahalanobis distance, or density-based clustering methods like k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) anomaly detec-tion. These techniques work by identifying data points that deviate significantly from a central tendency, often based on a predetermined threshold. For example, Z-score detection flags values that lie a certain number of standard deviations away from the mean, while Mahalanobis distance considers the correlation between variables to detect outliers in multi-dimensional datasets. In the context of structured numerical data, these methods can be highly effective. However, when applied to scientific data extraction, tra-ditional outlier detection techniques face significant limi-tations. Scientific studies are not uniform; they differ in methodology, reporting formats, and experimental condi-tions, meaning that what appears to be an outlier numerically may actually be a valid deviation due to a different exper-imental approach. Furthermore, statistical outlier detection assumes that an outlier is an inherently incorrect or anoma-lous value, but in scientific research, differences in method-ologies can lead to naturally high variance in reported data. This makes it difficult to determine whether a flagged value is truly an error or a meaningful deviation based on the study’s experimental design. Our method extends traditional anomaly detection by uti-lizing semantic embeddings to group comparable studies and establish peer relationships, comparing extracted nu-merical values against those of semantically similar papers, adjusting confidence scores based on alignment with estab-lished research, and automatically flagging results that devi-ate significantly from expected trends, reducing false posi-tives in anomaly detection. By incorporating peer validation, our approach provides a more nuanced and reliable assess-ment of data correctness than existing techniques. To evaluate this approach, we conducted experiments on scientific document corpora across 6 domains (Computer Science, Physics, Biology, Chemistry, Materials Science, and Environmental Science), demonstrating up to 98% pre-cision in identifying anomalous data points. The key out-come of our method is a ranked list of data points, scored based on how surprising they are relative to their peers. By generating these surprising scores, our system provides a clear and quantifiable measure of confidence, allowing re-searchers to prioritize their review process. Instead of man-ually verifying all extracted data, researchers can focus on the subset of flagged points that exhibit the most unexpected deviations. This dramatically narrows down the scope of hu-man review, significantly reducing effort while improving the overall reliability of automated data extraction. By incorporating corpus-wide validation into automated data extraction, P-COD increases trust in LLM-driven re-search workflows. This allows researchers to focus on gen-uinely ambiguous cases while streamlining large-scale in-formation synthesis, reinforcing LLMs as powerful tools for scientific discovery. 

# Related Work 

Existing tools for scientific data extraction, including those based on Large Language Models (Lee et al. 2019; Gougherty and Clipp 2024; Gao et al. 2021), effectively parse text but lack robust mechanisms to validate extracted data. While semantic embedding models such as SciBERT have been used to measure the similarity of scientific texts, they do not assess the experimental consistency of extracted data. Our work bridges this gap by introducing a field-wide validation mechanism that contextualizes extracted data within the broader scientific literature. One widely used approach to improving LLM accuracy is chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al. 2022; Zheng et al. 2023), which encourages the model to reason step-by-step before making a final decision. This method has been shown to improve logical consistency and reduce halluci-nations in tasks requiring multi-step reasoning. However, while CoT enhances reasoning, it does not inherently vali-date extracted data against external sources or flag potential inconsistencies within a corpus (Turpin et al. 2023). Another common strategy is prompt engineering (Chen et al. 2022), where carefully crafted prompts are designed to guide the model toward more accurate and reliable ex-tractions. This includes zero-shot and few-shot learning techniques that provide context-rich instructions to improve extraction precision. Despite their effectiveness, these ap-proaches still treat each document in isolation, leading to unchecked errors when results are taken at face value. Recently, work has explored the use of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) (Lewis et al. 2020; Xiong et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2023; He et al. 2024), where LLMs are supplemented with an external database or retrieval mech-anism to cross-check extracted data against authoritative sources. While this helps ground model outputs, it depends on the completeness and reliability of the retrieval system, which may not always cover the latest or most relevant sci-entific findings. The major limitation of these approaches is that they do not flag or alert the user when a data point is uncertain or contradicts established knowledge. Current methods often assume LLM outputs to be definitive (Li et al. 2024), without assigning confidence scores or contextualizing results within the broader scientific field. Our P-COD method addresses this gap by integrating a validation mechanism that compares extracted data across multiple documents, adjusting confidence scores based on peer-reviewed consistency. This enables automatic flagging of surprising data points for human review, increasing trust-worthiness in automated scientific data extraction. 

# Problem Definition 

The challenge of extracting accurate information from scien-tific literature using LLMs lies in their tendency to generate plausible but incorrect data. Automated extraction methods lack robust mechanisms for verifying extracted values, lead-ing to potential inaccuracies in high-stakes domains such as scientific research. To ensure reliability, a system must not only extract information efficiently but also identify which extracted data points require human verification. This is the fundamental problem P-COD seeks to address – a human-in-the-loop validation system that highlights uncertain extrac-tions for targeted review rather than relying on exhaustive manual verification. The system takes as input a collection of scientific papers along with a query about their contents. Each paper is pro-cessed to extract relevant information, and rather than treat-ing each extracted value in isolation, the system evaluates them in relation to other papers in the dataset. This ensures that outputs are assessed not just based on individual accu-racy but also on how well they align with broader trends in the field. The output of our method is a list of flagged papers and data points that are likely to contain hallucinated or inconsis-tent information extracted by the LLM. Each flagged point is associated with a surprising score, which quantifies how unexpected a data point is based on two primary factors: semantic similarity and experimental consistency. If a nu-merical value extracted from a paper deviates significantly from values in semantically related papers – those that dis-cuss similar methodologies and experiments – its surprising score increases. Similarly, if an experimental result diverges from established trends within a comparable set of studies, it is flagged as a likely outlier. While these scores do not explicitly form a ranking, they can be used as a ranking sys-tem, where the most surprising points (those with the highest scores) should be prioritized for human review. Researchers can also define a threshold to control how much deviation is tolerable before a paper is flagged, allowing flexibility in balancing automated extraction with human oversight. The key trait of the surprising score is its ability to rank data points by their deviation from expected values. Instead of requiring human reviewers to manually verify every ex-tracted value, the system pinpoints the most anomalous re-sults, directing human attention toward potential errors or novel findings. This significantly reduces the burden of man-ual verification while ensuring that genuinely uncertain or unexpected data points receive appropriate scrutiny. By structuring the problem this way, P-COD transforms an unmanageable task – manually validating all extracted data – into a more efficient process. Rather than discarding uncertain extractions or blindly trusting LLM outputs, the system integrates human expertise into an automated work-flow, allowing researchers to focus their efforts where it mat-ters most. This problem formulation serves as the foundation for the methodology that follows, detailing how surprising scores are computed and used to refine scientific data ex-traction. 

# Peer Context Outlier Detection (P-COD) 

The goal of our method is to identify value outliers within a peer group by comparing extracted data points against se-mantically similar and methodologically related scientific papers. Traditional outlier detection methods struggle with contextual variations across different studies, but our ap-proach ensures data reliability by validating it against its peers. Figure 2 illustrates this process, where extracted data is compared against semantically similar papers to deter-mine whether it aligns with expected trends or requires fur-ther review. This enables us to detect whether a data point is an expected result within its research context or an anoma-lous finding that requires further review. To achieve this, our method outputs a Surprising Score ,a metric that quantifies how unexpected a data point is com-pared to its peer group. This score reflects deviations from established norms within the field, ensuring that researchers can focus their attention on highly surprising extractions. ## Semantic Text Embedding of Papers 

In natural language processing (NLP), a text embedding is a high-dimensional vector representation of a given text that captures its semantic meaning. These embeddings transform text into vectors that can be compared to one another in a structured way. By converting words, sentences, or even en-tire scientific papers into vector representations, we can ana-lyze and compare their content based on meaning rather than just surface-level word similarities. LLMs generate these embeddings using transformer-based architectures with multi-head self-attention mech-anisms that encode positional and contextual relation-ships between tokens. Specifically, we use OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model (Sun et al. 2024). The underlying mechanism behind text embeddings relies on transformer-based neural networks, which process input text by encod-ing contextual relationships between words through multi-ple layers of attention mechanisms. This allows the model to represent words not only based on their individual mean-ings but also in relation to their surrounding context. Unlike traditional word frequency-based approaches (such as TF-IDF (Ramos 2003)), text embeddings capture deeper mean-ings by understanding word relationships at different levels – such as sentence structure, phrase composition, and even document-level semantics. In our approach, we represent scientific papers as embed-dings in a high-dimensional space to facilitate their compar-ison. Each paper is converted into a vector using a pretrained language model (GPT4o). As shown in Figure 2, these em-beddings are generated through the Text Embedding LLM, which converts scientific literature into a shared vector space where semantic similarities can be analyzed. Once vector-ized, papers with similar research topics, methodologies, and terminologies will have embeddings that are closer to-gether in this space. This allows us to quantify how related two papers are based on their semantic similarity rather than merely comparing keyword overlap. To measure semantic similarity, we use cosine similarity (Wu et al. 2023), a stan-dard for semantic vector comparisons. The ability to analyze text through embeddings enables us to enhance the reliability of scientific data extraction. When assessing an extracted numerical result, we do not just ex-amine the document it came from in isolation; instead, we compare it to semantically related documents to determine whether the extracted data aligns with established findings in the field. This embedding-based approach allows for a more context-aware evaluation, reducing the risk of relying on iso-lated or potentially misleading information. Surprising Score Calculation

# Experiments 

We conducted comprehensive experiments to validate P-COD’s effectiveness across multiple domains and scales. Our evaluation demonstrates P-COD’s capabilities through two main experiments: (1) multi-domain generalization across 6 scientific fields, and (2) large-scale performance on Computer Science papers. We compare P-COD against prompt engineering and chain-of-thought baselines in both experiments. Results demonstrate consistent high perfor-mance, with P-COD achieving up to 98% precision in de-tecting corrupted numerical data while maintaining strong generalization across diverse scientific domains. 

## P-COD on Multiple Domains 

Setup. To evaluate P-COD’s generalizability across scien-tific disciplines, we conducted experiments spanning 6 do-mains: Computer Science, Physics, Biology, Chemistry, Ma-terials Science, and Environmental Science. We selected papers from the scientific papers dataset (Cohan et al. 2018), which contains structured documents from ArXiv and PubMed repositories. Using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model to generate semantic vector representations that allow us to plot paper similarity in latent dimensional space, we created a controlled experimental dataset as fol-lows: For each domain, we started with real papers from the dataset as seeds and generated 168 synthetic papers (28 per domain) that maintain semantic similarity to their respec-tive seeds while varying experimental values. These syn-thetic papers serve as our ground truth dataset, organized into 4 clusters per domain based on methodological similar-ity. Our experiment tests P-COD’s ability to detect anoma-lous experimental values by strategically corrupting a subset of these papers and measuring detection accuracy. We identi-fied domain-appropriate experimental metrics and their typ-ical ranges through systematic analysis of the original scien-tific papers dataset. For each domain, we selected specific metrics that rep-resent common experimental outcomes: accuracy (0.70-0.95) for Computer Science, wavelength (400-800 nm) for Physics, protein levels (2-1000 ng/mL) for Biology, yield (50-100%) for Chemistry, strength (100-2000 MPa) for Ma-terials Science, and CO2 levels (380-450 ppm) for Environ-mental Science. We strategically corrupted 25% of papers by introducing values 3-5 times outside normal ranges to create obvious outliers for validation. 

Baseline Methods. To establish P-COD’s superiority, we compared against two common methods for detecting prob-lematic data: prompt engineering and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting. Results. P-COD significantly outperformed both baseline methods across all six domains. Analysis of the clustering results confirmed that papers discussing similar experimental approaches were positioned closely in semantic space, while corrupted papers were identified as clear outliers by P-COD.

# Conclusion 

This paper presents a novel approach for field-wide vali-dation of scientific data extraction using surprising scores. By integrating semantic similarity and experimental consis-tency, our method provides a robust mechanism to enhance trust in automated data extraction. Peer Context Outlier Detection enables cross-document validation, ensuring that extracted data aligns with estab-lished trends within a given research domain. Through this process, we reduce the likelihood of hallucinations and im-prove the reliability of information synthesized from large language models (LLMs). Our experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach in identifying anomalous data points with up to 98% precision. By using semantic embeddings and inter-document relationships, P-COD enables researchers to fo-cus on ambiguous cases while minimizing unnecessary man-ual review –streamlining large-scale information synthesis, and reinforcing LLMs as reliable tools for scientific discov-ery. From the accuracy, it can be deployed in the scientific community to improve the reliability of automated literature analysis. Although P-COD demonstrates strong performance in di-verse scientific domains, several areas present opportunities for future enhancement. Future work will explore domain-specific adaptation through fine tuned embedding models for individual scientific fields. Multi-modal integration repre-sents another promising direction, extending P-COD to ana-lyze figures, tables, images, videos, and supplementary data alongside textual content, enabling comprehensive valida-tion of experimental claims across all forms of information storage. Furthermore, integration with existing scientific databases and real-time validation pipelines could enable P-COD de-ployment in automated peer review systems and literature review workflows, providing confidence for researchers and flagging potentially problematic data points before publica-tion. These extensions build upon our validated approach to further enhance accuracy, confidence, reliability, and be-lief in the adoption of automated literature review systems across the broader scientific research ecosystem.
